### 2 Problem
[SCREEN: A12, Memo-Übersicht; dann die Karte „Must we label what our AI produces?“ zwei Sekunden halten]
Think of three things a small business publishes in a normal month. A short article on its website about a new rule in its trade. A picture for that article, made with an image tool, because nobody had a photo. And a chatbot in the corner of the site, supplied by a provider. Under Article fifty of the AI Act, these are three different cases, and two of them may ask nothing of you at all.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 3(3), (4) and (60), Art. 50, Art. 113, consolidated version of 27 July 2026 · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[SCREEN: Handbuch online, Abschnitt 5, erster Fakt-Absatz, Zoom auf die Quellenzeile]
[MARKER: Fact]
Article fifty has applied since 2 August 2026. Its first two paragraphs address providers. Paragraphs three and four address deployers: anyone who uses an AI system under their own authority, outside a purely personal activity. A business that uses tools made by others is a deployer.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 3(3), (4) and (60), Art. 50, Art. 113, consolidated version of 27 July 2026 · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[MARKER: Fact]
One thing to settle first. The deployer is the business, not the person at the keyboard: the Commission's guidelines on Article fifty say that employees acting under a company's instructions are not separate deployers. A word on those guidelines. They are the Commission's reading, published on 20 July 2026. They help with interpretation. They are not the law itself.
[SOURCE: European Commission, Guidelines on the implementation of the transparency obligations under Article 50 of the AI Act, C(2026) 5054 final of 20 July 2026, points 12, 14 and 16 · digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu · retrieved 2026-10-19]

So the question is not whether somebody used AI. The question is: what did we publish, does it look real, and did a person with knowledge check it.

<short pause>

### 3 Solution
[SCREEN: Handbuch online, Abschnitt 5, „The three cases for a deployer“, die drei Nummern nacheinander]
Here are the three cases, in the order of the article.

[MARKER: Fact]
Case one, paragraph three: emotion recognition or biometric categorisation. The people exposed to such a system are informed that it operates. For most small businesses this is no case at all, and emotion recognition in the workplace has been largely prohibited since 2 February 2025. If it touches you, ask a specialist.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 50(3), Art. 5(1), point (f), Art. 113, point (a) · retrieved 2026-10-19]

[SCREEN: Handbuch 5, Definition des Deepfakes, Zoom auf das Zitat, dann auf die Quellenzeile]
[MARKER: Fact]
Case two, paragraph four, first subparagraph: deep fakes. A deployer that generates or manipulates image, audio or video content that is a deep fake discloses that it was artificially generated or manipulated. The definition stands in Article three, point sixty: AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events, and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful. In an evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional work, the duty shrinks to a note that such content exists.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 50(4), first subparagraph, and Art. 3(60) · retrieved 2026-10-19]

Two words carry the weight: resembles, and falsely.

[MARKER: Interpretation]
Our reading, with the guidelines: a photo-like AI image of a technician at a switch cabinet is in most cases a deep fake, because it shows a scene that could have happened. A stylised graphic of a lightning bolt is not. The guidelines count what could plausibly exist, and treat photorealism as a strong sign. Advertising usually cannot rely on the artistic exception.
[SOURCE: Guidelines C(2026) 5054, points 113, 114, 122 and 124 · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[SCREEN: Handbuch 5, Fall drei und der Absatz zu Rn. 131]
[MARKER: Fact]
Case three, paragraph four, second subparagraph: text. A deployer that generates or manipulates text which is published to inform the public on matters of public interest discloses that the text was artificially generated or manipulated. Unless two conditions are both met: the content went through human review or editorial control, and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility for the publication.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 50(4), second subparagraph · retrieved 2026-10-19]

[MARKER: Fact]
The guidelines unpack three words. Published means accessible to an indeterminate, fairly large number of unrelated readers; an email to one customer and an internal note are not published. Informing means communicating knowledge, opinions or facts. Matters of public interest are those relevant to society at large: politics, justice, public health, the environment, the economy, among others. Outside the scope, by the guidelines' own examples: a company's advertisements and product descriptions that make no claims about health, consumer safety or sustainability.
[SOURCE: Guidelines C(2026) 5054, point 131 with examples · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[SCREEN: Handbuch 5, Absatz zu Rn. 133 bis 138, Quellenzeile sichtbar]
[MARKER: Fact]
What counts as human review? The guidelines say: the deliberate examination of the substance by persons with relevant knowledge; fact-checking is the minimum. Spell-checking, an automated check or a cursory approval do not count. Any substantive change by an AI tool after the review voids the exception. Editorial responsibility can be named in the legal notice of a website.
[SOURCE: Guidelines C(2026) 5054, points 133 to 138 · retrieved 2026-10-19]

[SCREEN: Handbuch 5, Absatz zu Rn. 38]
[MARKER: Fact]
Now the form. Paragraph five: the information is provided in a clear and distinguishable manner, at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure. The guidelines add what is not enough on its own: a disclosure only in terms and conditions, in a URL or in documentation; a machine-readable marking nobody perceives; a generalised notice somewhere on the site. The label sits where the content is, when it is seen.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 3(60) and Art. 50(5) · retrieved 2026-10-19; Guidelines C(2026) 5054, point 38 · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[SCREEN: Muster D5 „Example Electrical Ltd“, Seite 1: Kopf, dann Punkte 1 bis 3 langsam; Bildschirmtext „Example Electrical Ltd · fictional“]
What does a business do with this? Four steps.

Step one: write down the role for each tool. For tools made by others, you are the deployer, with the provider's name next to it.

Step two: take inventory of four uses. Does an AI system talk to customers for you? Do you publish AI images, audio or video? Do you publish AI text? Do you use emotion recognition? Four times no is a result too: write "no case at present" with the date.

Step three: record what each provider states about its own two duties, the notice in a chatbot and the machine-readable marking of outputs. You claim nothing yourself.

[MARKER: Fact]
Providers of generative systems that were on the market before 2 August 2026 have until 2 December 2026 for that marking. That is the provider's deadline, not yours.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 111(4), inserted by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, Art. 1, point (39) · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-19; Guidelines C(2026) 5054, point 153 · retrieved 2026-10-19]

Step four: one sheet per case. For every real-looking image, audio or video: deep fake, yes or no, one sentence why, and where the disclosure sits. For every published text: is it public, is it a matter of public interest, and then either the label or the review, with who reviewed it and when. One folder, dated. That is the whole method; the toolkit's checklist is these steps on paper.

### 4 Evidence
[SCREEN: A3, die vier Marker-Kacheln, Formen zeichnen sich]
Everything so far carried a marker. Here is what is fact, what is our reading, and what is open.

[MARKER: Fact]
Fact: the article, the definition of a deep fake, the exception for reviewed text, and the dates, read in the consolidated text of 27 July 2026 at EUR-Lex.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 3(3), (4) and (60), Art. 50, Art. 113, consolidated version of 27 July 2026 · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[MARKER: Fact]
Fact, with a caveat: the guidelines of 20 July 2026 are the Commission's reading, and we treat them as guidance, not law.
[SOURCE: European Commission, Guidelines on the transparency obligations under Article 50, annex to C(2026) 5054 final of 20 July 2026, points 12 to 16, 38, 113 to 124, 131, 133 to 138, 153, 154 · digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu · retrieved 2026-10-19 and 2026-10-23. Guidance, not law.]

[MARKER: Fact]
Fact: content generated before 2 August 2026 need not be labelled retroactively. Text generated before but published on or after that date needs the label.
[SOURCE: Guidelines C(2026) 5054, point 154 · retrieved 2026-10-19]

[MARKER: Interpretation]
Interpretation: the technician and the lightning bolt. The guidelines give the criteria; the call on a particular image is ours, and you may argue with it.
[SOURCE: Guidelines C(2026) 5054, points 113, 114, 122 and 124 · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[SCREEN: Handbuch 5, Unsicher-Kasten zum eingebetteten Chatbot, Zoom auf „Last checked 2026-10-23“]
[MARKER: Uncertain]
This is open, first: the embedded chatbot. Paragraph one addresses the provider. Whether a business that embeds someone else's chatbot on its own website has an own duty to show the notice, the guidelines do not settle. The checklist therefore asks only this: open your chatbot as a visitor, confirm the notice appears before the first answer, and keep a dated screenshot. A business that puts a chatbot into service under its own name may itself be a provider; if you plan that, ask a specialist first.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 50(1) · retrieved 2026-10-19; Guidelines, annex to C(2026) 5054 final, points 12 to 16 · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[SCREEN: Handbuch 7.1, Unsicher-Kasten zur Behörde]
[MARKER: Uncertain]
This is open, second: who supervises this in Austria. As of 23 October 2026, no official source names the market surveillance authority for the AI Act in Austria. Germany has named the Federal Network Agency, by an act of 22 July 2026, in force since 29 July 2026. The duties apply either way, because the regulation applies directly.
[SOURCE: Datenschutzbehörde (Austria), Künstliche Intelligenz und Datenschutz · dsb.gv.at · retrieved 2026-10-23; RIS, Federal Law Gazette, full-text search · retrieved 2026-10-23; KI-MIG of 22 July 2026 (BGBl. 2026 I No. 223), §§ 2, 6, 8, 15 · gesetze-im-internet.de · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[MARKER: Fact]
And the fines, because someone will ask: up to fifteen million euros or three percent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher; for small and medium-sized enterprises, the lower of the two.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 99(4), point (g) and Art. 99(6), consolidated version of 27 July 2026 · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-19]

### 5 Example business
[SCREEN: Memo „Must we label what our AI produces?“ auf scutoria.com ausfüllen: Rolle „We use AI tools made by others“, Einsatzart „published text“, Thema „matters of public interest“, Prüfung „Yes, both“; dann die Ergebnisseite mit der Ampel]
The example business in the toolkit is fictional: an electrical contractor in Austria with ten to forty-nine staff; no person in it is real. This month it publishes an article on its website about a new inspection duty for electrical installations, drafted with a text assistant. The free memo on scutoria.com asks: your role, what you publish, what it is about, and whether a person with knowledge checks the substance, with someone named as responsible in the legal notice. The answers: deployer, published text, public interest, yes to both.

[MARKER: Interpretation]
The memo's result reads: partly. No label where the exception holds, but conditions to keep. The article is a matter of public interest; because the owner reviews the substance and is named in the legal notice, the exception holds. The condition: a dated note per article, who reviewed it and when, and no AI change afterwards.
[SOURCE: Guidelines C(2026) 5054, points 133 to 138 · retrieved 2026-10-19]

[SCREEN: Muster D5 „Example Electrical Ltd“, Blatt 5 Deepfake-Prüfung und Blatt 6 Textprüfung, je ein ausgefülltes Feld; Bildschirmtext „Deep fake: yes / no“]
[MARKER: Interpretation]
The same business wants a picture. An AI image of a technician at a switch cabinet is a real-looking scene: deep fake, yes, and the caption says artificially generated. A stylised lightning bolt: deep fake, no, one sentence why, nothing to disclose. On the checklist: point five for the image, point six for the text, each dated.
[SOURCE: Guidelines C(2026) 5054, points 113, 114, 122 and 124 · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[SCREEN: Beschreibungstext dieses Videos als Textblatt auf dunklem Grund (kein Konto im Bild), Absatz „The voice in this video is synthetic“; dann der Bildschirmtext „Synthetic voice · Sources on screen · No legal advice“ groß]

The script was drafted with an AI tool and checked by us against the sources on screen. The legal notice names who holds editorial responsibility.

Our reading on the voice: it resembles no existing person, and from second zero the screen says that it is synthetic, so it would not falsely appear authentic. By the definition, not a deep fake. We disclose it anyway, in the form paragraph five asks for: on screen from second zero, spoken at the end.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 3(60) and Art. 50(5) · retrieved 2026-10-19; Guidelines C(2026) 5054, point 38 · retrieved 2026-10-23]
