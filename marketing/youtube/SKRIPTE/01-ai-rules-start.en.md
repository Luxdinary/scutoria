### 1 Hook

Are your staff already using AI tools without the business having decided anything?

### 2 Problem
[SCREEN: A12, the four memo cards; hold on "Where do we start with AI rules?"]
Take a typical situation. A business without an IT department, somewhere in the EU. Somebody in the office opens a text assistant to draft a quote. It works, so the next person does the same. Nobody decided it.

[SCREEN: the memo page, the question "Do your staff use AI tools at work today?" with its three answers]
The free start memo asks whether your staff use AI tools at work today, and offers three answers. Yes, with business accounts the business set up. Yes, with private accounts, or we do not know. No, as far as we know. The middle answer is the one this video is about. Written rules: a few sentences or an email. A briefing: informally, without a record.

Three things follow. What goes into the tools is decided at the keyboard, not by the business. If something goes wrong, nobody can say what the rule was. And when a customer, an insurer or your own staff ask what is regulated, there is nothing to show.

[MARKER: Fact]
One reason this matters is the trade secret. Under the Trade Secrets Directive, information is a trade secret only if it meets three conditions, and the third is that reasonable steps were taken to keep it secret. Austria and Germany have the same condition in their national law.
[SOURCE: Directive (EU) 2016/943, Art. 2(1)(a) to (c) · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-18; § 26b(1)(3) UWG (Austria) · ris.bka.gv.at; § 2 no. 1(b) GeschGehG (Germany) · gesetze-im-internet.de · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[MARKER: Fact]
And the German data protection authorities say that removing names and addresses from an input is regularly not enough, because the connection to a person can be restored from the context.
[SOURCE: Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe Künstliche Intelligenz und Datenschutz, version 1.0 of 6 May 2024, sections 2.2, 2.4 and 3.1 · datenschutzkonferenz-online.de · retrieved 2026-10-18]

[MARKER: Interpretation]
Some businesses do have a document. Our interpretation: a paper in a drawer is documented, but it was never issued. We come back to that.

So where do you start? Not with the longest document.

<short pause>

### 3 Where to start
[SCREEN: the memo "Where do we start with AI rules?", the seven questions, filled in for the fictional example business; see Regie, shot 4]
The free memo asks seven questions and then names the one step that comes first. We walk through its order, because the order is the point.

[SCREEN: the memo result page: the lamp, the title "Start here: one step before anything else.", the first step, then the five lines under "Where you stand"]
Step one: take stock. Which tools are actually in use, with which accounts? One row per tool. "Unclear" is a permitted entry: a finding, not a failure.

Step two, and when accounts are private or unknown the memo puts it first: business accounts for the approved tools, with training on inputs switched off, under a functional address of the business, without personal names. No private accounts for work.

[MARKER: Fact]
The German data protection authorities advise that employees should not have to work with private accounts, because profiles of individual employees can arise, and that accounts should be set so that no inputs are used for training.
[SOURCE: German data protection conference (DSK), Orientierungshilfe "Künstliche Intelligenz und Datenschutz", version 1.0 of 6 May 2024, sections 2.2, 2.4, 2.5 and 3.1 · retrieved 2026-10-18]

[MARKER: Interpretation]
Our interpretation: a rule that only names the tool silently permits its use through a private account. The rule must name the tool and the access path.

And post the one page. It says what never goes into a tool, whatever the access: data by which a customer, an employee or an applicant can be identified; access data and passwords; calculations, prices, contracts, customer lists and other confidential information; health data; documents of third parties handed over in confidence. The list closes with the rule of doubt: in case of doubt, nothing is entered, and a named person is asked.

[MARKER: Interpretation]
Our interpretation: a list without a rule of doubt is incomplete, because no catalogue meets every case.

[SCREEN: A5, "What you get", hold on D1 and D2]
Step three: the policy itself. Nine areas: permitted tools and access; permitted and prohibited uses; what never goes in; handling unavoidable personal data; review before use; release of documents leaving the business; labelling; reporting incidents; responsibility in case of doubt. Signed by the management.

[MARKER: Interpretation]
Our interpretation: four things make a text a rule in force. A date from which it applies. A named responsible person. A traceable way in which it was announced. And a confirmation that each person received it. No source lists these four. They follow from the supervisory wording that instructions should be issued and documented. And the effective date lies after the briefing: a rule nobody knows is worse than none.

Step four: the briefing, and the record of it: date, contents, who attended.

[MARKER: Fact]
Article four of the AI Act asks providers and deployers to take measures to support the development of AI literacy of their staff. It does not require guaranteeing any specific level for any individual. The article has applied since 2 February 2025; its current wording since 27 July 2026.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 4 and Art. 113, point (a), consolidated version of 27 July 2026; Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, Art. 1, point (5) · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[MARKER: Fact]
The European Commission writes that there is no need for a certificate, and that organisations can keep an internal record of trainings. That is guidance, not law.
[SOURCE: European Commission, AI literacy, questions and answers, page last updated 20 October 2026 · digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[MARKER: Gap]
How often the briefing is repeated and how long the record is kept: we found no source for this. The business sets the rhythm.

[MARKER: Fact]
Step five: the labelling check under Article fifty of the AI Act, which has applied since 2 August 2026. If nothing you publish or run for customers comes from an AI system, the record is a dated note: no case at present. The three deployer cases get their own video.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 3(3), (4) and (60), Art. 50, Art. 113, consolidated version of 27 July 2026 · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-23]

Step six: date it. One page: what is regulated, what is open, who is responsible, when it is reviewed next.

[MARKER: Fact]
Where personal data are involved, the review is in the GDPR itself: the controller's technical and organisational measures shall be reviewed and updated where necessary.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 24(1) · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-19]

[MARKER: Gap]
An interval for that review: we found no source for this. The toolkit offers six or twelve months and recommends neither.

That is the order: stock, accounts and the one page, the policy, the briefing, the labelling check, the dated page.

<short pause>

### 4 Does any law require a policy
[SCREEN: A3, the four markers; then A11, handbook section 1, the interpretation "Neither the AI Act nor the GDPR", zoom on the source line]
Now the question behind all of this: does any law require a written policy?

[MARKER: Interpretation]
Our interpretation: no. Neither the AI Act nor the GDPR contains a provision that explicitly requires a business to keep a written AI usage policy. Three articles come closest. Article four of the AI Act asks for measures that support AI literacy. Article twenty-four, paragraph one of the GDPR asks the controller for technical and organisational measures. The Trade Secrets Directive protects information only if reasonable steps were taken to keep it secret. None of them says "policy". A policy is the usual way to meet all three at once, not a duty of its own.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 4; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 24(1); Directive (EU) 2016/943, Art. 2(1)(c) · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-18 and 2026-10-19]

So why write one? Because three bodies recommend it.

[MARKER: Fact]
The German data protection authorities, in their guidance of 6 May 2024, say that clear internal instructions should be issued and documented on whether, under which conditions and for which concrete purposes which AI applications may be used.
[SOURCE: Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe Künstliche Intelligenz und Datenschutz, version 1.0 of 6 May 2024, section 2.2, p. 10 · datenschutzkonferenz-online.de · retrieved 2026-10-18]

[MARKER: Fact]
The German Federal Office for Information Security says the same from the technical side. Its measure M sixteen asks employers to lay down which AI models and applications may be used for which purposes, which inputs are permitted and how outputs may be reused, bindingly, for example in policies.
[SOURCE: BSI, Generative KI-Modelle, Chancen und Risiken für Industrie und Behörden, version 2.0 of 17 January 2025, measure M16, p. 49 · bsi.bund.de · retrieved 2026-10-18]

[MARKER: Fact]
The Austrian Economic Chamber publishes a free model policy with ten numbered paragraphs in its AI guidelines for small and medium businesses, third edition, April 2026. Several passages are out of date since the amending regulation of 8 July 2026.
[SOURCE: Wirtschaftskammer Österreich, KI-Guidelines für KMU, 3rd edition, April 2026, pp. 14 to 27 · wko.at · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[MARKER: Interpretation]
Our interpretation: these three bodies are German and Austrian. Their recommendations are professional practice, not law, and they bind nobody outside their countries. They are still the best published reasoning we found, and the nine areas of the policy follow them.

[SCREEN: handbook section 1, the box "Does an AI usage policy count as a reasonable step to keep information secret?", half-circle Uncertain]
[MARKER: Uncertain]
This is open: whether a policy counts as a reasonable step to keep a trade secret. No court decision was searched. Without a rule there is no evidence of any step; with a rule the business can show it took one. Whether it is reasonable is decided case by case.
[SOURCE: Directive (EU) 2016/943, Art. 2(1)(a) to (c) · EUR-Lex · retrieved 2026-10-18; § 26b(1)(3) UWG (Austria) · ris.bka.gv.at; § 2 no. 1(b) GeschGehG (Germany) · gesetze-im-internet.de · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[MARKER: Uncertain]
This is open too: which authority supervises Article four in your country. Germany has named the Federal Network Agency, by an act in force since 29 July 2026. For Austria, as of 23 October 2026, no official source names the market surveillance authority. The duties apply regardless.
[SOURCE: Datenschutzbehörde (Austria), Künstliche Intelligenz und Datenschutz · dsb.gv.at · retrieved 2026-10-23; RIS, Federal Law Gazette, full-text search · retrieved 2026-10-23; KI-MIG of 22 July 2026 (BGBl. 2026 I No. 223), §§ 2, 6, 8, 15 · gesetze-im-internet.de · retrieved 2026-10-23]

### 5 The example business
[SCREEN: A9, questions 1 to 4 with the prefilled example business; overlay "Example Electrical Ltd · fictional"]
The generator is prefilled with a fictional business, so that no real person appears. Example Electrical Ltd: an electrical contractor in Austria with ten to forty-nine staff. An owner who is also the management, office staff, field staff and one apprentice. Three people in the office use a text assistant for quotes, summaries and translations. Two field staff set it up privately on their phones. Nobody here is real.

[SCREEN: the memo result page for these answers: red lamp, title "Start here: one step before anything else.", the first step]
Put that situation into the memo and the lamp is red, because the accounts are private. The first step reads: set up business accounts with training on inputs switched off, and post the one page: what never goes into a tool.

[SCREEN: sample D1, page 1: head with effective date, version line, responsible person; scroll to section 1]
In the policy, section one names the tools and the access. For the two field staff that means: either they get access through a business account, or their work use on the private phone ends on the effective date. "As before" is not an option here.

[SCREEN: sample D1, sections 2 and 3]
Section two lists the uses that actually occur: quote drafts, summaries of manufacturer texts, translations for the two field staff, wording help for complaints. Everything that concerns applications or the assessment of individual employees is excluded. Section three adds two things to the list of what never goes in: the calculation sheets per job and the access codes to customer sites.

[SCREEN: sample D2, the one page]
The one page takes its content from the policy, so that poster and policy cannot contradict each other: what never goes in, with boxes to tick during the briefing; what is checked before use; who is asked and where incidents are reported.

[SCREEN: A15, sample D7, the status lines]
And the evidence summary: one page, dated, with a document number, and one line per piece of evidence with the status regulated, planned or open. The example business chose a review after twelve months and on every occasion. Open items are listed, not hidden.

<short pause>

### 6 What it is not
[SCREEN: A4, footer "Source-based. No legal advice."]
It is not legal advice, and no document claims that your business complies with a rule. The documents show what you decided, since when, for which tools, and who knows about it.

[SCREEN: handbook online, section 8, the table of open points; hold on 8.4, 8.9, 8.10, 8.11 and 8.12; overlay "17 points · 8 open"]
The handbook of the toolkit lists seventeen points as of 23 October 2026. Eight remain open, and we name the ones that touch this video. Which authority supervises in Austria. How often the briefing is repeated and how long the record is kept. Whether a policy is a reasonable step to keep trade secrets. Whether the policy binds freelancers and contractors.

[MARKER: Fact]
On that last one: the Commission writes that Article four also covers contractors and service providers under the organisational remit; how a policy binds them is contract law, which we did not survey.
[SOURCE: European Commission, AI literacy, questions and answers, page last updated 20 October 2026 · digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu · retrieved 2026-10-23]

[MARKER: Uncertain]
This is open: whether an AI tool triggers the participation of a works council. That is national labour law, and a business with a works council clarifies it with a specialist before procurement, not after.
[SOURCE: Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 88(1) · retrieved 2026-10-23; Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe of 6 May 2024, section 1.12, p. 8 · retrieved 2026-10-18; Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe of 6 May 2024, section 2.2, p. 10 · retrieved 2026-10-23]

And the nine areas are not an official catalogue. A business may merge or add areas without missing anything a source requires. Your business decides. The documents show what it decided.
