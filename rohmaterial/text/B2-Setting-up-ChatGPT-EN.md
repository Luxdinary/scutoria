PRELIMINARY NOTE
Purpose and structure
This practical guide is written for owners, managing directors and office
managers of companies from the one-person business to the medium-sized
company without an IT or security department of its own. You have decided
to use ChatGPT in your business, or someone in the company already does.
The question is no longer what the rules are — Volume 1 "AI in the Company"
and Volume 2 "AI Policies for the Workforce" answer that — but where to
click so that the company is set up in a way that keeps secrets out of
prompts, keeps anyone from sharing more than they meant to, and makes
sure an access really is closed on the last working day.
It shows a single route, step by step: four decisions before the first click, an account that belongs to the
company, the settings a company looks at once, an order for recurring tasks, one complete job, the exclusion
list, three rules for employees and a monthly review of twenty minutes. Every operating path is written out in
words — menu path, button, what you see afterwards — and, where a picture helps, with a screenshot.
If you know the first practical guide
This guide has the same structure as "Setting up Claude in your business". That is deliberate: anyone who
lays both side by side knows where to look things up. Sections 1, 6, 7 and 9 are almost word for word the same,
because they do not depend on the interface. Sections 2 to 5 and 8 are newly written, because they do. The
difference that matters is in Section 3: the setting for the use of your data sits in a different place here,
has a different name — and, depending on the account type, does not exist as a switch at all. Anyone
who has read the other guide would otherwise look in the wrong place. The same goes for Sections 3 and 4:
with this provider, connections to other services are available in the business account from the start, not
only after an approval. The rule "none, to begin with" is a hands-on step here, not an omission.
Version line
Provider information checked on 15 September 2026; interface described as of 15 September 2026 —
check for changes. Interfaces age. A menu item may be called something else tomorrow, a switch may sit

What this guide says about the provider — account types, defaults, retention periods, roles — comes from
the provider’s own help and privacy pages, not from memory, not from forums. Every such statement carries a
source line with page, date of access and address. Where the page says nothing, that is stated: "no
information on the page checked". A gap is a result; a plausible guess would be harm, because people act on
this guide. The gaps are collected in Section 10; the list of sources is in Section 12.

SCOPE
This practical guide is a manual on operation, not a guide to the law and not a legally
binding document. Wherever a legal question arises — what may go into an AI tool,

does not say which tool is better; whoever buys it has decided. It is no substitute for the
AI Evidence Kit: this guide sets things up, the kit sets the rules. And it names no provider
price, because prices age.

SECTION 1
Before you begin
Most companies set up an account first and think afterwards about who
may use it. That is the wrong order, and you only notice when the first file is
in the wrong place or the first colleague has an access that nobody knows
the purpose of.
Four decisions belong before that. None of them needs the interface; that is why this section has no picture.
Anyone who is already clicking here is not deciding.
1.1 Who may use it?
Not "everyone" and not "just me, let’s see". Write down two or three names, or a function: the office manager
and me. The reason is practical: every further access is a further place where something can get in that does
not belong there — and a further person you have to see off when they leave.
Whoever starts small can expand. Whoever starts wide has to gather things back in.
With this provider there is a particular point you should know now: a business account starts at two paid
seats, even if only one is occupied. A one-person business therefore decides here whether it takes the
second seat as a reserve — for the deputy, the tax adviser, the temporary help — or whether a personal
account on a company address is enough for now. Section 2 shows what distinguishes the two routes.
Quelle: OpenAI Help Center, "Managing billing and seats in ChatGPT Business", accessed 15 September 2026 (search excerpt),
help.openai.com/en/articles/8792536-managing-billing-and-seats-in-chatgpt-business
What Volume 2 says about this: the decision about the group of users lies with management, and it is the first
of four that come before any policy (Volume 2, Section 4).
1.2 For what?
Two or three concrete tasks, not a wish list. Preparing quotation texts. Summarising long e-mails. Turning
minutes into tasks.
That is not bureaucracy but the yardstick by which you will measure in three months whether it was worth it.
Without it, only a feeling remains. And it is the basis for Section 4: for a recurring task, a project with stored
instructions pays off; for a wish list, nothing does.
1.3 With what data?
This is where it gets serious, and this is where this guide stops being an operating manual. What may go into
an AI tool and what may not is not decided at the interface but by your company and by what applies to you.
What has to be considered is in Volume 1, Section 12.3 — where the outflow of data through prompts is
described as a risk — and in Section 2.4, where the contractual checkpoints are: data processing agreement,
place of processing, training with business data, liability for third-party rights. Section 2 of this guide says
which of the provider’s documents belongs to which checkpoint and where it is; whether it is sufficient for
your company, it does not say.
For a start, a single line that you draw yourself is enough: What you would not send to an external service
provider without a contract does not go in here either. Section 6 turns that into a list.

1.4 Who tidies up?
One person, by name. Once a month they check who has access, what is in the projects, which connections
to other services are active and whether what was decided three months ago still holds. In a one-person
business that is you — so put an appointment in your calendar, otherwise it will not happen.
Section 8 says what this person looks at.
THE FOUR DECISIONS AT A GLANCE
Who: names or functions. For what: two or three tasks. With what data: your own line,
grounded in Volume 1. Who tidies up: one person, one appointment. Everything else in this
guide assumes these four.
SECTION 2
Account and access
The question a company really asks here is not "which plan" but: Does this
run in my name or in the company's? An access on a private address is
convenient until that person leaves the company or is unavailable. Then the
access hangs on a mailbox that nobody can reach any more — and
everything in it with it.
2.1 Which kind of access fits
The provider offers two groups of accounts. On one side the personal accounts — the provider calls them
Free, Go, Plus and Pro — one account per person, without shared administration. On the other side the
business accounts: a shared working area the provider calls a workspace, with roles, central billing and the
removal of people. For small companies the offer is called Business (until August 2025 it was called Team;
older help pages and some addresses still carry the old name). For large organisations there is Enterprise,
which runs through the provider’s sales team and is only named in this guide, not described.

2.4 Who administers
One person administers, at least a second can reach the mailbox in an emergency. That is not a question of
size: in a one-person business the second person is your own fallback — a password deposited somewhere
that someone can reach when it matters.
The Business workspace knows four roles. Owners have full access including billing, identity management
and configuration of the workspace and can appoint further Owners. Admins manage invitations and routine
member settings. Analytics Viewers have limited rights for analytics. Members use ChatGPT fully and can
create GPTs, but administer nothing.

NOT LIKE THIS
Merging an employee’s private account into the company workspace "so that nothing
gets lost". Afterwards, private conversations belong to the workspace, and the person can
no longer reach any of them after leaving. The better way: the workspace starts empty;
whatever is needed from the private account, the person brings over deliberately and
individually — as text, as a file, into a project. What happens to projects and GPTs in a
merge is not stated on the page checked (Section 10, no. 1).

What happens to what this person created — that is the question that counts when it matters, and the

Projects and GPTs of the removed person pass to an Owner of the workspace and are not flagged for

SECTION 3
The settings to check first
Defaults are made for the average case, not for your company. That is no
reproach to the provider — it only means that you have to go through once
what applies to you.
Every setting gets three things here: where it sits, what it does, why it concerns a company. The third is
the reason someone buys this guide.
You have to tell two places apart, or you will look in the wrong one. The personal settings are reached by
every person via their profile menu at the bottom left or top right, then Settings; they apply to that one
person. The Workspace settings are reached only by Owners and Admins, via the same profile menu; they
apply to everyone in the workspace. The most important things in this section are in the workspace settings.
Anyone using a personal account only has the first place.
3.1 What happens to your prompts — the switch that sits somewhere else here
The most important setting of all, and the only one about which this guide says: look for yourself, today.
What a provider does with entered content can change, and what is printed here is always a snapshot.
If you know the other practical guide in the series: there, this switch sits under Settings, Privacy, and
applies to consumer accounts. Here it sits in a different place, has a different name — and in the account type
this guide recommends, it does not exist as a per-person switch at all. So do not look for "Privacy".
In a personal account (Free, Plus, Pro; for Go, no information on the page checked) the use of your prompts
to improve the models is, according to the provider, switched on by default. The path to switching it off:
profile menu, Settings, Data controls, switch Improve the model for everyone — set to off. The provider
says: if the switch is on, it may use content you have shared with ChatGPT, including conversations and
saved memories, to improve its models; after switching off, new conversations are no longer used for that.
The history remains; switching off deletes nothing.

SECTION 6
What never goes in
This section is the shortest and the most important. It does not answer the
question of what is permitted — that is decided by your company and by
what applies to you, and that is what Volume 1 is for. It answers the question
where there is no weighing up.
The list is grounded in Volume 1, Section 11 (data protection, especially 11.1 and 11.2) and Section 12.3 (AI-
specific risks). This guide lists, it does not justify. The list is word for word the same as in "Setting up Claude
in your business", because it does not depend on the tool.
6.1 The list
Access data. Passwords, keys, recovery codes, access links. Not even "just for formatting", not even in a
picture.
Health data. Diagnoses, findings, sick leave with names — including those of your own employees, including
in a list you "just want summarised".
Personnel data. Applications, appraisals, salaries, warnings, dismissals. The temptation is great, because
precisely these texts are tiresome to write. The text may be created — the person in it stays anonymous until
the paper leaves the company.
Customer data without a basis. In practice the most frequent case, and the only one with a condition:
without a basis. Whether there is one is not stated here but in Volume 1, Sections 11.1 and 11.2.
Whatever is under a duty of confidentiality. Client, professional or contractual secrets. If you would not be
allowed to send it to a service provider without a contract, you are not allowed to enter it here.
Third-party documents without permission. Competitors' quotations, forwarded contracts, everything
that belongs to someone else and is merely in your hands.
6.2 The rule of thumb for everything not on the list
Would it be uncomfortable for you if the person concerned learned that you had entered
this text here?
If yes: do not enter it, or take the names out first. The question replaces no legal review; it only catches the
cases in which you already have an uneasy feeling and push it aside.

can get in, and at each the list applies in the same way:
in the instruction of a project and in custom instructions (Section 4.2) — there it stays permanently;
in the files of a project and in the Library (Section 4.3);
in memories, if memory is switched on (Section 3.2) — there it turns up again in other conversations;

6.4 If something has got in after all
No drama, but no shrug either. In order:
1. Delete — the conversation, not just the single message. The path is in Section 3.4: in the sidebar the

3. Note what happened. One sentence with a date is enough. Whoever notes nothing cannot say later
