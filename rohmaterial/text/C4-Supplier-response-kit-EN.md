PRELIMINARY NOTE
Preface — Purpose and structure
In brief: Who this kit is for, what it contains and what it is not; in which order
the files are filled in; what you never promise the customer.
This kit is written for the management of small and medium-sized businesses that have received a security
questionnaire from a customer — with a deadline, with thirty or more questions, and without anyone in the
company ever having answered such a thing before.
The occasion is almost always the same. The customer is large enough to fall under rules that direct it to vet
its suppliers. It passes that vetting on: to the installation firm, the engineering office, the software developer,
the agency, the parts supplier. The questionnaire is the form this takes. Whether the answering business
itself falls under any of these rules is irrelevant — usually it does not, and the questionnaire arrives anyway.
What the kit is
Six ready-to-fill files and this guide. For every question that recurs in such questionnaires, the files provide
three model answers: one for the case that the business has what is asked; one if it partly has it; one if it
does not have it yet — with a date. In addition, a self-disclosure that you fill in once and enclose with every
questionnaire, a list of the evidence a business without an IT department can produce, a cover letter, a
checklist for the contract clauses customers require, and a sheet with the minimum that must be written
down before any answer.
The rule that distinguishes this kit from a collection of templates: every gap is marked and explained.
Wherever you have to decide or enter something yourself, there is a square bracket, highlighted in yellow in
the Word files, and next to it a sentence explaining what the entry means. A template in which you only insert
the company name would assert things on your behalf that you cannot oversee.
What the kit is not
It does not determine whether your business falls under the NIS2 Directive, the Austrian NISG 2026, the
DORA Regulation or the Cyber Resilience Act. For the first of these questions there is Scutoria’s free NIS2
self-check; the context is explained in handbook A3 “What your major customer requires of you”. This kit
assumes the questionnaire is already there and helps you answer it.
It contains no assurance that an answer will satisfy the customer. What satisfies is decided by the customer
according to its own risk assessment; the kit can only make sure that your answer is true, complete and
traceable.
It is not legal advice. No model answer claims that anything is legally required. Every question names the
source it comes from — an article of the directive, a clause of the implementing regulation, the title of a
standard, an insurers' questionnaire — but that source explains the customer’s question, not an obligation of
your business. Wherever a question concerns your contract, your liability or the permissibility of a measure, it
says “check with a professional”, and that is meant literally.
And: nobody fills in the questionnaire for you. The answers describe your business; only you can do that.
Structure
Four parts. Part I explains in one section why you are being asked and demonstrates the three answer levels
with examples from a business with eight employees. Part II devotes one section to each of the six files, in
the order in which they are filled in: first the sheet “Write down first”, then the question catalogue, the self-

<!-- Seite 3 -->
disclosure, the evidence list, the cover letter, and last the clause checklist. Part III deals with the moment
after sending: the customer’s follow-up questions. Part IV contains the uncertainties of this guide, a
glossary of questionnaire terms and the list of sources.
Order
If you have little time, work in this order and stop when the deadline demands it:
1. “Write down first” — the seven boxes. Without them no answer holds.
2. Question catalogue — choose the level for each question and adapt the model answer.
3. Self-disclosure — fill in once, date, sign.
4. Evidence list — what is in place gets an attachment number; what is missing gets a date.
5. Cover letter — the gaps in one paragraph, with dates.
6. Clause checklist — only once a contract or contract addendum is presented for signature.
What you never promise the customer
Four things appear in no file of this kit, and they should not appear in your answer either:
that your business “meets all requirements” of a directive or regulation — you cannot know that, and the
obligations there are not yours;
that your systems are “secure” — nobody can guarantee that; say what you do;
a deadline, an audit or a liability that you cannot keep, deliver or bear;
a “yes” that falls apart at the first follow-up question.
An honest answer with a date is more useful to the customer than a false yes. It has to be able to assess its
suppliers; it can only do so with true information. That is the basic idea of this kit, and it recurs in every file.
Handling of sources
Every question in the catalogue, every field of the self-disclosure and every clause in the checklist carries a
reference. These references come from research conducted in September 2026 under difficult conditions:
the official legal portals were not reachable from the working environment, the wording of the Union’s legal
acts was read from unofficial full-text copies, the Austrian NISG 2026 only via search excerpts. What that
means for reliability is set out in section 9. Where a question has no public source — for instance the
question about cyber insurance — this is stated explicitly in the column “Source of the question”. No
questionnaire was invented; the questions are assembled from the documented sources.

SECTION 1
Why you are being asked, and how to answer
In brief: Three sets of rules direct large customers to vet their suppliers; the
questionnaire is the consequence. The three answer levels, with examples
from a business with eight employees.
The questionnaire is not a claim that your business is subject to a rule. It is the customer’s attempt to
meet an obligation of its own. Whoever understands that answers more calmly — and more honestly.
1.1 Three sets of rules, one mechanism
The NIS2 Directive requires the entities it covers — energy suppliers, hospitals, transport operators, larger
manufacturers of certain goods, digital services and many more — to take measures for the security of their
network and information systems. These measures expressly include “supply chain security, including
security-related aspects concerning the relationships between each entity and its direct suppliers or service
providers”. In doing so the entity is to take into account “the specific vulnerabilities of each direct supplier
and service provider and the overall quality of products and cybersecurity practices of their suppliers”.
Source: Art. 21(2)(d) and (3) Directive (EU) 2022/2555, OJ L 333, 27.12.2022, p. 80; read from an unofficial full-text copy, reproduced in
research/lieferkette/01, section 1.1; official text eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022L2555 (accessed 14
September 2026).
The addressee of these sentences is the entity, never the supplier. But the entity can only take its supplier’s
“specific vulnerabilities” into account if it knows them. Hence the questionnaire. It can only assess its
“cybersecurity practices” if the supplier describes them. Hence the questions about policy, backups,
updates. And it is to include security measures “in contractual arrangements with their direct suppliers”.
Hence the clauses.
Source: recital 85 Directive (EU) 2022/2555, as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
In Austria the NISG 2026 transposes this requirement. According to the information documented in the
research it enters into force on 1 October 2026; covered entities have to register and, within twelve months,
submit a self-declaration on their measures, which according to the available excerpts also covers
information on supply chain security. Whoever has to provide that information will have questioned its
suppliers beforehand. The wording of the relevant sections could not be read against the official text for this
kit — check current status, see section 9.
Source: sections 33(1) and 51 NISG 2026, Federal Law Gazette I No 94/2025, ris.bka.gv.at/eli/bgbl/I/2025/94; section 33 from a search
excerpt (research/lieferkette/01, section 1.2), section 51 confirmed in the browser on 10 September 2026 (research/nis2/05, section 4)
(accessed 14 September 2026).
The second set of rules is DORA, the regulation on digital operational resilience for the financial sector. It has
applied since 17 January 2025 to banks, insurers, payment service providers, investment firms and other
financial entities — and it prescribes to them what every contract on ICT services must contain. Financial
entities may “only enter into contractual arrangements with ICT third-party service providers that comply
with appropriate information security standards”. Whoever supplies software, hosting, IT operations or other
digital services to a financial entity is such a third-party provider, regardless of size.
Source: Art. 28(5), Art. 30(2) and Art. 64 Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, OJ L 333, 27.12.2022, p. 1; unofficial full-text copy,
research/lieferkette/01, section 2; official text eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2554 (accessed 14
September 2026).

<!-- Seite 7 -->
The third is the Cyber Resilience Act for products with digital elements. It obliges manufacturers to exercise
due diligence when integrating sourced components. A small software or component supplier delivering to a
manufacturer is therefore asked how long it provides updates and how it handles vulnerabilities. The
manufacturer obligations apply from 11 December 2027; the questions arrive earlier.
Source: Art. 13(5) and Art. 71(2) Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, OJ L, 20.11.2024; unofficial full-text copy in English, research/lieferkette/01,
section 3 (accessed 14 September 2026).
1.2 What the questionnaires contain
There is no official model supplier questionnaire for any of the three sets of rules. What customers send out
is assembled from four sources: the contract requirements of the legal acts themselves, the titles of the
ISO/IEC 27001 controls on supplier relationships, assessment schemes for small businesses such as DIN
SPEC 27076, and the questionnaires of insurers. The questions in this kit are compiled from exactly these
sources; each carries its reference.
Source: research/lieferkette/01-zulieferer-pflichten.md, section 4 “What questionnaires ask”, with the sources Q4, Q7, Q9, Q24 to Q30
named there (accessed 14 September 2026).
The twelve topics of the question catalogue are: organisation and responsibility; access and identity;
backups; emergency and business continuity; updates and device protection; incidents and reporting; staff
and training; subcontractors; data protection and encryption; evidence, audit rights and termination;
insurance; location and data. A questionnaire rarely asks everything; most ask half.
1.3 The three answer levels
Every question in the catalogue has three model answers. You choose the level — honestly.
Level 1: we have it. What is asked exists, is written down and can be evidenced. The answer states what
applies, since when, who is responsible and which attachment shows it.
Level 2: partly. One part exists, one part is missing. The answer separates the two clearly and names a date
for the missing part.
Level 3: not yet — with a date. What is asked does not exist. The answer says so, says what applies instead,
and names a date by which it will exist. This is the most important level of the kit: a “not yet, by the end of the
quarter” is a complete, usable answer. A “yes” that is not true is not.
Add to this not applicable — for questions that do not fit your service, always with the reason.
WHY LEVEL 3 IS THE MOST IMPORTANT
The customer has to assess its suppliers. An assessment based on false information is
worthless to it, and if it comes to light it is expensive for you: then not only the one answer
is in question, but every one. A named shortcoming with a date, on the other hand, is
something it can work with. It sees that you know your business. That is the actual
information a questionnaire is looking for.
1.4 A business with eight employees
The following examples come from a fictitious installation business with eight employees that has
maintenance contracts with an energy supplier. The supplier has sent it a questionnaire with 34 questions,
deadline four weeks. The names are placeholders, as in the files.

<!-- Seite 8 -->
Question: “Do you have a written security policy?” The business has none. There is a notice in the
workshop saying who may unlock the server cabinet, and the managing director sets up all accounts herself.
Level 3:
Not yet. There is no written policy so far; in practice only management sets up accounts,
and access to the server room is limited to two people. From now on management is
responsible. We will provide a policy by 31 December 2026.
Question: “Is your data backed up regularly, and have you tested the restore?” The accounts are backed
up automatically to a provider every night; the workshop files are on a computer that is occasionally copied to
an external hard disk; nobody has ever tried a restore. Level 2:
Partly. The accounts are backed up automatically every day; the workshop files have so far
been backed up manually and irregularly. We will move them to the same automatic backup
by 15 November 2026. A full restore test is outstanding and scheduled for 30 November
2026; we will forward the log.
Question: “Do you report security incidents to us within 24 hours?” The business has never reported an
incident and has no reporting route. But the managing director can be reached by phone at any time, and the
questionnaire names a contact point at the energy supplier. Level 3, but with a commitment the business can
keep:
So far there is no defined route for reporting to customers. From now on: incidents that
could affect your systems or data are reported by management within 24 hours of
becoming aware, by phone to your contact point, and confirmed in writing. We will define
deputy and availability outside office hours by 31 October 2026 and inform you.
Question: “Do you carry out background checks for persons with access to our systems?” The business
has no access to the supplier’s systems; its fitters enter its facilities with visitor passes. Not applicable, with
reason:
Not applicable: our staff have no access to your network and information systems. They
enter your facilities as registered visitors under your rules. Should this change, we will first
clarify with a professional which check is permissible and sensible for us, and agree it with
you.
Question: “Is there cyber insurance?” No, and the business has never considered it. Level 3 — and the
counter-question that makes the answer usable in the first place:
No, no cyber insurance at present. We will decide by 31 December 2026 whether to take
one out. If you make cover a condition, please state the sum you expect.
Four of the five answers are “not yet” or “partly”, and the business has answered well: the supplier knows
where it stands, who is responsible and when what will change. The dates lie between six weeks and three
months, none in the holiday season, each assigned to one person.

1.5 What the answers rest on
All five answers above rest on things the business knew beforehand or could establish in an afternoon: who
sets up accounts, what is backed up, who can be reached, whether there is access to customer systems,
whether a policy exists. That is no coincidence. A questionnaire can only be answered if seven things are
settled in the business: responsibilities, backup, access, emergency, training, subcontractors, reporting
route. They are on the sheet “Write down first”, with which part II begins.

SECTION 2
Write down first
In brief: The file “vorher-aufschreiben-en.docx”: seven boxes that must be
ticked before any answer, with a sentence on where each comes from.
No questionnaire can be answered from memory. Before you touch the first question, seven things
must be written down in your business. The sheet takes an afternoon if you take it seriously — and a
week if you count the restore test.
2.1 What the sheet is for
The model answers in the question catalogue are only as good as what you enter in the square brackets.
“[Function, name] is responsible” presupposes that someone is responsible. “On [date] we restored [what]”
presupposes a test that took place. The sheet “Write down first” collects these preconditions in one place:
seven rows, one box each, one sentence on what must be written down, and one sentence on what to create
it with.
The seven items are not arbitrary. They are what the documented questionnaire sources most often
presuppose, and according to the research they already cover seven of the twelve topics of the catalogue.
Source: research/lieferkette/01-zulieferer-pflichten.md, section 5, table “Evidence — form — how it is created — answers” and the paragraph
“Order by effort” (accessed 14 September 2026).
2.2 The seven items
Responsibilities. Who is responsible for information security, who for backups, who for reports, who
deputises — with phone number and e-mail address. In a business with eight employees this is usually the
management itself; it is fine to write that. Scutoria’s free sheet “Responsibilities” is made for this; signed by
management, it is the first attachment of every answer. The customer asks because, under the implementing
regulation to the NIS2 Directive, it is to keep a contact point per supplier.
Source: Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, Annex point 5.2(a); unofficial copy, research/lieferkette/01, section 1.3; official text eur-
lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2024/2690/oj (accessed 14 September 2026).
Backup. What is backed up how often to where, where the separate copy is, when the restore was last
tested. The free Backup Check walks you through the questions; the restore test you have to do yourself —
restore one folder, time it, note the result. This test is the point at which the sheet takes a week instead of an
afternoon. It is worthwhile regardless of the questionnaire.
Access. Who has which accounts, where the second login step is active, who is an administrator, what
happens when someone leaves. The free Login Check asks the questions; the account list is copied from the
admin view of each service — without passwords.
Emergency. Whom to call, what first, in which order to restore, how long the business can stand still. The free
sheet “Emergency Card” is the format; the outage time comes from the restore test.
Training. Who was briefed when on what, with an attendance list. Half an hour on phishing, passwords and
the reporting route, held by management itself, counts — if it is recorded.
Subcontractors. Which services and suppliers touch customer data: provider, country, purpose, contract.
The e-mail provider, the file storage, the accounting program, the website host belong in it. Scutoria’s free
Inventory tool shows the shape of such a list; the country is in the respective provider’s terms.

<!-- Seite 12 -->
Reporting route. Within what time do you report to a customer an incident that could affect it, via which
route, by whom. Choose the period so that you can keep it — at night too, on holiday too. It goes on the
emergency card.
2.3 Why the reporting route carries so much weight
Of the seven items, the reporting route is the one the customer needs most urgently. Covered entities must
report significant incidents under the directive “without undue delay and in any event within 24 hours of
becoming aware” as an early warning; the NISG 2026 adopts this period. The customer’s clock starts when it
becomes aware — and if the incident happens at its supplier, it only becomes aware when the supplier
reports. That is why the implementing regulation requires suppliers to notify incidents “without undue delay”,
and why the reporting period is in almost every contract.
Source: Art. 23(4)(a) Directive (EU) 2022/2555 and section 34(2)(1) NISG 2026, both read in the browser on 10 September 2026
(research/nis2/05, section 5); Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, Annex point 5.1.4(d), unofficial copy (research/lieferkette/01,
section 1.3) (accessed 14 September 2026).
For you this does not mean that you must commit to a 24-hour period. It means you should know which
period you can commit to before the customer writes it into the contract. A business with office hours and
one person who holds the phone can commit to “within 24 hours of becoming aware, by phone, by
management or its deputy”. It cannot commit to “within two hours, around the clock”. The difference belongs
on the sheet.
2.4 Filling it in
The file is a table with five columns: box, item, what must be written down, where it comes from, done on. The
box is only ticked once the item is on paper or in a file, with a date. The last column takes the date and the
number under which the document will later run as an attachment to the self-disclosure.
Whoever has ticked all seven boxes can answer every question in the topics organisation, access, backup,
emergency, incidents, training and subcontractors at least at the level “partly”. For the remaining five topics —
updates, data protection, evidence, insurance, location — a look at the supplier list and the device list is
usually enough.
HOW YOU KNOW IT IS ENOUGH
Seven boxes ticked, each with a date, each document filed. The sheet asks for no more.
Whether it satisfies the customer is for the customer to decide — but it will rarely demand
more than a business of your size can deliver once it sees that the basics are in place.

3.2 The twelve topics
Organisation and responsibility (four questions): policy, responsible persons, review, role in the supply
chain. The questions come from the titles of the ISO/IEC 27001 controls on supplier relationships, from the
topic “Organisation” of DIN SPEC 27076 and from the implementing regulation, which requires entities to
define their role in the supply chain and communicate it to their suppliers.
Source: ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Annex A 5.19 to 5.23 (titles only, standard is paid; secondary sources research/lieferkette/01, Q24); DIN SPEC
27076 (BSI pages via web search, Q25); Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, Annex points 5.1.1 and 5.2 (accessed 14 September 2026).
Access and identity (five questions): individual accounts, second login step, withdrawal of rights,
administrator rights, passwords. The directive lists “access control policies” and “multi-factor
authentication” among the minimum measures; the insurers require “individual login credentials” and “strong
passwords” as the basis of cover.
Source: Art. 21(2)(i) and (j) Directive (EU) 2022/2555; GDV, general cyber insurance conditions (February 2024) and model cyber risk
questionnaire, both via web search (research/lieferkette/01, Q26) (accessed 14 September 2026).
Backups (four questions): regularity, separate storage, tested restore, encryption. Emergency and
business continuity (four questions): emergency plan, rehearsal, recovery time, substitutes. In both topics
the restore test provides the answer one should not estimate: whether the backup holds, and how long
recovery takes.
Updates and device protection (five questions): update period, devices without manufacturer support,
reporting route for vulnerabilities, support period of own products, anti-malware protection and firewall. The
fourth question concerns only software and device suppliers; it comes from the Cyber Resilience Act, which

<!-- Seite 14 -->
obliges manufacturers to exercise due diligence for sourced components. For everyone else it is “not
applicable”.
Source: Art. 13(5) and (8) Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, unofficial English full-text copy (research/lieferkette/01, section 3.4); Art. 21(2)(e)
Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (accessed 14 September 2026).
Incidents and reporting (four questions): reporting period, reporting route, incidents of the last 24 months,
assistance in investigation. The question about past incidents has no public source with wording; it is
common in customer and insurer questionnaires, and the source column says so. Answer it truthfully — an
incident that comes to light later costs more than one disclosed.
Staff and training (four questions): briefing, confidentiality, background check, participation in the
customer’s programmes. The background check is the most delicate question in the catalogue: what a
business may check is a legal question. The model answers therefore say what the business actually does
and refer permissibility to a professional.
Source: Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, Annex point 5.1.4(b) and (c); Art. 30(2)(i) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (accessed 14
September 2026).
Subcontractors and own suppliers (four questions): list, contractual requirements, notice before change,
monitoring. For DORA customers this is the master data query for their register of information: they must
know whom their service provider uses and where. Whoever cannot say counts as hard to monitor.
Source: Art. 28(3), Art. 29(2) and Art. 30(2)(a) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (accessed 14 September 2026).
Data protection and encryption (three questions): data processing on behalf, encryption, deletion.
Whether a data processing agreement is needed in a given case is not settled by this kit; the term is the one
from the glossary of volume 1 “AI in the Company”, and the decision belongs with a professional.
Evidence, audit rights and termination (five questions): certificates, audit rights, return and deletion,
notice periods and transition, cooperation with authorities. Here the questions of the questionnaire and the
clauses of the contract converge; what you commit to in the questionnaire, you should later also be able to
commit to in the contract. Section 7 deals with the clauses.
Insurance (two questions): cyber insurance, business liability. Neither question has a legal basis in the three
sets of rules or a public questionnaire source with wording; they are customer practice. “No” is a complete
answer — with the counter-question whether the customer makes cover a condition.
Location and data (four questions): processing location, cloud services, notice of change of location,
access to the customer’s systems. For DORA customers the processing location is a minimum content of
every contract: “the regions or countries” where data is processed, “including the data storage location”.
Source: Art. 30(2)(b) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (accessed 14 September 2026).
3.3 How to fill it in
Place the customer’s questionnaire next to the catalogue. Go through its questions in order and find the
matching catalogue question for each; the topics help you find them. Then:
1. Choose the level. The drop-down in “Your level” has four entries. Choose the one that is true — not the
one that looks good.
2. Take over the model answer. Copy the model answer of the chosen level into “Your answer”.
3. Fill the brackets. Every square bracket is a gap: date, function, rhythm, period, attachment. What you do
not know, look up in the sheet “Write down first”. If it is not there, perhaps the level is wrong.
4. Put it in your own words. The model answers are deliberately sober. Cut what does not apply to your
business; add what makes it special. A sentence that sounds like a template convinces the customer less
than one that sounds like your business.

<!-- Seite 15 -->
5. Enter the date. For level 3 in the last column; for level 1 the attachment number.
You then transfer the answers into the customer’s form. If it provides no form but only a list of questions, you
can enclose the completed sheet as an attachment.
3.4 What the catalogue cannot do
It does not know the questions only this one customer asks. For them the same method applies: choose the
level, answer truthfully, name a date. And it cannot know whether your answer will be enough — a
questionnaire is an assessment by the customer, not an examination with a fixed pass mark.
NOT LIKE THIS
Answering every question with “we have it” because the order matters. At the first follow-
up — “Please send us your policy” — the answer falls apart, and with it the trust in all the
others. The catalogue is built so that you do not have to do that.
SECTION 4
The self-disclosure
In brief: The file “selbstauskunft-en.docx” (plus a sample view as PDF): 32
fields in nine sections, filled in once, dated, signed — the attachment that
goes with every questionnaire.
The question catalogue answers the questionnaire you have right now. The self-disclosure answers the
next one before it arrives. Whoever has filled it in once encloses a dated document with the second
questionnaire and answers only what goes beyond it.
4.1 What the self-disclosure is for
Questionnaires from different customers ask the same things eighty per cent of the time, only in a different
order and different words. The self-disclosure gathers this common ground in one document: nine sections,
32 fields, each with the answer type and an italic sentence on what is asked. It describes the state of your
business on a particular day, carries a signature and a list of attachments, and ends with the sentence that
puts it in its place:
This self-disclosure describes the situation as at [date]. It is not a certification and does
not establish any legal consequence.
The fields come from the research for this kit; each names the source that justifies it, and without a source
there is no field.
Source: research/lieferkette/01-zulieferer-pflichten.md, section 6 “Fields of the supplier self-disclosure” (accessed 14 September 2026).

<!-- Seite 16 -->
4.2 The nine sections
A — Business and role (six fields): master data, contact person, size, type of service, access to the
customer’s systems, processing locations. This is what the customer enters in its register of suppliers. Size is
stated openly here because it is the yardstick for what can proportionately be expected of you: the directive
expressly names “the size of the entity” among the criteria of proportionality.
Source: Art. 21(1), second subparagraph, Directive (EU) 2022/2555; Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, Annex point 5.2; Art. 28(3)
Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (accessed 14 September 2026).
B — Organisation (three fields): policy, responsible person, cyber insurance. The third field has no legal basis
and is here nonetheless because it appears in almost every questionnaire; “no” is a complete answer.
C — Access (four fields): individual accounts, second login step for e-mail, remote access and
administration, withdrawal of rights on leaving, limited administrator rights. The field on the second login step
requires three answers, not one.
D — Backup and operations (four fields): backup, tested restore, recovery time, emergency plan.
E — Updates and vulnerabilities (three fields): update period, reporting route for vulnerabilities, support
period of own products. The third field applies only to software and device suppliers.
F — Incidents and reporting (three fields): reporting period to the customer, reporting route, incidents of the
last 24 months.
G — Staff and training (three fields): briefing, confidentiality undertaking, background check.
H — Own suppliers (three fields): subcontractors with country and purpose, contractual requirements,
notice before change.
I — Evidence and termination (three fields): certificates, audit reports or audits, return and deletion at the
end of the contract.
4.3 Filling it in
You fill in the self-disclosure after the sheet “Write down first” has been ticked off — not before. Then it takes
two hours. For every field the same rule as in the catalogue applies: “yes”, “partly”, “not yet — by [date]” or “not
applicable, because …”. A field is never left empty; the customer reads an empty field as “no” or as “not
understood”.
The header details — business, customer, date, prepared by — you enter afresh for each recipient; the rest
stays the same as long as nothing changes in the business. The list of attachments at the end names the
nine pieces of evidence from the evidence list; what is in place gets a date and a tick, what is missing stays
unticked and is named as a gap in the cover letter.
Finally the signature. It belongs to management. A self-disclosure signed by an office clerk will not be read by
the customer as a statement of the business.
4.4 The PDF
A sample view as PDF belongs to the file. It shows what the completed self-disclosure should look like and
serves as a model for printing. The Word file is what you fill in; the PDF you send the customer you generate
from it after all yellow parts are filled and the italic explanatory sentences are deleted. A PDF with yellow
brackets is a draft, not a document.

4.5 When to renew it
The self-disclosure describes one day. It is to be renewed when something material changes — a new service
provider for e-mail or storage, a new contact person, a completed restore test, an introduced second login
step — and in any case once a year. Whoever keeps the dates from level 3 has, after a year, a self-disclosure in
which “not yet” has everywhere become “yes”. That is the real return of the kit, and it only shows with the
second questionnaire.
FOR CUSTOMERS IN THE FINANCIAL SECTOR
If the customer is a financial entity, the scope of what it must require depends on whether
it classifies your service as supporting a “critical or important function”. That is its
decision, not yours. Ask it in writing before you send off the self-disclosure — the question
is a sentence in the cover letter. If the answer is “yes”, the six additional contract contents
from part C of the clause checklist are coming your way.
Source: Art. 28(4)(a) and Art. 30(3) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (accessed 14 September 2026).
SECTION 5
The evidence list
In brief: The file “nachweisliste-en.xlsx”: thirteen pieces of evidence that
questionnaires typically ask for — what a small business can realistically
provide, how it creates them, and whether they are in place.
A questionnaire does not only ask; it wants to see. “Please enclose your policy”, “evidence of the last
backup”, “copy of the certificate”. The evidence list says what of this a business without an IT
department can produce with its own means — and in which order.
5.1 Structure
One sheet, thirteen rows, ten columns. For each piece of evidence it states what a questionnaire typically
asks for, what a small business can realistically provide, how the evidence is created — with reference to the
free Scutoria tools where one exists —, which source justifies the question and which topic of the catalogue
it answers. The last three columns are yellow: “In place” with the choice yes, partly, no; the date; the
attachment number for the self-disclosure. Below the table three formulas count how many pieces of
evidence are marked yes, partly and no.
Source: research/lieferkette/01-zulieferer-pflichten.md, section 5, table of evidence (accessed 14 September 2026).
5.2 The thirteen pieces of evidence, ordered by effort
The order of the list is the order of effort. The first three are done in an afternoon because the sheets for
them exist:
1. Responsibilities sheet — function, name, availability, deputy; signed.
2. Emergency card with reporting route — whom to call, what first, where the backups are, the customer’s
contact point with deadline.
3. Supplier list — service, provider, country, purpose, contract.
According to the research these three already answer the topics organisation, incidents, termination and
supply chain — and thus all eight clauses of the implementing regulation at least with an honest answer.

The next four take days to a week:
4. Account list and access evidence — from the admin view of each service, without passwords; plus the
leaver procedure.
5. Backup plan and restore log — the plan is quick, the test takes time.
6. Update rule and update evidence — the rule in one sentence, a screenshot per device with date, a
device list.
7. Training record — attendance list with date, topic, signatures. A template for it is in Scutoria’s AI evidence

Then the documents that arise from the previous ones or come from third parties:
8. Security policy — two to four pages, written up from sheets 1 to 7; dated, signed. It is deliberately only

9. Confidentiality undertakings — the clause in the employment contract or a separate declaration; agree
the template with a professional.
10. Insurance confirmation — request from the insurer, or the statement “none”.
11. Data processing agreement — only if you process personal data on behalf of the customer; whether
that is the case is for a professional to clarify.
12. Certificate or label — rarely realistic for businesses with fewer than ten employees. What certification
and label cost and how long they take was not researched for this kit; that is in section 9.
13. Self-disclosure — everything above in one document; two hours once 1 to 5 are in place.
5.3 What the customer accepts instead of a certificate
The question about a certificate appears in most questionnaires; according to a survey by the European
agency for cybersecurity, 61 per cent of surveyed entities require a certification from their suppliers. The
Austrian Chamber of Commerce calls ISO/IEC 27001 “a very good basis”; whether it suffices as evidence is “to
be examined in each individual case depending on the respective risk assessment”. For a business with eight
employees an ISO certification is as a rule not the right instrument.
Source: ENISA, Good Practices for Supply Chain Cybersecurity, 13 June 2023, via web search (research/lieferkette/01, Q30); WKO, “Die
Sicherheit der Lieferkette im NISG 2026”, via web search (Q16) (accessed 14 September 2026).
What a small business can provide instead is exactly what this list produces: a dated self-disclosure with
attachments. The implementing regulation places the “right to receive audit reports” next to the right to
audit; a signed self-disclosure with backup log, account list and emergency card is such a report — not an
audit report by a third party, but a report the customer can assess. Whether it regards it as sufficient is its
decision. A self-declaration label such as the one offered in Austria can be an intermediate step; its criteria
could not be read for this kit.
Source: Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, Annex point 5.1.4(e) (accessed 14 September 2026); on the label research/lieferkette/01,
Q31, and section 9 of this guide.

SECTION 6
The cover letter
In brief: The file “antwortschreiben-en.docx”: the cover letter to the
customer, with gaps highlighted in yellow, explanatory sentences in italics
and the rule for naming gaps without endangering the order.
The questionnaire does not go back alone. A one-page cover letter tells the customer who you are, what
is enclosed, where the gaps are and by when they will be closed. It is the document the clerk reads first
— and often the only one the customer’s management ever sees.
6.1 Structure of the file
The file has two parts. On the first page are the rule for naming gaps and the sentences that do not belong in
the letter. From the second page on is the letter itself: seven paragraphs, each with gaps highlighted in yellow
in square brackets and an italic explanatory sentence below it saying why the paragraph is built that way. You
delete the explanatory sentences before sending; you do not send the first page.
6.2 The seven paragraphs
First paragraph: what is enclosed. Thanks for the questionnaire, date, list of attachments. No praise, no
apology. A letter that begins with “Unfortunately we cannot …” is already lost.
Second paragraph: who you are. Number of employees, no IT department of its own, responsible person,
contact for incidents. Size is stated openly in the letter because it is the yardstick of proportionality. A
customer who knows it is questioning a business with eight employees scales its requirements differently
from one who does not.
Source: Art. 21(1), second subparagraph, Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (“the size of the entity” as a criterion of proportionality); Art. 28(1)(b)
Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (accessed 14 September 2026).
Third paragraph: what you supply and what you touch. One sentence on the service, one sentence on
access and data — and the counter-question whether the customer classifies the service as supporting a
critical or important function. For customers in the financial sector this is the most important line of the
letter, because this classification determines which contract contents it must require. For all others the
question does no harm; it shows that you know what this is about.
Source: Art. 28(4)(a) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (accessed 14 September 2026).

<!-- Seite 20 -->
Fourth paragraph: the gaps. All points you cannot evidence, or can only partly evidence, in one paragraph,
each with three pieces of information: what is missing, what applies instead, by when. At most five points; if
you have more, group them by topic and refer to the questionnaire. The sentence “We have set the dates so
that we can keep them” is only true if you have checked the dates against the calendar.
Fifth paragraph: what does not apply. Questions that do not fit your service, with the reason in half a
sentence. “Not applicable” without a reason the customer reads as “not understood”.
Sixth paragraph: the classification. The documents describe the situation on a date, are not a certification
and establish no legal consequence. The sentence also appears in the self-disclosure; here it is repeated
deliberately. It protects you against the questionnaire later being read as a warranty.
Seventh paragraph: the open door. Contact for queries — and the note that contractual commitments
going beyond the practice described will only be agreed after review. This makes clear that the questionnaire
is a description and not a contract.
6.3 The rule for naming gaps
Five sentences that stand on the first page of the file and that one should have read once before writing:
1. Every gap is named, never hidden.
2. Every gap gets three pieces of information: what is missing, what applies instead, by when.
3. Gaps are collected in one paragraph, not scattered.
4. You promise only what the business will deliver; a date is a promise.
5. Where a question does not fit: “not applicable, because …” — never without a reason.
The rule sounds strict and is in truth a relief. Whoever follows it does not have to remember what was glossed
over where. And it is the reason why an answer with four gaps endangers the order less often than one
without: the customer is not looking for a supplier without gaps — there is none — but for one who knows its
gaps.
6.4 Sentences that do not belong in the letter
The first page of the file repeats the four sentences from the preface — “we meet all requirements”, “our
systems are secure”, “we accept unlimited liability”, “of course” before a first commitment — so that they are in
view while writing. The reason is always the same: the directive addresses the covered entity; the supplier is
asked, not obliged, and it commits to what it does, not to what it is.
Source: Art. 21(1) Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (“Member States shall ensure that essential and important entities …”), research/lieferkette/01,
section 1.1 (accessed 14 September 2026).
6.5 Filling in and sending
Fill the yellow brackets, delete the italic sentences, read the letter aloud once. What sounds like a template,
rewrite. Then management’s signature, attachments according to the list, dispatch by the route the
customer has specified — usually a portal or a particular address. Note the date of dispatch: it is the day from
which the dates in the fourth paragraph run, and the day on which you enter those dates in your own
calendar.

<!-- Seite 21 -->
ONE LETTER, TWO READERS
The customer’s clerk checks whether the information fits its scheme. Its management
checks whether this supplier is a risk. The letter serves both: the fourth paragraph gives
the clerk dates it can follow up; the second and sixth paragraphs give management the
picture of a business that knows what it is doing.
SECTION 7
The clause checklist
In brief: The file “klausel-pruefliste-en.docx”: 23 contract contents that
customers require — eight from the implementing regulation to the NIS2
Directive, fifteen from DORA —, for each clause what it means, what you
check before you sign, and what you should not commit to without being
able to deliver it.
After the questionnaire comes the contract. Often as an annex to the existing framework agreement,
sometimes as a “security agreement”, twelve pages, for signature within two weeks. The checklist says
which clauses you must expect and which questions to ask yourself about each before you sign. It is a
negotiation aid. It does not replace a contract review.
7.1 Why the clauses come
The implementing regulation to the NIS2 Directive is the only official text that says what an entity is to lay
down “in service level agreements” with its suppliers: eight points, from cybersecurity requirements to
obligations on termination. It applies directly only to certain digital providers — cloud, data centre, managed
services and a few more — but customers in other sectors adopt the list as a model because there is no
other; and according to the available excerpts the Austrian act allows the authority to extend it to further
sectors — check current status.
Source: Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 of 17 October 2024, Annex point 5.1.4(a) to (h); unofficial copy (research/lieferkette/01,
section 1.3); official text eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2024/2690/oj; section 32(5) NISG 2026 according to secondary sources
(research/lieferkette/01, section 1.2) (accessed 14 September 2026).
DORA goes further. It prescribes what every contract of a financial entity on ICT services must contain “at
least” — nine elements — and six more if the service supports a “critical or important function”. The small
service provider with a financial customer sees the nine in any case; the six only if its customer classifies it
that way.
Source: Art. 30(2) and (3) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554; unofficial copy (research/lieferkette/01, section 2.4) (accessed 14 September 2026).
7.2 Structure of the file
Three tables in landscape format, one row per clause with five columns: the clause with reference, what it
means, what you check before you sign, what you should not commit to without being able to deliver it, and a
box. Part A is the eight points of the implementing regulation, part B the nine DORA minimum contents, part C
the six additional ones. At the end four sentences for the conversation with the customer.
Where a clause touches liability, audit rights, termination or insolvency, the column “What you check” carries
the note “check with a professional”. These places are not marked for decoration. What a business signs
there can cost it more than the order is worth.

<!-- Seite 22 -->
7.3 Part A — the eight points
(a) Cybersecurity requirements for the supplier. Usually the topics of this kit. Check: whether every
measure named exists or can exist by a date; whether they fit your size. Do not commit to: a catalogue you
have not read; “state of the art” without a description of what is meant.
(b) Awareness, skills and training requirements, where appropriate certifications. Check: whether your
own briefing with an attendance list is enough; which certificates are meant. Do not commit to: certificates
nobody holds and that are not planned.
(c) Background checks of staff. Check: what you may legally check — with a professional. Do not commit to: a
“security clearance” without knowing what it covers.
(d) Notification of security incidents without undue delay. The clause you will see most often, because
the customer only keeps its own 24-hour deadline if you report immediately. Check: whether the required
period is compatible with your availability, at night and on holiday too. Do not commit to: periods in hours your
emergency card does not cover; reporting of “all” incidents without relation to the customer.
(e) Right to audit or to receive audit reports. Check: whether reports suffice — the self-disclosure with
attachments is one —; notice period, frequency, cost, third parties' premises — with a professional. Do not
commit to: unannounced or unlimited audit rights; audits at your subcontractors that you cannot arrange.
(f) Remediation of vulnerabilities. Check: whether you have a reporting route; whether periods are graded
by severity; what “remediate” means for third-party software. Do not commit to: fixed remediation periods for
products you do not make yourself.
(g) Subcontracting and pass-through of requirements. Check: whether your cloud services count as
subcontractors; whether their standard terms cover the pass-through; whether a consent requirement
would paralyse your business. Do not commit to: that your large providers accept clauses you cannot put to
them; liability for their errors — with a professional.
(h) Obligations on termination. Check: format and period of return; where data sits in backups and with
service providers; statutory retention periods. Do not commit to: immediate complete deletion although
backups only expire after weeks.
7.4 Parts B and C — DORA
The nine minimum contents of paragraph 2 range from the service description over processing locations,
data protection, access to data in insolvency, service levels, assistance in incidents and cooperation with
authorities to termination rights and training participation. The file deals with each individually. Three
deserve a sentence here:
Access to data in insolvency (point (d)). The customer must be able to get its data even if your business no
longer exists. What you can commit to is a question for a professional; escrow arrangements you cannot set
up, you do not commit to.
Assistance in incidents (point (f)) “at no additional cost or at a cost that is determined ex-ante”. Check: how
much effort you can absorb in the price. Do not commit to: unlimited free assistance.
Termination rights (point (h)). The customer must be able to terminate if you breach obligations or the
supervisor requires it. Check: whether the periods are bearable — with a professional.
The six additional contents of paragraph 3 apply only if the customer classifies your service as critical or
important: measurable performance targets, extended reporting obligations, tested contingency plans,
participation in penetration testing, unrestricted audit rights and exit strategies with a transition period. The
audit rights are the point at which small service providers most easily overreach: “unrestricted rights of

<!-- Seite 23 -->
access, inspection and audit by the financial entity, or an appointed third party, and by the competent
authority”, including copies on site. DORA knows a relief — but it depends on the size of the financial entity,
not yours: only if the customer is a microenterprise can the audit rights be transferred to a designated third
party.
Source: Art. 30(3)(e) and last subparagraph Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (accessed 14 September 2026).
7.5 Four sentences for the conversation
The file ends with four sentences you can say to the customer when the contract is on the table:
1. “We can commit to [clause] as it stands; to [clause] only in this form: [your version].”
2. “Our size is [number] employees; which requirements do you consider appropriate for a supplier of this
size?”
3. “Do you classify our service as supporting a critical or important function? This determines which
evidence we prepare.”
4. “On audit rights, liability and termination we will come back to you after review with a professional by
[date].”
The second sentence is the most important. Both sets of rules demand proportionality; neither says what it
looks like for a business with eight employees. That is a negotiation question, not a legal question — and the
negotiation begins with you asking it.
NOT LIKE THIS
Signing the security agreement because the framework agreement will otherwise not be
renewed, and meeting the clauses “somehow” later. The two-hour reporting period, the
unlimited audit right and the liability for subcontractors are then in the contract — and on
the table at the first incident. The four sentences above cost one conversation; the
clauses can cost the business.

SECTION 8
When the customer asks follow-up questions
In brief: The five follow-ups that come after sending, and how to meet them
— without promising afterwards something you did not promise in the
questionnaire.
The questionnaire is gone, the attachments too. Two weeks later an e-mail arrives: “Thank you. We have
questions on three points.” That is not a bad sign. A customer who asks has read — and intends to keep
working with you.
8.1 “Please send us the document”
The most frequent follow-up. The customer wants to see what you described at level 1: the policy, the backup
log, the account list. If the document exists, send it. If it does not exist, you chose the wrong level in the
questionnaire — and now is the moment to say so, not the moment to write a document that looks
backdated:
The policy is not yet finished in the form in which we want to present it to you. I stated “in
place” in the questionnaire because the rules apply in practice; they are not yet
summarised in writing. We will deliver it by [date]; until then attachment 2 describes the
responsibilities.
That costs standing, but less than a document that on closer inspection dates from the day before. And it
does not happen if the level is right from the start.
8.2 “Not enough for us — we need …”
The customer considers your answer at level 3 too little: it wants the second login step now, not in three
months; it wants a certificate, not a self-disclosure. Three possible answers, depending on what is true:
You can do it faster. Then commit to the new date — and keep it. A second missed date weighs more than a
first.
You cannot do it faster. Then say why, and what you do instead until then: “Converting all accounts requires
the cooperation of our provider, which needs [time] lead time. Until then we restrict access to your data to
two people with personal accounts.”
You cannot do it at all. A business with eight employees will not acquire an ISO certificate to keep an order.
Say so, and ask which evidence would satisfy the customer instead. The Chamber of Commerce itself says
that whether a certificate suffices as evidence is to be examined in each individual case according to the risk
assessment — so there is an individual case, and it can be discussed.
Source: WKO, “Die Sicherheit der Lieferkette im NISG 2026”, via web search (research/lieferkette/01, section 1.4) (accessed 14 September
2026).
What you do in none of the three situations: say “yes” afterwards. The questionnaire is with the customer; an
answer that contradicts it devalues both.

<!-- Seite 26 -->
8.3 “Please sign the enclosed agreement”
The follow-up that has no question mark. With the thanks for the answers comes a security agreement or a
contract annex. Here section 7 applies: clause by clause through the checklist, and on audit rights, liability,
termination and insolvency a professional. The four sentences from the checklist are the answer; the fourth
— “we will come back to you after review by [date]” — is the one you write first. Two weeks of review time are
usual for a contract annex; whoever does not take them signs what they have not read.
8.4 “We will rate you as part of our supplier assessment”
Some customers share the result: a level, a colour, a score. The result is an assessment by the customer
under its own scheme; it is not an official finding and says nothing about whether your business satisfies a
rule. Ask what the rating means for the order and what would improve it. The answer is usually a list — and the
list is your dates from level 3.
According to a survey by the European agency for cybersecurity, 43 per cent of surveyed entities use rating
services and 37 per cent due diligence to assess their suppliers. The schemes differ; the procedure is always
the same: the customer collects what it knows about you and turns it into a number. What it knows, you
determine with the self-disclosure.
Source: ENISA, Good Practices for Supply Chain Cybersecurity, 13 June 2023, via web search (research/lieferkette/01, Q30) (accessed 14
September 2026).
8.5 No follow-up
This also happens: the questionnaire goes off, and nothing happens. That does not mean nobody read it. It
means the answer fitted the scheme, or that the customer’s deadline for its own self-declaration is not yet
pressing. The dates from level 3 apply nonetheless: you committed to them in the letter, and the next
questionnaire will come. Enter them in the calendar, keep them, and report completion without being asked —
one sentence by e-mail to the contact point. That is the cheapest trust-building measure there is.
8.6 What you have after the first questionnaire
A business that has answered the first questionnaire with this kit afterwards has: a responsibilities sheet, an
emergency card, a supplier list, an account list, a backup plan with test log, a self-disclosure with date and
signature, and a list of dates on which “not yet” becomes “yes”. For the second questionnaire it fills in the
header of the self-disclosure afresh, checks the date, answers the three questions this customer asks
differently, and writes the letter. That takes an afternoon.
That is the purpose of the kit: not to survive a questionnaire, but to stop fearing the next one.
THE ONE SENTENCE
If you keep only one sentence from this guide: an honest answer with a date is better than
a yes that falls apart at the first follow-up question. Everything else follows from it.

SECTION 10
Glossary
In brief: The terms that appear in questionnaires and contract annexes,
against everyday language. Terms from the glossary of volume 1 are
adopted and not redefined.
Questionnaires speak the language of the standards and legal acts they are assembled from. This
glossary translates them. For provider, deployer, data processing agreement, impact assessment, AI
system and the other terms explained there, the glossary of volume 1 “AI in the Company”, section 18,
applies.
Audit right
The customer’s right to audit your business itself or through an appointee: inspect documents, enter
premises, ask questions. In contracts often next to the right to receive audit reports instead (Implementing
Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, Annex point 5.1.4(e)).
Background check
The vetting of persons before access to sensitive systems: certificates, references, possibly a criminal
record extract. The implementing regulation names it as a contract content (Annex point 5.1.4(c)); what of it is
permissible in your business is for a professional to clarify.
Contact point
The person or function the customer can reach at your business for security questions and incidents. The
implementing regulation requires the customer to keep a register with “contact points for each direct
supplier” (Annex point 5.2(a)).
Concentration risk
The customer’s risk of depending on a supplier “that is not easily substitutable” (Art. 29(1) Regulation (EU)
2022/2554). The reason for questions about your substitutability and about transition periods.
Critical or important function
Under DORA a function of the financial entity whose disruption would materially impair its financial
performance, the continuity of its business or its compliance with authorisation conditions (Art. 3(22)
Regulation (EU) 2022/2554). Whether your service supports such a function is decided by the financial
entity; the scope of contract contents depends on it.
Cyber hygiene
Everyday measures every business can take: updates, passwords, backups, caution with e-mails. The
directive names “basic cyber hygiene practices” among the minimum measures (Art. 21(2)(g) Directive (EU)
2022/2555).
Early warning
The first report by the covered entity to the authority, “without undue delay and in any event within 24 hours
of becoming aware” (Art. 23(4)(a) Directive (EU) 2022/2555; section 34(2)(1) NISG 2026). The reason why
your reporting period to the customer should be shorter than 24 hours.
Essential and important entities
The two classes of entities covered by the directive and the NISG 2026. Whether your business belongs to
them is told by the free NIS2 self-check; usually not, and the questionnaire comes anyway.
Exit strategy

<!-- Seite 31 -->
The customer’s plan for how it carries on without you: transition period, return of data, switch to another
provider. For critical or important functions a mandatory content of a DORA contract (Art. 30(3)(f) Regulation
(EU) 2022/2554).
ICT service
Under DORA “digital and data services provided through ICT systems to one or more internal or external users
on an ongoing basis”, including hardware as a service and technical support via updates (Art. 3(21)
Regulation (EU) 2022/2554). Very broad: software, hosting, maintenance, operations.
ICT third-party service provider
“An undertaking providing ICT services” (Art. 3(19) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554). Whoever supplies an ICT
service to a financial entity is one — regardless of size.
Implementing regulation
A legal act of the Commission that specifies a directive or regulation for certain areas. Implementing
Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 says, for certain digital providers, what “supply chain security” means in detail —
and is therefore the model on which contract clauses are based.
Multi-factor authentication, second login step
Login with a second element besides the password: a code on the phone, a key, a fingerprint. The directive
names it among the minimum measures (Art. 21(2)(j) Directive (EU) 2022/2555). This kit says “second login
step”.
Not applicable
The answer to a question that does not fit your service. Valid only with a reason.
Penetration testing, threat-led
A controlled attack on the financial entity’s systems to find vulnerabilities. Suppliers of critical functions must
take part (Art. 30(3)(d) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554). In the jargon TLPT.
Product with digital elements
Under the Cyber Resilience Act, software or hardware with a data connection, including separately
distributed components (Art. 3(1) Regulation (EU) 2024/2847). Whoever manufactures such a thing or
markets it under its own name is a manufacturer; whoever only buys and uses it is not.
Proportionality
The principle that measures must fit size, risk and cost. The directive expressly names “the size of the entity”
(Art. 21(1), second subparagraph, Directive (EU) 2022/2555). Your most important argument in the
conversation with the customer.
Recovery time
The time you need after an outage until you are operational again. Not estimated, but measured in the
restore test.
Register of information
The register every financial entity must keep on all contracts with ICT third-party service providers (Art. 28(3)
Regulation (EU) 2022/2554). The reason for the master data questions: legal entity, registered office,
service, subcontractors.
Restore test
The attempt to actually restore data from the backup, with a log: date, what, duration, result. The evidence
that a backup is more than a copy.
Risk management measures

<!-- Seite 32 -->
The technical, operational and organisational measures a covered entity must take under Art. 21 of the
directive or section 32 NISG 2026. The topics of the questionnaires are the ten points of that article.
Self-declaration
The report by the covered entity to the cybersecurity authority on its implemented measures, “in structured
form” (section 33(1) NISG 2026, according to search excerpt). An obligation of the customer, not yours — but
the reason why it questions you beforehand.
Self-disclosure
The document from this kit: your description of your business, dated and signed, not a certification. Not to be
confused with the self-declaration.
Service level
What you deliver in what quality and speed, as a measurable commitment: availability, response time,
recovery time.
Significant incident
An incident that triggers the reporting obligation of the covered entity; criteria are severe operational
disruption, financial loss or damage to others. The classification is made by the entity; you as supplier report
what could affect its systems or data and leave the classification to it.
Software bill of materials
A list of the third-party components contained in a software product. Manufacturer customers ask for it as
part of their due diligence for sourced components (Art. 13(5) Regulation (EU) 2024/2847). In the jargon
SBOM.
Subcontractor
Anyone you use to provide your service and who touches the customer’s data in doing so — including your e-
mail provider and your file storage. Customers ask for name, country and purpose.
Supply chain
In the sense of the directive, the entity’s relationships with its “direct suppliers or service providers” (Art.
21(2)(d) Directive (EU) 2022/2555). You are part of your customer’s supply chain; your own suppliers are part
of yours.
Transition period
The time after termination during which you continue to deliver so that the customer can switch. DORA
requires for critical functions a “mandatory adequate transition period” (Art. 30(3)(f) Regulation (EU)
2022/2554).
