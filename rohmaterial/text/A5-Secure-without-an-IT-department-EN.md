PRELIMINARY NOTE
Purpose and structure
This handbook is written for the people who run small and medium-sized
businesses that have no IT department and no security officer — the
workshop with six employees, the law firm with two partners and an
assistant, the medical practice, the agency, the retailer, the one-person
business. The computers run, e-mail works, an IT contractor comes when
something breaks. And at some point somebody asks a question nobody in
the house can answer: the insurer in a questionnaire, the major customer in
a supplier survey, the tax adviser after a computer failure, or — on the worst
day — the staff, when nothing opens in the morning.
It answers four questions in this order: What has to be in place in a business before AI, cloud services or a
major customer can even be a topic? Who asks about it, and why does everyone ask the same things? What
of it is an obligation, and what is a recommendation? And how do you do it — with the means a business
without an IT department has?
The order is deliberate. This handbook is the only one in the series that mostly describes practical work
rather than the legal position. It therefore says "do it like this" in many places — and under each of those
places it says what the recommendation rests on: a recommendation of the German Federal Office for
Information Security, of the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, of the Austrian computer emergency
response team, of the Chamber of Commerce, of the Federal Criminal Police Office, or on what audit
checklists and insurers ask. A recommendation is not a command. Where a legal act requires something, that
is stated separately, with a reference, and there are few such places.
Structure
The handbook is divided into four parts. Part I explains what this is about: why basic protection comes before
any use of AI, what the audit checklists and insurers ask and why they resemble one another, and what of it is
an obligation. Part II sets out the four things to get right — access, backups, devices and updates, e-mail —
and with them the question of who in the business is responsible. Part III describes the emergency: the first
hour and the first day, the reporting channels and points of contact in Austria and in the Union, and what a
business may demand of its IT contractor. Part IV contains a self-assessment, the collected uncertainties,
the glossary and the list of sources.
Every section in Parts II and III ends with a box headed "How you can tell that it has been done". That is the
sentence you can write into a questionnaire when it is true.
Scope
The practical work is the same everywhere; the points of contact are not. Wherever this handbook names an
authority, a telephone number or a deadline, Austria is the example and is marked as such. For businesses in
other member states of the European Union: the General Data Protection Regulation applies there just the
same, the national computer emergency response team can be found through the Union agency’s map
(Section 11.6), and the recommendations of the German Federal Office are drawn on as an example of official
recommendations, not as a German rule. Switzerland is not covered by this handbook.

Where a statement could not be substantiated, this is expressly noted — as not ascertainable, with the

A distinction is drawn throughout between what a law requires, what an authority recommends, and what an
audit checklist or an insurer asks. A questionnaire is a matter of contract. It is not a law.
DELIMITATION
This handbook is a guide. It is not a legally binding document, it does not replace legal
advice and it does not replace insurance advice. It recommends no product and names no
tool; it says which properties a tool should have so that you can decide for yourself. It
introduces no standard and replaces none. It does not examine whether your business
falls under a law. And it contains no figures on incidents, losses or fines, because in this
field such figures are frequently passed on without their population — this handbook
does not pass them on.

SECTION 1
Why basic protection comes before AI
Anyone who looks into the use of artificial intelligence in a business quickly
runs into an order that is not negotiable: first the foundation of the
information technology, then the risks that new tools bring with them.
Volume 1 of this series said so in one sentence and set it out in five points.
This handbook is the execution of those five points at the depth a business
without an IT department needs in order to implement them itself — or to
check whether its contractor has implemented them.
1.1 The five points from Volume 1
Volume 1 states in Section 12, "Information security", that the order is "professionally predetermined: first the
basic foundation of information technology, then the AI-specific risks. Not as a substitute." Before any AI-
specific measure, five points are to be put in order, which are "unspectacular" and cover "the greater part of
the incidents that actually occur":
Data backup, kept separate from the network, with a recovery successfully rehearsed at
least once. — Current software on all devices — operating systems, browsers, servers,
including rarely used devices. — Access: personal accounts, two-factor login, immediate
blocking on departure. — Responsibility: one named responsible person, even where
external support is brought in. — Emergency arrangement: defined availability for the event
of an outage.
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, "AI in the Company", Section 12.2 "Basic measures" (final version, read 15 September 2026); the five points are
set there as a list, here separated by dashes; rendered in substance from the German final version.
These five points are the structure of Parts II and III of this handbook: access (Sections 4 and 5), backups (6),
current software and devices (7), responsibility (9), emergency (10 and 11). To these is added a point that
Volume 1 lists among the AI-specific risks but which affects every business whether it uses AI or not: e-mail
and fraud (8).
1.2 What "foundation" means in practice
A business that has not put these five points in order can answer every other question only with
assumptions. Three examples, all invented:
A tax advisory firm with four employees has for years run a mailbox whose password also opens the
accounting service. The password becomes known at an unrelated service where an employee had used it as
well. Nobody in the house notices that somebody is logging in; the login looks like her own. Whether client
data has leaked in the process the firm cannot say afterwards — and that is exactly the question the data
protection authority asks (Section 11.2).
A plumbing business has for years backed up every night to a hard disk hanging off the server. One morning all
files are encrypted, the backup too, because it was connected at the moment of encryption. The backup
existed, but it was not separate (Section 6.3).

<!-- Seite 8 -->
An agency with nine employees has an emergency arrangement: "In case of problems, call the IT contractor."
On the Saturday it happens, the IT contractor’s phone is off, and nobody knows the second number, the
customer portal of the cloud service or the router’s credentials. The business stands still until Monday
(Section 10).
None of these examples needs artificial intelligence to happen. None is prevented by an AI policy. All three are
prevented or caught by the five points. That is the reason for the order.
1.3 Why businesses without an IT department in particular are meant
In June 2021 the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity published a report on the situation of small and
medium-sized enterprises and, alongside it, a guide with twelve steps. According to the report, the
enterprises interviewed named as the most common incidents attacks with ransomware, stolen laptops,
phishing and so-called CEO fraud — four things against which no expensive technology is needed, but the
four things this handbook is about.
Quelle: ENISA, "Cybersecurity for SMEs – Challenges and Recommendations", June 2021, and "Cybersecurity guide for SMEs – 12 steps to
securing your business", 28 June 2021, enisa.europa.eu/publications/cybersecurity-guide-for-smes (search excerpt, accessed 15 September
2026; the twelve steps not read in their wording, Section 14 no. 4).
For the same reason the German Federal Office for Information Security, together with an association of
medium-sized businesses, developed an audit procedure that is expressly aimed at "small and micro-
enterprises with up to about 50 employees without an IT department of their own" — the CyberRisikoCheck
under DIN SPEC 27076, which Volume 1 already names and which Section 2 of this handbook describes. That
such procedures exist is the authorities' admission that the big standards were not made for these
businesses.
Quelle: BSI, "CyberRisikoCheck nach DIN SPEC 27076", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Unternehmen-und-Organisationen/Informationen-und-
Empfehlungen/KMU/CyberRisikoCheck/ (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.2.
1.4 What distinguishes this handbook from a standard
There are standards for information security, and for some businesses they are the right way: the
international standard ISO/IEC 27001 for a management system, in Germany the Federal Office’s IT-
Grundschutz and the guideline VdS 10000 for small and medium-sized enterprises, in Austria the cyber risk
rating scheme. This handbook introduces none of them. It names them where they occur as the origin of a
recommendation or as evidence towards a customer, and then says what they are. Whoever needs a
certificate because a major customer demands one will find in Volume 3 of this series what the customer
means by it and whether a supplier statement suffices.
Quelle: Volume 3 of this series, "What your major customer will ask of you", Section 7 "The evidence" and Section 10 "The minimum that every
answer rests on".
What this handbook does instead: it describes four things to get right in such a way that a business can
implement them without a standard, without a consultant and without a new product, and for each of them it
says what an auditor, an insurer or a customer will later want to see. That is less than a standard. For most of
the businesses that read this handbook, though, it is the part that is missing.
THE SENTENCE THAT CARRIES EVERYTHING
A business without an IT department does not need a security strategy. It needs four
things that are right, one person who knows that they are right, and one sheet of paper
that says whom to call when they no longer are.

SECTION 2
What audit checklists ask — and why they resemble one another
A small business receives security questions from four directions: from an
audit procedure it commissions itself; from a label it wants to obtain; from
an insurer before it gets a cyber policy; and from a major customer that has
to vet its suppliers. The checklists look different. Their questions do not.
This section presents the most important ones and shows what they have
in common — and what none of them is: a law.
2.1 The CyberRisikoCheck under DIN SPEC 27076
In Germany the Federal Office for Information Security, together with the Federal Association of Medium-
Sized Businesses, developed a procedure it calls "standardised IT security consulting for small and micro-
enterprises". According to the Federal Office, a qualified IT service provider interviews the enterprise in one to
two hours on 27 requirements from six subject areas and then hands over a report with concrete
recommendations for action ordered by urgency; the target group is enterprises with up to about 50
employees without an IT department of their own. The result consists of a score, a chart across the six areas,
and a recommendation for each unmet requirement. It is expressly not a certification.
Quelle: BSI, "CyberRisikoCheck nach DIN SPEC 27076", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Unternehmen-und-Organisationen/Informationen-und-
Empfehlungen/KMU/CyberRisikoCheck/ (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.2.
The six subject areas are, according to concurring descriptions by service providers that offer the procedure:
organisation and awareness; identity and access management; data backup; patch and change
management; protection against malware; IT systems and networks. Questions asked under them include,
for example, whether every person has an individual user account, whether strong passwords or a second
factor are used, whether rights are granted on the principle of least privilege and withdrawn on departure,
whether backups can be restored and are kept off site, and whether security updates are installed promptly.
Quelle: Descriptions by several providers of the procedure (secondary sources, accessed 15 September 2026); the wording of the standard
was not read, and the names of the areas do not come from the Federal Office’s page (Section 14 no. 2).
2.2 The Austrian label and the cyber risk rating scheme
In Austria the Kuratorium Sicheres Österreich, in cooperation with a credit protection organisation,
developed a cyber risk rating scheme on which the Cyber Trust Austria Label is based. According to the
publishers there are tiers: an entry tier that rests on a self-declaration against a question catalogue, whose
statements are checked for completeness and plausibility, and a higher tier with an external audit. The
scheme is published annually as a document; the Chamber of Commerce refers to it as freely available. For
the entry tier, Austrian advisory sites concurringly name fourteen criteria, among them in substance: a valid
information security policy, a named security responsibility, regular training, secure passwords, access on
the need-to-know principle, secure configuration of systems, protection against malware, regular updates,
emergency planning with a backup concept.
Quelle: Cyber Trust Austria, cyber-trust.at, and KSV1870, ksv.at (search excerpts, accessed 15 September 2026); scheme documents "Cyber
Risk Rating & Cyber Trust Label Schema Policy" 2024, 2025 and 2026 (only the table of contents readable) · advisory sites on the entry tier
(secondary sources) · WKO, "Die Sicherheit der Lieferkette im NISG 2026" (research/lieferkette). Names of the tiers and wording of the
criteria not read (Section 14 no. 3).
For this handbook it is not decisive what the tiers are called. What is decisive is that an Austrian business
that wants a label has to answer the same topics as a German business in the CyberRisikoCheck — and that
the entry tier, according to the publishers, rests on self-declaration. What you declare yourself has to be
true. That is why every section in Part II ends with the box "How you can tell that it has been done".

WHAT THIS HANDBOOK DOES NOT SAY ABOUT INSURANCE
Whether your business needs cyber insurance, which one, and whether an existing policy
covers a particular loss, this handbook does not say. That is insurance advice, and it
belongs with your broker or insurer. What this handbook does say: read the questionnaire
before you fill it in, and the policy before you need it. Answer every question as it is —
"partly, by the end of the quarter" is an answer, "yes" without a basis is not. And write the
notification deadline and the emergency number from your policy onto the emergency
card (Section 10.5). Volume 1 recommends in Section 12.5 a written enquiry to your own
insurer, the answer to which is kept.
2.4 What major customers ask

SECTION 3
What is an obligation and what is a recommendation
This handbook often says "do this". So that the reader knows what of it he
must do and what he should do, this section separates the few obligations
from the many recommendations. The obligations have a reference in a

<!-- Seite 12 -->
legal act; the recommendations have a body that issues them and a date.
Both stand in the handbook beneath the respective statement.
3.1 The one security obligation: Article 32 of the General Data Protection Regulation
Almost every business processes personal data — of customers, employees, suppliers. For all of these data
the General Data Protection Regulation requires "a level of security appropriate to the risk":
Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation and the nature,
scope, context and purposes of processing as well as the risk of varying likelihood and
severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor
shall implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure a level of
security appropriate to the risk.
Quelle: Art. 32(1) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, OJ L 119 of 4.5.2016, read in a text copy of the Official Journal version in German and rendered in
substance (accessed 15 September 2026); official: eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj (Section 14 no. 1 and no. 14).
The Regulation does not say which measures those are. It names four examples, and three of them are the
things this handbook is about: the pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data (point (a)), the ability
to restore the availability and access to personal data in a timely manner in the event of a physical or
technical incident (point (c)), and a process for regularly testing, assessing and evaluating the effectiveness
of the measures (point (d)). Whoever encrypts his laptops (Section 7.4), can restore his backups (Section 6)
and once a year checks whether that is still so (Section 13) is therefore not only doing what authorities
recommend — he is doing what this provision names as an example.
Quelle: Art. 32(1)(a), (c) and (d) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, reference as above.
How much is "appropriate" depends on the risk and is to be judged case by case. This handbook does not
judge it; it says that the obligation exists and that the four things it describes cover the provision’s examples.
Whoever processes special categories of data — health data in a medical practice, client data in a law firm —
needs more than the examples, and for that data protection advice is the right way. Volume 1 deals with data
protection in the use of AI tools in Section 11.
3.2 The duty to notify after an incident
If security is breached and this leads to the destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or
access to, personal data, the Regulation speaks of a "personal data breach". An attack with ransomware that
encrypts customer data falls under this wording even if nobody has read the data — "loss" suffices.
Quelle: Art. 4(12) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, reference as above (rendered in substance).
The controller notifies such a breach to the supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible,
not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to the
rights and freedoms of natural persons; where the notification is not made within 72 hours, it is accompanied
by reasons for the delay. Where there is likely to be a high risk, the persons concerned are to be informed as
well — unless the data were rendered unintelligible to unauthorised persons, such as by encryption.
Quelle: Art. 33(1) and Art. 34(1) and (3)(a) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, reference as above (rendered in substance) · Volume 1 of this series,
Section 12.4 (the period begins with awareness, not with the event).
How and where this notification is made in Austria is in Section 11.2. For this handbook two things matter
here: the period is short, and it begins with awareness — which is why a business has to know in advance who
notifies (Section 9) and whom it calls for that (Section 10). And the encryption of mobile devices is not
merely a recommendation: it is the reason why a lost laptop need not entail informing every customer.

3.3 The contractor as processor
Whoever lets an IT contractor, a cloud service or a data centre work with personal data may do so only with
processors providing sufficient guarantees to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures,
and only on the basis of a contract that stipulates, among other things, that the processor takes all
measures required under Article 32. Volume 1 calls this contract, in its glossary, the data processing
agreement. What follows from it for selecting and checking a contractor is in Section 12.
Quelle: Art. 28(1) and (3)(c) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, reference as above (rendered in substance) · Volume 1 of this series, Section 18, entry
"Auftragsverarbeitungsvertrag" (data processing agreement).
3.4 Retention — Austria
The following applies to Austria. For other member states the periods were not surveyed (Section 14 no. 7).
A backup that keeps only the latest state fulfils no retention obligation. Under the Federal Fiscal Code, books,
records, the associated vouchers and the business papers relevant for tax assessment are to be retained for
seven years, counted from the end of the calendar year for which the last entry was made, and longer as long
as they are relevant for pending proceedings; electronic records have to remain readable for the whole
period.
Quelle: § 132 Bundesabgabenordnung (Federal Fiscal Code), after WKO, "Aufbewahrungspflichten für Unternehmen",
wko.at/steuern/aufbewahrungspflichten (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026); the statutory wording was not read at the Legal
Information System — verify current status (Section 14 no. 7).
For backups this means: older states have to be preserved, not just the latest, and a change of accounting
service must not make the old data unreadable (Section 6.5). Which periods apply to your business in detail
your tax adviser or the chamber will tell you; this handbook names only the basic rule.
3.5 What is not an obligation for the reader: NIS2
The NIS2 Directive applies to entities of the kinds listed in its annexes that qualify as medium-sized
enterprises or exceed their thresholds, and regardless of size only in individual, expressly named cases. In
Austria the Network and Information System Security Act 2026 transposes this; a medium-sized enterprise
there is one with at least 50 employees or with more than ten million euro in annual turnover and balance
sheet total.
Quelle: Art. 2(1) and (2) Directive (EU) 2022/2555, text copy of the Official Journal version (accessed 15 September 2026) · § 24 and § 25(3)
NISG 2026, Federal Law Gazette (BGBl.) I No. 94/2025, ris.bka.gv.at/eli/bgbl/I/2025/94 (read 10 September 2026, research/nis2).
The reader of this handbook is, as a rule, not covered. For him there is no reporting obligation under this act,

SECTION 4
Access I: passwords and the password manager
The mailbox is the key to almost everything else: whoever has it can request
a new password from most services. That is why this handbook begins with
access, and within access with the password. The rules have been the same
for years and are still not kept in most small businesses — not out of
carelessness, but because nobody has ever said them in one sentence.
Here they are.
4.1 One password per service
A password that applies in two places is, in both, only as secure as the weaker one. If it becomes known at an
unrelated service — and that happens without the business being able to do anything about it — attackers try
it at other services. The only rule that helps against this is simple: every service gets its own password. For e-
mail, for the bank, for the payment service, for the shop administration, for the cloud administration, for the
router.
Scutoria’s free login check (scutoria.com/werkzeuge/anmeldung) begins with exactly this question and
classifies the answer; it names no standard and no rule.
4.2 Long rather than complicated
The German Federal Office for Information Security names length and complexity as decisive for a good
password and describes three ways that lead to the same goal: a password is secure if, for example, it is 20 to
25 characters long and uses two character types — such as a sequence of words; a short and complex
password should be at least eight characters long and contain four different character types; and eight
characters with three character types suffice, according to the Federal Office, if the account is additionally
secured by a second factor. For Wi-Fi encryption it recommends a password of at least 20 characters.
Quelle: BSI, "Sichere Passwörter erstellen", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Verbraucherinnen-und-Verbraucher/Informationen-und-
Empfehlungen/Cyber-Sicherheitsempfehlungen/Accountschutz/Sichere-Passwoerter-erstellen/ (search excerpt, accessed 15 September
2026; Section 14 no. 2). Recommendation of the Federal Office, not a command.
For a business without an IT department the first way is the most practical: a sequence of four or five words
that nobody but the person can remember because they have nothing to do with one another. It is easier to
type than eight special characters and, according to the Federal Office, no weaker.
4.3 No change without cause
For a long time the rule was: change passwords every three months. According to the Federal Office, as
several specialist sites concurringly report, it no longer recommends a preventive, regular change in its IT-
Grundschutz catalogue; a change should be made on a concrete occasion, such as suspicion that the
password has become known. The reason: whoever has to change every three months changes by patterns,
and patterns can be guessed.
Quelle: Reproduction of the BSI’s recommendation (IT-Grundschutz-Kompendium, module on identity and access management) by several
specialist sites (secondary sources, accessed 15 September 2026); the wording and edition of the module were not read at the Federal
Office’s text (Section 14 no. 6). Verify current status.
For the business this means: the calendar entry "change passwords" can go. What remains are the occasions
— a suspicion, a leak at a service, the departure of a person who knew a shared password (Section 5.4).

4.4 The password manager
For whoever has many accounts the Federal Office recommends a password manager: a program that stores
passwords in encrypted form, enters them when logging in and generates strong passwords. Nobody then
has to remember anything except the one password for the manager itself — and that is where the word
sequence from Section 4.2 belongs.
Quelle: BSI, "Passwörter verwalten mit dem Passwort-Manager", bsi.bund.de/…/Accountschutz/Sichere-Passwoerter-erstellen/Passwort-
Manager/ (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026). Recommendation of the Federal Office.
This handbook names no password manager. What a business should check when choosing one are
properties — the same approach that Volume 1 describes for tools in Section 2.4:
Can it be used by several people, with separate vaults per person and a shared vault for accounts the
business has to share (Section 5.4)?
Can the business withdraw a person’s access without her taking the passwords with her — and change
the shared passwords afterwards in one pass?
Where do the data lie, encrypted with a key that only the business knows? Whoever asks the provider
gets an answer; whoever does not ask does not know.
Is there a way in if the person who set up the manager is not reachable — an emergency access or a
printed recovery key in the business’s safe?
Does it have a business account with a contract, or is it a private account of one person?
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 2.4 "Contractual checkpoints" (approach: criteria instead of names; approved-tools list). The
questions are this handbook’s questions.
INVENTED EXAMPLE: THE LIST IN THE DESK DRAWER
A medical practice keeps its credentials in a table, printed out in the drawer at reception:
billing service, laboratory portal, e-mail, bank access, router. When the assistant leaves,
the list stays, and nobody changes the passwords because there are "so many". A year
later the bank notices a login from an unknown device. With a password manager with a
shared vault, the doctor would have withdrawn the assistant’s access on her last working
day and renewed the shared passwords in half an hour. The drawer would have stayed
empty.
4.5 What does not belong in a password — and what not on a sticky note
No names of children, pets, clubs; no business name; no date of birth; no keyboard sequences. And no note
on the screen, no notebook next to the till, no table on the shared drive. Where a business needs passwords
outside the manager — the recovery key of the manager itself, the backup codes of the second factor
(Section 5.2) — they belong on paper in a locked place that two people know.
4.6 Logging in without a password
Newer methods replace the password with a key that resides on the device or on a hardware key and that the
service does not know; the Federal Office counts such device-bound methods among those that transmit no
confidential information to the service and are resistant to common phishing attacks. Where a service offers
this, it is a good choice for a small business — with the same question as for the second factor: what happens
if the device is lost (Section 5.2)?
Quelle: BSI, "Wie sicher und einfach sind Verfahren der Zwei-Faktor-Authentisierung?", bsi.bund.de/…/Accountschutz/Zwei-Faktor-
Authentisierung/Bewertung-2FA-Verfahren/ (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026). Recommendation of the Federal Office.

HOW YOU CAN TELL THAT IT HAS BEEN DONE
Every person has her own access to the business’s password manager, every account in it
has its own password generated by the manager, and the manager’s recovery key lies
printed in a locked place that two people know. There is no list in a drawer. Whoever writes
that into a questionnaire writes the truth — and can show it.
SECTION 5
Access II: second factor, administrative rights, who leaves
A password can be copied, intercepted or guessed. A second factor ensures
that this alone is not enough: to the password is added something the
person has with her — a code from an app, a hardware key, in the worst case
a text message. This section says where the second factor belongs first,
which methods the Federal Office considers fit for purpose, and what about
the two other questions every audit checklist asks: who has administrative
rights, and what happens when somebody leaves?
5.1 Where the second factor belongs first
The Federal Office recommends setting up and using two-factor authentication wherever it is possible. For a
business that cannot convert everything at once there is an order that follows from the damage: first the e-
mail account, because it opens access to all other accounts; then everything where money moves — bank,
payment service, shop administration; then everything where access is granted — the administration of the
cloud service, of the domain, of the router, of the password manager; then the remote accesses through
which somebody gets into the business’s network from outside. Insurers, according to descriptions of
practice, ask above all about these four groups.
Quelle: BSI, "Zwei-Faktor-Authentisierung – mehr Sicherheit für Geräte und Daten", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Verbraucherinnen-und-
Verbraucher/Informationen-und-Empfehlungen/Cyber-Sicherheitsempfehlungen/Accountschutz/Zwei-Faktor-Authentisierung/ (search
excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026); the order is a classification by this handbook · insurers' practice according to advisory sites (Section
2.3).
For accounts with administrative rights the Federal Office is clearer: in its list of ten measures against
ransomware it says that privileged accounts should always be protected with two-factor authentication.
Quelle: BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Unternehmen-und-Organisationen/Cyber-
Sicherheitslage/Analysen-und-Prognosen/Ransomware-Angriffe/Top-10-Ransomware-Massnahmen/ (search excerpt, accessed 15
September 2026; Section 14 no. 2). Recommendation.
5.2 Which second factor is fit for purpose — and what about the backup codes
Not every second factor is equally good. According to the Federal Office’s assessment, one-time codes from
a hardware generator or an app are better than codes that arrive by text message; it advises against
receiving text-message codes on the same device used for logging in, because the factors are then not
separated. Hardware-based methods — a small key plugged into the computer or held to the phone — it
considers resistant to common phishing attacks because they transmit no confidential information to the
service.
Quelle: BSI, "Wie sicher und einfach sind Verfahren der Zwei-Faktor-Authentisierung?", bsi.bund.de/…/Zwei-Faktor-
Authentisierung/Bewertung-2FA-Verfahren/ (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026). Recommendation; the Federal Office's
assessment tables were not read.

<!-- Seite 19 -->
For a business without an IT department this means: an app on the work phone is the normal case, a
hardware key the better choice for the accounts in the first group, a text message better than nothing. And in
every case, setting it up includes the question the login check asks last: where are the backup codes? Every
service issues recovery codes when the second factor is set up. If they lie on the phone that gets lost, the
account is lost. They belong printed in the locked place from Section 4.5 or in the password manager — and
rehearsed once: can you get back in without the phone?
5.3 Administrative rights: only for those who need them, and never for e-mail
On every computer and in every service there are accounts that may do everything: install, delete, change
settings, create other accounts. In small businesses everybody frequently works with such an account
because that is how it was when it was set up. The Federal Office recommends the opposite: privileged
accounts should be used only for administrative activities, not for reading e-mail or browsing the internet;
whoever administers needs a separate account for that and a normal one for daily work — and this should be
enforced technically, not merely agreed.
Quelle: BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen", reference as above (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026). Recommendation.
The reason is simple: malware that comes in through an e-mail has the rights of the account in which the e-
mail was opened. With administrative rights it can do everything; with normal rights often nothing lasting.
The conversion costs a business with ten computers an afternoon — and the question to the contractor
whether he will do it for the business (Section 12.3).
The same rule applies to services: the access with which the cloud service or the shop is administered is not
the access with which one works in it every day. Audit checklists call this the principle of least privilege: every
person gets what she needs for her work, and no more.
Quelle: Descriptions of the CyberRisikoCheck under DIN SPEC 27076 (secondary sources, Section 2.1; accessed 15 September 2026).
5.4 Shared accounts
Some accounts cannot be assigned to one person: the info mailbox, the access to the shop system, the
account with the parcel service. For them: if shared at all, then through the shared vault of the password
manager, not through a password everybody has in their head — because only then can it be changed when a
person leaves without asking everyone else. And wherever a service allows several users per account, that is
always the better way, because it remains visible who did what.
5.5 Who leaves: the last working day
Volume 1 says it in half a sentence: "immediate blocking on departure". Audit checklists ask for a procedure for
it. In a business without an IT department the procedure is a list — for each person all services she has access
to, kept from the first day and added to whenever a service is added. On the last working day the list is worked
through: own accounts blocked, access to the password manager withdrawn, shared passwords renewed,
the second factor deleted on the person’s devices, devices taken back, forwarding rules removed from the
mailbox, accesses at the contractor cancelled. That "nobody has left yet" is no reason not to keep the list; it
is the best moment to create it.
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.2 · descriptions of the CyberRisikoCheck (procedure for withdrawing access rights on departure;
secondary sources, accessed 15 September 2026) · Art. 21(2)(i) Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (human resources security and access control
policies — obligation of the covered entities, for the reader the reason for the customer’s question; text copy, accessed 15 September 2026,
rendered in substance).

INVENTED EXAMPLE: THE FORWARDING RULE
A retailer with seven employees parts with a salesman on good terms. His mailbox is
blocked. What nobody sees: months earlier he had — innocently, to work from home — set
up a rule forwarding all incoming messages to a private mailbox. The forwarding keeps
running, because the mailbox is blocked but not deleted and the rule stays stored in the
service. On the business’s list there has been one more line since: "check forwarding and
rules".
5.6 The same applies to AI tools — and Volume 2 says how
Accounts with AI services are accounts like any other: personal, with a second factor, with a default setting
that does not release the inputs for training, and with withdrawal on departure. Volume 2 of this series
describes this in Section 6 "Access, accounts and default settings"; this handbook does not repeat it.
Quelle: Volume 2 of this series, "AI Policies for the Workforce", Section 6.
HOW YOU CAN TELL THAT IT HAS BEEN DONE
On every e-mail account, every account with money and every account with
administrative rights the second factor is switched on, the backup codes lie printed in a
locked place, and getting in without the phone has been rehearsed once. Nobody reads e-
mail in an account with administrative rights. For every person there is a list of her
accesses, and the last working day is a procedure, not a coincidence.
SECTION 6
Backups: what, where, how often — and the one test almost nobody does
If this handbook had only one section, it would be this one. The backup is
the measure that lets the business work again after the worst day — and the
one that in small businesses is most often present and nevertheless
unusable: because it lies in the same place as the original, because it has
stood still for months, because nobody has ever tried to get anything back
out of it. Scutoria’s free backup check (scutoria.com/werkzeuge/sicherung)
asks six questions on this and provides a rehearsal plan to print; this
section is the full form of the answers.
6.1 What gets backed up: the question before the technology
Before a business thinks about hard disks and cloud services, it answers one question on paper: which data
does the business need tomorrow in order to work, and where do they lie? The accounts with the accounting
service, the customer file on the server, the quotations on the employees' computers, the photos on three
phones, the e-mails with the mailbox provider, the drawings in a cloud folder. What is on this list is the object
of the backup; what is not on it is either unimportant or forgotten. The Federal Office sums this up in its
ransomware catalogue as preparatory work: identifying the business-critical systems in advance.
Quelle: BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Unternehmen-und-Organisationen/Cyber-
Sicherheitslage/Analysen-und-Prognosen/Ransomware-Angriffe/Top-10-Ransomware-Massnahmen/ (search excerpt, accessed 15

<!-- Seite 21 -->
September 2026; Section 14 no. 2). Recommendation.
For many small businesses this list has a reassuring property: a large part of the data lies with services that
back up themselves. That is good — and it is no reason not to keep the list. Because "the service backs up"
does not answer two questions: how does the business get at the data if the service is cancelled, blocked or
gone? And how long does the service keep older states? Both belong on the list, per location (Section 12.4).
6.2 How often: automatically, and more often than you think
A backup somebody has to remember does not take place. It runs automatically, and the only question is how
much work the business is prepared to lose: with a daily backup it is at worst one day; with a weekly one, a
week. The Austrian portal onlinesicherheit.gv.at recommends making backups regularly; the Federal Office
calls the backup the most important preventive measure. How often "regularly" is for the individual business
is decided by the answer to the question of what a lost day costs.
Quelle: onlinesicherheit.gv.at, "Erpressungstrojaner – Vorbeugende Maßnahmen", onlinesicherheit.gv.at/Services/Technologie-
Schwerpunkte/Ransomware/ (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen", reference as
above. Recommendations.
6.3 Where: separate from the original
This is the point at which most backups fail, and here the Federal Office is as clear as nowhere else:
A backup is the most important preventive measure with which the availability of the data
can be guaranteed in the event of a ransomware incident. The data must be saved in an
offline backup. These backups are separated from the other systems of the IT network
after the backup process and are therefore protected against attacks and encryption.
Quelle: BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen", reference as above; rendered in substance from the German search excerpt of the page
(accessed 15 September 2026; Section 14 no. 2 and no. 14). The "must" is the Federal Office’s wording within its recommendation, not a law.
The Austrian portal says the same in everyday language: store backups on cloud storage or external media
that are connected to the computer only for the copying process, because many ransomware trojans also
encrypt connected drives and network drives.
Quelle: onlinesicherheit.gv.at, "Erpressungstrojaner – Vorbeugende Maßnahmen", reference as above (search excerpt, accessed 15
September 2026). Recommendation.
A copy that is permanently attached to the server is not a separate copy. A copy in the same room burns and
disappears with the original. A copy with a cloud service that is reachable with the same account and the
same password as the original can be deleted by the same attacker. Separate means: whoever deletes or
encrypts the original does not reach the copy — because it is unplugged, because it lies under a different
account, or because the service keeps older states that cannot be overwritten.
In practice a rule of thumb has spread for this which no authority in the excerpts read calls by that name, but
which reflects the Federal Office’s requirements: three copies of the data, on two different media, one of
them off site — and, as an addition against ransomware, one of them offline or immutable. It is usable as a
memory aid, not as a rule.
Quelle: Rule of thumb ("3-2-1"), reported on several specialist sites (secondary sources, accessed 15 September 2026); not named as a rule
in the excerpts read from BSI, ENISA and onlinesicherheit.gv.at.
6.4 Versions: keeping older states
A backup that overwrites the original every night has one flaw: if a file is damaged on Monday and backed up
on Tuesday, the good version is gone. Ransomware exploits exactly that — it encrypts, and the next backup
backs up the encrypted files. That is why a usable backup keeps older states: yesterday's, last week's, last

<!-- Seite 22 -->
month's. With cloud services this is called versioning, and the question to the service is: how long do older
versions remain, and can an attacker with the business’s account delete them?
Quelle: Scutoria’s backup check asks this as its fourth question (scutoria.com/werkzeuge/sicherung); the reasoning is the Federal Office's
on separation (Section 6.3).
6.5 How long: retention is not backup
Older states for a few months are backup. Books and vouchers for seven years are retention — in Austria an
obligation under the Federal Fiscal Code (Section 3.4), and the backup fulfils it only if the old data remain
readable for the whole period. Whoever changes accounting service needs the old years in a format readable
without the service. Whoever switches off a program that was the only software able to open a file has lost
the file, even if it is backed up.
Quelle: § 132 Federal Fiscal Code after WKO, "Aufbewahrungspflichten für Unternehmen" (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026;
verify current status, Section 14 no. 7). Not surveyed for other member states.
6.6 The one test
A backup from which nothing has ever been restored is an assumption. The Federal Office writes that
planning and preparing the restart and the restoration of the data always belong to a backup, and that these
plans should be put to a practical test in order to recognise complications and challenges in restoration
before an emergency. Volume 1 demands in its first point "a recovery successfully rehearsed at least once".
And the General Data Protection Regulation names as an example of a security measure the ability to restore
the availability and access to personal data in a timely manner in the event of a physical or technical incident
— an ability, not a copy.
Quelle: BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen", reference as above (search excerpt, rendered in substance) · Volume 1 of this series,
Section 12.2 · Art. 32(1)(c) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, text copy of the Official Journal version (accessed 15 September 2026).
The test is not a project. The rehearsal plan of the backup check has five steps that together take an hour:
restore a file from yesterday and time it; restore a folder from a month ago and check whether that state still
exists; walk through what is missing to replace an entire computer — programs, accesses, licences; check
whether the copy stays reachable if network or account are gone; and record the result: date, who
rehearsed, what was missing, next appointment in six months.
Quelle: Rehearsal plan of Scutoria’s backup check (scutoria.com/werkzeuge/sicherung), five steps; not an official document.
INVENTED EXAMPLE: THE BACKUP THAT RAN FOR FOUR YEARS
A planning office with five employees had a backup that the previous IT contractor had set
up. It ran every night for four years, and every night it wrote a message into a file that
nobody read. When a computer failed, it turned out: for two years the backup had no
longer found a folder that had been renamed — the one with the current projects. A single
rehearsal of restoring one file from that folder would have shown it on the first day.
6.7 Who knows how it is done
The last question of the backup check is the one on which businesses fail in an emergency: who can restore?
If it is one person, the business stands still when she is on holiday. If it is only the contractor, he has to be
reachable and has to know what should come back first. The Federal Office calls in its emergency planning for
regularly rehearsed processes for restoring the business-critical systems; for a small business that means:
two people who know the procedure, and one page on which it is written — in the password manager or
printed next to the emergency card (Section 10.5).
Quelle: BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen" (emergency planning), reference as above (search excerpt, accessed 15 September
2026). Recommendation.

HOW YOU CAN TELL THAT IT HAS BEEN DONE
There is a list of which data lie where and who backs them up. The backup runs
automatically and daily, one copy lies separate from the original — unplugged, under a
different account or with versions that are not overwritten — and older states are
preserved. Within the last six months a restore has been rehearsed, with date and result
on a sheet, and two people know how it is done. That is the sentence for every
questionnaire — and the example that Article 32 names.
SECTION 7
Devices and updates
The second point from Volume 1 reads "current software on all devices —
operating systems, browsers, servers, including rarely used devices". It is so
unspectacular that questionnaires often ask about it in one sentence —
"Are security updates installed promptly?" — and so rarely fully done in
businesses without an IT department because nobody knows which devices
there are at all. This section therefore begins with the list and ends with the
router.
7.1 The list of devices
A business can keep current only what it knows. The list is simple: every computer, every laptop, every work
phone, every tablet, the server or the network storage device, the router, the network printers, the till, the
telephone system, the camera, the alarm system with a network connection, the device in the workshop that
a manufacturer connected years ago with a network cable. Per device: who uses it, which operating system,
whether it gets updates by itself, and until when the manufacturer supports it. Audit checklists call this IT
documentation; one sheet is enough.
Quelle: Insurers' practice according to advisory sites (IT documentation as a typical question; Section 2.3, secondary sources, accessed 15
September 2026) · Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.2 ("including rarely used devices").
7.2 Updates: immediately, and by themselves
The Federal Office recommends installing all updates immediately or promptly after their publication — for
the operating system, the browser and all extensions — because patches are among the most effective
means against attacks from the internet. The Austrian portal says the same: keep operating system, browser
and software up to date, because the manufacturers' updates close known security gaps. And the insurers
name "promptly installed security updates" among the policyholder duties.
Quelle: BSI, "Softwareupdates schnellstmöglich installieren", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Verbraucherinnen-und-Verbraucher/Informationen-
und-Empfehlungen/Cyber-Sicherheitsempfehlungen/Updates-Browser-Open-Source-Software/Wichtige-Softwareupdates/ (search
excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · onlinesicherheit.gv.at, "Erpressungstrojaner – Vorbeugende Maßnahmen" (search excerpt,
accessed 15 September 2026) · GDV, media release on the model conditions (Section 2.3). Recommendations; the policyholder duty is
contract content, not law.
For a business without an IT department there is only one way to keep to this: switch on automatic updates
on every device and fix one date a month on which one person goes through the list from Section 7.1 and
checks on each device whether it has received them. The server, the router and the device in the workshop
usually do not get them by themselves; for them the monthly date is there — or the contractor with whom
this is agreed (Section 12.3).

7.3 Devices without manufacturer support
An operating system for which the manufacturer no longer supplies security updates gets no more gaps
closed; every newly discovered one stays open. The Federal Office writes in its IT-Grundschutz module on
patch and change management that if hardware or software products are to be used that are no longer
supported by the manufacturer or for which no support is available, it must be checked whether they can still
be operated securely. For a small business this check almost always comes to the same answer: not on the
network. An old device that controls a machine and cannot be replaced comes off the internet and into a
network of its own; an old office computer is replaced.
Quelle: BSI, IT-Grundschutz-Kompendium, module OPS.1.1.3 "Patch- und Änderungsmanagement", edition 2023,
bsi.bund.de/SharedDocs/Downloads/DE/BSI/Grundschutz/IT-GS-Kompendium_Einzel_PDFs_2023/ (search excerpt, accessed 15
September 2026; Section 14 no. 2). Recommendation.
This handbook names no manufacturers and no dates on which a particular system leaves support; they are
with the manufacturer, and they belong in the column "supported until" of the list.
7.4 Laptops and phones: encrypt
According to the Union agency’s report, stolen laptops were among the most common incidents that small
enterprises named. Against the loss of the device the backup helps; against the loss of the data on it only
one thing helps: the storage medium is encrypted. The Federal Office describes it like this: full-disk
encryption protects sensitive data especially in the event of loss or theft; information on laptops and mobile
storage media does not fall into the wrong hands if the device is stolen or lost and the data on it are
encrypted.
Quelle: ENISA, "Cybersecurity for SMEs – Challenges and Recommendations", June 2021 (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) ·
BSI, "Datenverschlüsselung" and "Soft- und hardwaregestützte Verschlüsselung", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Verbraucherinnen-und-
Verbraucher/…/Daten-sichern-verschluesseln-und-loeschen/Datenverschluesselung/ (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026).
Recommendations.
Here the recommendation meets the obligation: the General Data Protection Regulation names the
pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data as the first example of a security measure, and it makes
informing the persons concerned after an incident unnecessary if the data were rendered unintelligible to
unauthorised persons, such as by encryption. An encrypted laptop left on the train is a loss. An unencrypted
one is an incident with a notification (Section 11.2).
Quelle: Art. 32(1)(a) and Art. 34(3)(a) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, text copy of the Official Journal version (accessed 15 September 2026;
Section 14 no. 1), rendered in substance.
Current operating systems for laptops and phones bring the encryption with them; it has to be switched on
and secured with a login, and the recovery key belongs in the locked place from Section 4.5, not on the
device. The same rule applies to USB storage and external hard disks that leave the house — including the
backup that lies off site.
7.5 Protection programs
A program protecting against malware belongs on every computer, and most operating systems include one.
The Federal Office points out in its ransomware catalogue that new variants of ransomware are rarely
recognised at once by local signatures and that most infections with new variants are detected by the
additional protection modules and cloud services of the protection programs — these modules should be
used. For the business this means: leave it switched on, keep it current, do not click away its messages. What
insurers, according to descriptions of practice, expect beyond that — programs that recognise and report
suspicious behaviour on the devices — is a question for the contractor (Section 12.3), not for this handbook.
Quelle: BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen", reference as in Section 6 (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · insurers'
practice according to advisory sites (Section 2.3). Recommendations.

7.6 Router and Wi-Fi
The router is the device that runs longest and is looked at least. Three things: the password for its
administration is not the one it was delivered with, and it lies in the password manager; it gets updates, or it
is replaced; and remote administration from the internet is switched off if nobody needs it. For the Wi-Fi the
Federal Office recommends a password of at least 20 characters; and a guest network, separate from the
business’s network, ensures that customers, visitors and the devices from Section 7.3 are not in the same
network as the server. The Federal Office names the separation of networks in its ransomware catalogue as a
measure that limits the damage, because ransomware reaches only what is in its immediate vicinity; for a
small business the guest network is the simplest case of it.
Quelle: BSI, "Sichere Passwörter erstellen" (Wi-Fi, search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-
Maßnahmen" (network segmentation, search excerpt). Recommendations.
INVENTED EXAMPLE: THE DEVICE IN THE WAREHOUSE
A wholesaler with fourteen employees has a computer in the warehouse that prints the
labels. It has run for nine years on an operating system that the manufacturer has not
supported for years, and it hangs in the same network as the server because that is how it
always was. When malware comes in through an e-mail, the label computer is the device
through which it spreads. On the device list that the business draws up afterwards, it is in
the first line — with the note "own network, no internet".
HOW YOU CAN TELL THAT IT HAS BEEN DONE
There is a list of all devices with operating system and end of support. Automatic updates
are switched on everywhere, and once a month one person checks that they have arrived.
No device without manufacturer support is on the internet. Every laptop and every work
phone is encrypted, with the recovery key in a locked place. The router has its own
password, gets updates, and guests have their own network.
SECTION 8
E-mail and fraud
Most incidents in small businesses begin with a message: an e-mail with an
attachment, a link to a login page, an invoice with changed bank details, a
request from the boss to transfer urgently. No protection program keeps all

<!-- Seite 26 -->
of them away. What helps is that every person in the business knows the
three most common schemes and has a rule she can apply without thinking.
This section provides both.
8.1 Phishing: the message that wants a password
Phishing is the attempt to obtain credentials through a forged message or website. The Federal Office names
features at which one should become suspicious: the message claims urgent need for action or threatens; it
asks for confidential data such as a PIN or a credit card number to be entered; it contains links or forms; or it
appears to come from a known person or organisation but contains an unusual request.
Quelle: BSI, "Wie erkenne ich Phishing in E-Mails und auf Webseiten?", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Verbraucherinnen-und-Verbraucher/Cyber-
Sicherheitslage/Methoden-der-Cyber-Kriminalitaet/Spam-Phishing-Co/Passwortdiebstahl-durch-Phishing/ (search excerpt, accessed 15
September 2026; Section 14 no. 2). Recommendation.
What no longer helps: looking for spelling mistakes. Forged messages today are linguistically flawless, and
Volume 1 explains in Section 12.3 why — tools that generate text are available to everyone. What remains are
the sender address (not the displayed name, but the address behind it), the target of a link (visible when the
mouse rests on it without clicking) and the one question: was I expecting this message?
Three things a business without an IT department can do itself: the second factor (Section 5) ensures that a
phished password alone opens nothing. The password manager (Section 4.4) enters a password only on the
genuine page — on a forged page the field stays empty, and that is the warning sign. And whoever is not sure
does not open the page through the link but types the known address himself.
8.2 Attachments
An attachment nobody expected is not opened. The Austrian portal Watchlist Internet expressly warns, in
the case of forged invoices, against opening attached compressed files because they may contain malware;
the Austrian recommendations against ransomware name e-mail as the way in which it usually enters the
house. For the business this means: invoices come from known suppliers in a known form; everything else is
checked with the sender before it is opened — through a number the business already had, not through the
one in the message.
Quelle: Watchlist Internet, "Ihre Bank hat die Lastschrift zurück buchen lassen – wieder gefälschte Rechnungen", watchlist-
internet.at/news/ (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · onlinesicherheit.gv.at, "Spam und E-Mail-Viren" and "Erpressungstrojaner
– Vorbeugende Maßnahmen" (search excerpts, accessed 15 September 2026). Recommendations.
8.3 CEO fraud
The Austrian Federal Criminal Police Office describes under the term "CEO fraud" a scheme in which criminals
pose as an executive and get employees to transfer money to third-party accounts or to disclose
confidential data. It recommends informing employees about the scheme so that cases are recognised and
reported early; verifying the identity of the supposed superior by looking up the official contact details in the
internal directory, calling and asking whether the transaction was actually authorised; and being particularly
careful with urgent requests outside the usual procedures and with large amounts to unknown accounts.
Quelle: Bundeskriminalamt Österreich, "CEO Fraud / Business E-Mail Compromise (BEC)",
bundeskriminalamt.at/212/eigentum/betrug/ceo_fraud.html (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026). Recommendation of the
authority.
The Union agency names CEO fraud among the most common incidents that small enterprises reported to it.
And Volume 1 describes in Section 12.3 the variant that has been added: the call or video conference with the
imitated voice or image of a superior.
Quelle: ENISA, "Cybersecurity for SMEs – Challenges and Recommendations", June 2021 (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) ·
Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.3 ("identity deception").

<!-- Seite 27 -->
8.4 The changed bank details
The third scheme is the quietest: a supplier — or somebody who looks like one — announces that the bank
details have changed, and the next invoice is paid to the new account. Or an employee — or somebody who
looks like her — asks the payroll office to transfer the salary to a different account from now on. Watchlist
Internet describes both variants; the messages often carry the right signature and are addressed to exactly
the right contact person, because the perpetrators researched beforehand.
Quelle: Watchlist Internet, "'Ich möchte meine Bankdaten ändern': Dieses Mail an die Personalabteilung könnte Betrug sein" and "Nehmen
Sie sich vor gefälschten Zahlungsanweisungen in Acht!", watchlist-internet.at/news/ (search excerpts, accessed 15 September 2026).
8.5 The one rule: the call-back
Against CEO fraud, the changed bank details and the forged call there is a single countermeasure, and it
needs no technology. Volume 1 puts it like this:
Payments and changes of bank details are never approved on the basis of a call, a message
or a video conference, but only after a call-back on the telephone number stored in the
system. Without exception, regardless of urgency and hierarchy.
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.3, box "Effective countermeasure against identity deception" (final version, read 15 September
2026; rendered in substance from the German); the same recommendation from the Austrian Federal Criminal Police Office (Section 8.3).
Volume 1 adds two things a business easily forgets: the rule belongs in the payment instruction — that is,
where it says who approves payments —, and it is to be made expressly clear that a call-back is welcome.
Because the scheme lives on the person who is supposed to transfer not daring to disturb the boss. The
boss has to have said beforehand: disturb me. Always.
INVENTED EXAMPLE: FRIDAY, 4:40 P.M.
The bookkeeper of a metalworking business receives an e-mail from the managing
director, who is at a trade fair: a supplier is waiting for a down payment, it is urgent, the
bank details are below, he is in meetings and not reachable. The e-mail has the right
signature and the right tone. The bookkeeper calls the number that is in the business’s
address book — not the one in the mail. The managing director picks up. He has written no
e-mail. The transfer would have been the largest of the year. On the back of the payment
instruction it has said since the previous year: "Call back always. Even me."
8.6 What the mailbox provider can do
Part of the protection lies not in the business but with the service that delivers the mail: spam and phishing
filters, the check whether a message really comes from the domain it claims, and the labelling of messages
from outside the business. A business without an IT department does not have to understand these
methods; it has to ask its mailbox provider or contractor whether they are set up for its domain, and keep the
answer (Section 12.3). This includes the question of whether its own domain is protected against imitation —
so that nobody sends invoices to its customers in the business’s name.
8.7 Training is a conversation
Audit checklists ask about "awareness" and "training"; the Federal Office names raising employees'
awareness for handling e-mail among its measures against ransomware. In a business with eight employees
the training is a conversation of twenty minutes, twice a year, with this section as the template: the three

<!-- Seite 28 -->
schemes, the call-back rule, and the sentence that nobody gets into trouble for reporting a suspicious
message or delaying a transfer through a call-back. Whoever has held the conversation writes date and
participants on a sheet — that is the evidence that gets asked for (Section 9.4).
Quelle: BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen" (awareness; search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · Volume 1 of this series,
Section 12.4 ("reporting instead of concealing"; freedom from sanctions).
HOW YOU CAN TELL THAT IT HAS BEEN DONE
Every person in the business knows the three schemes and the call-back rule, the rule is
in the payment instruction, and the boss has said that he wants the call-back.
Unexpected attachments are not opened, unexpected links not clicked. The mailbox
provider has confirmed in writing which filters and checks are set up for the domain. And
on a sheet it says when this was last talked about and with whom.

Responsibility, rules, training
The fourth point from Volume 1 is the one audit checklists ask about first
and small businesses skip fastest: "Responsibility: one named responsible
person, even where external support is brought in." It is not the technology
that saves a business in an emergency. It is the person who knows where
the list is.
9.1 One person — and a second
The Federal Office’s audit procedure begins with the subject area "organisation and awareness"; the Austrian
label asks for a "defined security responsibility"; Volume 1 demands a person named by name. What is meant
is not an expert. What is meant is the person in the business who keeps the lists of this handbook — access,
devices, backups, emergency card —, who instructs and checks the contractor, and who takes the decisions
in an emergency. In a business with five employees that is usually the owner herself. And because this person
too can be ill or on holiday, there is a second one who knows where everything is.
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.2 · BSI, "CyberRisikoCheck nach DIN SPEC 27076" (subject areas; Section 2.1) · descriptions of the

Nobody works with administrative rights.

SECTION 10
The emergency: the first hour and the first day
The emergency is the morning on which nothing opens any more — files
encrypted, a ransom note on the screen —, or the afternoon on which the
bank calls, or the evening on which the bookkeeper notices that the
transfer went to a wrong account. In all three cases the first hour decides
more than the first week, and in all three cases the first hour is the worst
time to think about whom to call. This section describes what is to be done
in the first hour and on the first day, and what has to be on a sheet of paper
beforehand.
10.1 The first hour: disconnect, do not delete, call
The Austrian portal onlinesicherheit.gv.at recommends, on suspicion of ransomware, switching off the device
at once or disconnecting it from the network and the internet, because the malware spreads to all devices
connected in the network; then getting professional support to secure what can be secured. The Federal
Office provides a card for IT emergencies that — similar to safety signs for behaviour in case of fire — hangs at
central places and tells employees what to do and whom to call.
Quelle: onlinesicherheit.gv.at, "Erpressungstrojaner – Was tun bei Infektion?" and "Ransomware",
onlinesicherheit.gv.at/Services/Technologie-Schwerpunkte/Ransomware/ and /Themen/Erste-Hilfe/ (search excerpts, accessed 15
September 2026) · BSI / Allianz für Cyber-Sicherheit, "IT-Notfallkarte 'Verhalten bei IT-Notfällen'", bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Unternehmen-
und-Organisationen/Informationen-und-Empfehlungen/Empfehlungen-nach-Angriffszielen/Unternehmen-allgemein/IT-Notfallkarte/
(search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026). Recommendations.
For a business without an IT department the first hour means:
1. Disconnect. Take the affected device off the network — pull the network cable, Wi-Fi off. If it is not clear
which device is affected: disconnect the router from the internet. Do not switch off if an expert is coming
shortly, because traces in the running device are lost; switch off if nobody is coming and the encryption is
visibly spreading. Whoever is unsure disconnects and leaves it on.
2. Do not delete, do not reinstall, do not connect the backup. A backup that is plugged into an infected
device now is the next encrypted backup. The Federal Office names checking backups before restoration
as a measure of its own.
3. Call. The person from Section 9.1, the contractor, and — in Austria — the Cyber Security Hotline of the
Chambers of Commerce, which gives free initial information around the clock (Section 11.1). The numbers
are on the emergency card, not in the encrypted computer.
4. Write down. Time, what was seen, which device, who did what, a photo of the screen with the phone.
Volume 1 calls this "recording the facts"; the data protection authority and the insurer will ask about it.
Quelle: BSI, Maßnahmenkatalog Ransomware (checking backups before restoration; search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · Volume
1 of this series, Section 12.4 · WKO, "Cyber-Security-Hotline", wko.at/it-sicherheit/cyber-security-hotline (search excerpt, accessed 15
September 2026).
10.2 The first day: access, notifications, communication
On the first day three things are added. First, access: if credentials might be affected — and with an infection
through e-mail that is to be assumed — the passwords of the important accounts are changed from a clean
device, e-mail, bank and cloud administration first, and the second factor is checked. Volume 1 calls this
"blocking access and changing credentials where login information was affected". Second, notifications:

<!-- Seite 33 -->
whether personal data are affected is checked now, because the period under the General Data Protection
Regulation runs from awareness (Section 11.2); the report to the police is decided (Section 11.3); the insurer
is informed within the deadline in the policy (Section 2.3). Third, communication: the Federal Office
recommends in its emergency planning preparing alternative communication channels outside the affected
network — who in the business reaches whom and how when e-mail does not work, and what to say to
customers who call.
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.4 · BSI, "Top 10 der Ransomware-Maßnahmen" (emergency planning: alternative communication
options outside the compromised network; search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026). Recommendations.
10.3 Ransom
The Austrian Federal Criminal Police Office advises not to pay ransom: whoever pays confirms to the
criminals that their business model works, and has no guarantee of receiving the decryption. The Austrian
portal says the same — a transfer to criminals never offers a guarantee — and points out that for some
ransomware trojans decryption programs by security researchers exist and that the police have in individual
cases been able to provide tools. The computer emergency response team says it most clearly: whoever is
hit unprepared has no really good options left; the only effective measures are preventive — regular, well-
implemented backups.
Quelle: Bundeskriminalamt Österreich, "Ransomware", bundeskriminalamt.at/212/internet/ransomware.html, and BMI magazine
"Cybercrimebekämpfung", 2023 (search excerpts, accessed 15 September 2026) · onlinesicherheit.gv.at, "Erpressungstrojaner – Was tun bei
Infektion?" (search excerpt) · CERT.at, notice on ransomware, October 2020, cert.at/de/aktuelles/2020/10/ (search excerpt, accessed 15
September 2026). Recommendations; the decision lies with the business, and this handbook does not take it.
Whether a payment can have legal consequences in an individual case is a legal question this handbook does
not answer; whoever has to ask it asks legal advice before deciding.
10.4 After the incident
Volume 1 closes its procedure for the event with the point "adjusting the rules so that the same route is not
open again". That is the part that most often falls away, because after the incident everyone is tired. One
sheet in the folder (Section 9.4): what happened, by which route, what was missing, what was changed, who
checked it. And an appointment in six months at which it is checked whether the change still stands.
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.4 · Art. 33(5) Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (documentation of breaches; text copy of the Official
Journal version, accessed 15 September 2026).
10.5 The emergency card: print it now
Everything in this section assumes that the numbers and accesses needed in the first hour do not lie in the
computer that is currently encrypted. The Federal Office’s emergency card has a field for the business’s own
emergency number; Scutoria’s emergency card (scutoria.com/werkzeuge/notfallkarte, in the premium
account) holds the same details and prints them as a card and as a page. What is on it is the same in both
cases, and it can also be written by hand on a sheet:
The person from Section 9.1 and her deputy, with mobile numbers.
The IT contractor: name, number, second number, customer number, what is agreed (Section 12).
The hotline of the Chambers of Commerce (Austria): 0800 888 133, around the clock.
The computer emergency response team: reports@cert.at, with the hours from Section 11.1.
The reporting office of the Federal Criminal Police Office and the way to a police report (Section 11.3).
The data protection authority and the deadline (Section 11.2).
The insurer: number and notification deadline from the policy.
The mailbox provider, the cloud service, the bank: customer portal and blocking number.
Where the backup lies, where the recovery key lies, who can restore.

The three actions of the first hour: disconnect, do not delete, call.
Quelle: BSI / Allianz für Cyber-Sicherheit, "IT-Notfallkarte" (editable field for the emergency number; search excerpt, accessed 15 September
2026) · numbers and addresses per Section 11 (search excerpts, accessed 15 September 2026; numbers change — check before printing).
The card hangs next to the fire extinguisher and lies in the drawer of the person from Section 9.1. The deputy
has a copy at home.
INVENTED EXAMPLE: THE SATURDAY
A bicycle shop with four employees opens on Saturday at nine. The till does not work, the
computer in the office shows a ransom note. The owner pulls the office computer’s
network cable and disconnects the router from the internet. She calls the hotline of the
Chambers of Commerce, which within ten minutes tells her what not to do and names her
a firm nearby. She photographs the screen. She calls her IT contractor, whose second
number is on the card. The backup from Friday evening lies on a disk in the safe,
unplugged since Friday 7 p.m. On Monday the till runs again. What was missing has since
been on a sheet in the folder: the till was in the same network as the office.
HOW YOU CAN TELL THAT IT HAS BEEN DONE
An emergency card with all numbers, accesses and the three actions is posted and lies
with two people — on paper, not only in the computer. Every person in the business knows
that on suspicion she should disconnect, not delete, and call. It is decided who informs
the data protection authority and who files the police report. And the backup that is
needed after the incident was not connected at the time of the incident.

11.2 The obligation: data protection authority (Union-wide; Austria as the example)
If personal data are affected — lost, encrypted, leaked, altered — the General Data Protection Regulation
requires notification to the supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72
hours after awareness, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural
persons (Section 3.2). The processor — the IT contractor, the cloud service — notifies the business without
undue delay of what it notices; the obligation towards the authority remains with the business.
Quelle: Art. 33(1) and (2) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, text copy of the Official Journal version (accessed 15 September 2026; Section 14 no. 1;
rendered in substance) · Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.4.
Under the Regulation the notification contains at least: the nature of the breach with the categories and
approximate number of persons and records concerned; the name and contact details of a contact point;
the likely consequences; the measures taken or proposed. What is not yet known may be provided in phases
without undue further delay — the deadline requires a notification, not a complete investigation.
Quelle: Art. 33(3) and (4) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, reference as above.
In Austria the supervisory authority is the Datenschutzbehörde. According to its own statements it provides
an online form and a PDF form; a notification can also be made by e-mail to dsb@dsb.gv.at or by post to
Österreichische Datenschutzbehörde, Barichgasse 40-42, 1030 Vienna. In the case of larger incidents such
as ransomware attacks the controller can inform in stages through several notifications.
Quelle: Datenschutzbehörde, "Meldung Data Breach" and "Data-Breach-Verfahren", dsb.gv.at/eingabe-an-die-dsb/-meldung-data-breach
(search excerpts, accessed 15 September 2026; Section 14 no. 8).
Whether a risk exists in an individual case and whether the persons concerned are to be informed (Section
3.2) is a judgement this handbook does not make. It says two things: the deadline is short, and not notifying
out of uncertainty is the worse mistake — the authority itself points out that notification can be made in
stages. Whoever is unsure notifies what he knows and adds what he does not yet know.

11.5 What NIS2 has to do with it — and what not
The Austrian Network and Information System Security Act 2026 obliges covered entities to report
significant cybersecurity incidents to the responsible computer emergency response team — an early

THREE CONFUSIONS
The 72 hours of the General Data Protection Regulation are not the 72 hours of the NIS

The reporting office of the Federal Criminal Police Office is not a complaint; the complaint
is the police station. And the hotline of the Chambers of Commerce is not an authority; it
is initial information that tells you what not to do and refers you to a firm.

SECTION 12
Contractors: what you may demand of an IT provider
A business without an IT department usually has an IT contractor, a systems
house, a cloud service, a mailbox provider — and the assumption that they
take care of everything. That is the most dangerous assumption in this
handbook. The contractor does what is agreed, and in most contracts of
small businesses it says: come when something does not work. This section
says what a business may agree, demand and check without being an
expert itself.
12.1 What a contractor is — and what he is not
The Union agency names the management of third parties among the points small enterprises should
observe when confidential or sensitive information is concerned; the Federal Office’s audit procedure is
carried out by a "qualified IT service provider"; and the General Data Protection Regulation permits the
processing of personal data on behalf of a controller only by processors providing sufficient guarantees for
appropriate measures, and only on the basis of a contract.
Quelle: ENISA, "Cybersecurity for SMEs – Challenges and Recommendations", June 2021 (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) ·
BSI, "CyberRisikoCheck nach DIN SPEC 27076" (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · Art. 28(1) and (3) Regulation (EU) 2016/679,
text copy of the Official Journal version (accessed 15 September 2026; Section 14 no. 1).
What the contractor is not: the person from Section 9.1. The responsibility stays in the business even when
the work goes out of house. Volume 1 says it in its fourth point: "one named responsible person, even where
external support is brought in". And the obligation towards the data protection authority stays with the
business even if the incident happened at the contractor (Section 11.2).
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.2 · Art. 33(1) and (2) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, reference as above.
12.2 Eight questions before the contract
Volume 1 describes in Section 2.4 the approach of judging tools by criteria rather than by names. For an IT
contractor there are eight questions, and each has a good and a bad answer:
1. Who backs up, and where to? Good answer: "We do. Daily, one copy separate, older states three
months, you get a monthly confirmation." Bad answer: "That runs." (Section 6)
2. When was the last restore? Good answer: a date. Bad answer: "That works." (Section 6.6)
3. Who installs updates — on all devices, including server and router? Good answer: "We do, monthly,
with a list." Bad answer: "The computers do that themselves." (Section 7)

<!-- Seite 38 -->
4. With which access do you work in our network, and who at your firm has it? Good answer: an
individual account per technician, with a second factor, and a list. Bad answer: a shared account with the
password that has applied for years. (Section 5)
5. What happens if something happens at your firm? Good answer: "We report to you without delay every
incident that may affect your systems." Bad answer: silence. (Section 12.3)
6. How do we reach you on a Saturday? Good answer: a number, a second number, a time. Bad answer:
"Open a ticket." (Section 10)
7. What do we get if we terminate? Good answer: all data, all accesses, all documentation, in a readable
format, by a fixed date. Bad answer: "We'll sort that out then."
8. Is there a data processing agreement? Good answer: yes, here it is. Bad answer: "What is that?"
(Section 3.3)
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 2.4 (approach) · the questions are this handbook’s questions; their basis is the recommendations
from Part II and the obligations from Section 3.
12.3 Six commitments in the contract
There is an official text that says what a major customer should write into the contract with its IT provider:
the implementing regulation to the NIS2 Directive for digital providers. It does not apply to the reader of this
handbook. But it is a usable list of what a business may demand of its contractor, because it says what the
big ones demand: cybersecurity requirements for the provider; the obligation to report without undue delay
security incidents that pose a risk to the customer’s systems; the right to audit or to receive audit reports;
the obligation to remedy vulnerabilities; requirements for subcontracting; and obligations at contract
termination, such as retrieval and disposal of the information the provider obtained.
Quelle: Annex, point 5.1.4(a), (d), (e), (f), (g) and (h) Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 of 17 October 2024, read in a
catalogue copy in German and rendered in substance (research/lieferkette, accessed 14 September 2026; Section 14 no. 1); directly
applicable only to the digital providers named there and their contracts — here as a list of what is customary, not as an obligation of the
reader.
For a small business this translates into six sentences that can stand in any contract or in an agreement by e-
mail — this handbook supplies no clause, only the content:
What the contractor does for the business is listed: backups, updates, protection programs, access, with
frequency.
The contractor reports to the business without delay every incident at his firm that may affect the
business’s systems.
The business receives once a year, or on request, a short report: what was backed up, what installed,
what restored.
Known vulnerabilities are remedied within an agreed time.
If the contractor uses subcontractors — a data centre, a cloud service — the business knows which.
At contract end the business receives its data, accesses and documentation in readable form, and the
contractor deletes what he holds and confirms it.
Where personal data are involved, the data processing agreement is added; that is a legal document, and
whoever has none gets it from the contractor or from data protection advice, not from this handbook.
12.4 Cloud services and mailbox providers
For services with which a business negotiates no contract but accepts terms — mailbox, cloud storage,
accounting service — the same questions apply, except that the answers are in the provider’s terms and help
pages. Four of them are decisive: does the service keep older states, and for how long (Section 6.4)? Can the
business get all its data out in a readable format, and has it tried once? Who at the provider can access the

<!-- Seite 39 -->
data, and where do they lie? And is there a data processing agreement — with business accounts of large
providers usually as part of the terms. Volume 1 puts the same checkpoints in Section 2.4 for AI tools; they
apply to every service.
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 2.4 "Contractual checkpoints" · Art. 28(3) Regulation (EU) 2016/679, reference as above.
12.5 Once a year: have it shown
The General Data Protection Regulation names a process for regularly testing the measures as an example;
the implementing regulation names the right to audit reports. For a small business that is one appointment a
year with the contractor at which it has four things shown: the last restore, with date; the list of devices with
the state of updates; the list of accesses the contractor has; and the answer to the question of what
security incidents occurred at the contractor in the past year. The result goes as a sheet into the folder
(Section 9.4). Whoever has done that two years running can answer any questionnaire.
Quelle: Art. 32(1)(d) Regulation (EU) 2016/679 · Annex, point 5.1.4(e) Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 (references as above).
INVENTED EXAMPLE: THE CONTRACT THAT WAS CALLED "SUPPORT"
A tax adviser with six employees has for years paid a monthly flat rate for "IT support".
When an insurer’s questionnaire asks about the last restore test, he calls his contractor.
The contractor says: the backup was never part of the flat rate, the predecessor had set it
up, he had only "let it run". Nobody had lied. Nobody had ever written down what the flat
rate included. It has since been on one page, and once a year the contractor shows the
last restore.
HOW YOU CAN TELL THAT IT HAS BEEN DONE
It is written down what the contractor does for the business and what not — backups,
updates, access, availability, incident reporting, contract end —, and a data processing
agreement exists. The contractor has his own accesses with a second factor, and the
business knows who has them. Once a year he shows the last restore and the state of
updates, and the sheet lies in the folder. The responsibility has nevertheless stayed in the
house.

SECTION 15
Glossary
From the glossary of Volume 1 (Section 18) are adopted and not repeated
here: data processing agreement, deployer, deepfake. Volume 3 explains
questionnaire, supplier statement and evidence from the supplier’s side.
Administrative rights
Rights of an account to change everything on a device or in a service; also administrator. Should not be used
for daily work.
Audit checklist
Question catalogue by which an audit procedure, a label, an insurer or a customer asks about a business’s
security; here the CyberRisikoCheck, the cyber risk rating, policyholder duties, supplier questionnaire.
CEO fraud
Fraud in which somebody poses as an executive and demands a transfer or data; the Federal Criminal Police
Office calls it CEO fraud or business e-mail compromise.
CSIRT

<!-- Seite 45 -->
Computer security incident response team, the computer emergency response team of a state or sector; in
Austria CERT.at.
Cyber risk rating
Austrian assessment scheme of the Kuratorium Sicheres Österreich on which the Cyber Trust Austria Label
is based; entry tier on the basis of a self-declaration.
Disk encryption
Encryption of the storage of a laptop, phone or storage medium so that the data remain unreadable on loss
or theft.
End of support
Point in time from which a manufacturer no longer supplies security updates for an operating system or
device.
Emergency card
Notice with the numbers, accesses and actions for the first hour of an incident; the model is the Federal
Office’s IT emergency card.
Password manager
Program that stores passwords in encrypted form, enters them on login and generates strong passwords;
recommended by the Federal Office.
Personal data breach
Legal term of the General Data Protection Regulation (Article 4(12)): destruction, loss, alteration,
unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data; triggers notification under Article 33 where a risk
exists.
Phishing
Attempt to obtain credentials through a forged message or website.
Policyholder duty
Requirement of conduct in an insurance contract whose breach can impair the cover; in cyber policies for
instance backups, passwords, updates.
Principle of least privilege
Every person and every account gets only the rights needed for the work; audit checklists ask about it.
Processor
Whoever processes personal data on behalf of a business, such as an IT contractor or a cloud service; Article
28 of the General Data Protection Regulation requires a contract for it (Volume 1: data processing
agreement).
Ransomware
Malware that encrypts files and demands money for decryption; in Austria also called extortion or encryption
trojan.
Reporting office
Contact point of the Federal Criminal Police Office for tips on internet crime; a report there is not a criminal
complaint.
Restore test
Rehearsal of whether files, folders or whole devices can actually be recovered from the backup; without it the
backup is an assumption.
Second factor

<!-- Seite 46 -->
Second proof at login besides the password: a code from an app, a hardware key, a text message; also two-
factor or multi-factor authentication.
Self-declaration
Statement by a business about its own state that a label or a customer accepts and checks for plausibility;
has to be true.
Separate copy
Backup that after the copying process is separated from the network and the original, so that an attacker
with access to the original does not reach it; the Federal Office calls this an offline backup.
Versioning
Keeping older states of a file or backup so that a damaged or encrypted version does not overwrite the good
one.
