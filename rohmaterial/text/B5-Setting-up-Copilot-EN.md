PRELIMINARY NOTE
Purpose and structure
This guide is addressed to owners, managing directors and office managers
of small and medium-sized businesses that already use Microsoft 365 — for
e-mail, for files, for Teams — and now face the question of whether and how
to add Copilot. They have no IT department and nobody in the building who
sees an admin center every day. They want to know where to click, and
above all: what is different afterwards.
The most important thing first, because everything else depends on it: Copilot is not a new place where
data is kept. It is a new way of reaching the data that is already there. It reads the mailbox, the files and
the Teams messages of the person using it — precisely those the person can access anyway. Not more, but
not less either. That sounds reassuring, and it is the actual task: in most businesses, people have access to
more than anyone ever decided. A folder that was shared "with everyone" three years ago. A link that went "to
everyone in the business" because that was the default. Until now nobody noticed, because nobody looked.
Copilot looks. It answers the question "What do my colleagues earn?" from a file that has been lying open for
years — and it has done nothing wrong.
That is why setting up Copilot is, to a small extent, assigning a licence, and to a large extent, tidying up the
shares. This guide is built accordingly.
Two things are called Copilot
The provider uses the name for two different things. Copilot Chat is a chat with web search that comes with
every business account and needs no licence of its own; it works on what you write to it and on the web, not
on your files. The licensed Copilot — called "Microsoft 365 Copilot", "Microsoft Copilot" or "Copilot Business"
in the admin center, depending on the date of purchase — is the assistant in Word, Outlook, Excel and Teams
that reaches mailbox, filing and conversations through the business’s own services. This guide is about the
licensed Copilot; the chat appears where it is controlled in the same administration interface.
Source: Microsoft Learn, "Enterprise data protection in Microsoft Copilot and Microsoft Copilot Chat", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-
365/copilot/enterprise-data-protection (search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026) · Microsoft Learn, "Data, privacy, and security for
web search in Microsoft Copilot and Microsoft Copilot Chat", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/manage-public-web-access
(search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026).
How this guide came about, and what that means for you
This guide describes the administration interfaces as they stood on 15 September 2026. Interfaces age;
menus get renamed, switches move. That is why every operating path states where it leads and what must
be visible afterwards — if your screen shows something else, your screen is right, and the path here is the
clue for what to look for.
The statements about the provider — what happens to inputs, where data is processed, how long it is kept —
come exclusively from the provider’s own pages, with the date of access and the address under every
statement. What is not there is not here either; what could only be verified with an excerpt of the page is
marked as a search excerpt and listed in the appendix under "Uncertainties". A gap is a result. A guess would
be a harm, because you act on this guide.

What this guide is not
Not legal advice. Whether your business may use Copilot on customer data, whether your contract with the
provider covers processing on your behalf, whether a data protection impact assessment is needed — that is
decided by your business and by what applies to you. Volume 1 of this series, "AI in the Company", answers
these questions in Section 2.4 (the contractual checkpoints) and Section 11 (data protection). Wherever a
legal question arises here, the reference is given, not an answer.
Not a usage policy. Who in the business may use Copilot for what is a separate document. Section 7 gives
four rules for a start; the complete set of rules with approval procedure and records is Volume 2, "AI Policies
for the Workforce", and the AI Evidence Kit of the same series.
Not purchasing advice and no price. Whether Copilot fits your business as an add-on licence to your
existing plan or as a bundle is a question for your invoice, not for this guide. Prices are not stated here
because they go out of date.
Not a guide to Excel, PowerPoint, agents or Copilot Studio. The subject is Word, Outlook and Teams — the
three places where a small business meets Copilot first. Other providers, such as Google Workspace, are not
covered.

SECTION 1
Before you begin
Most businesses buy the licence first and only then think about who gets it
and what the assistant should reach. With a chat tool that only sees what
you type into it, that is a small mistake. With Copilot it is a large one,
because on day one the assistant finds everything that lies open in your
business. Five decisions belong before that. Together they take half an hour
and spare you the clean-up.
1.1 Who gets a licence?
Not "everyone" and not "just me, let’s see". Write down two or three functions: the office manager and me.
Every licence is a person who can reach everything they are allowed to see with one question — and whom
you have to take out again on their last working day.
Copilot is an add-on per person, not a switch for the whole business. That is convenient: you can start with
one licence, on your own account, and extend once the shares are in order. Whoever starts small can grow.
Whoever starts wide has to collect again.
1.2 For what?
Two or three concrete tasks, not a wish list. Summarise long e-mail threads. Prepare quotation texts from the
enquiry. Turn meetings into tasks. That is not bureaucracy but the yardstick by which you measure in three
months whether the licence has paid off. Without it, only a feeling remains.
1.3 Where are your files?
This question does not exist with other tools, and here it decides almost everything. Copilot in Word works
with files that are stored in OneDrive or SharePoint and that the person has access to; files that only sit on
the computer or on an old server it does not see.
Source: Microsoft Support, "Draft and add content with Copilot in Word", support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/draft-and-add-content-with-
copilot-in-word-069c91f0-9e42-4c9a-bbce-fddf5d581541 (search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026) · Microsoft Support, "Frequently
asked questions about Copilot in Excel", support.microsoft.com/en-gb/office/frequently-asked-questions-about-copilot-in-excel-7a13758f-
d61e-4a56-8440-f2c9a07802ec (search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026).
If your files are not yet in the cloud storage, introducing Copilot is at the same time introducing the cloud
storage — and that is the larger of the two decisions. It has nothing to do with the assistant and should not
depend on it: first the filing, with order and shares (Section 4), then the assistant on top. If your files are
already there, then this applies: what is shared today is findable for Copilot tomorrow. Section 4 shows how
to check.
1.4 With which data?
Here this guide stops being an operating manual. What may go into an AI tool and what may not is not
decided at the interface but by your business and by what applies to you. With Copilot the question
sharpens, because "going in" no longer means only what somebody types, but also what sits in a filing area
the assistant searches. Personnel files in a folder that three people can access used to be a folder. With
Copilot they are an answer to a question.

<!-- Seite 7 -->
What to consider is set out in Volume 1, Section 12.3 — where data leakage through inputs is described as a
risk — and in Section 2.4, where the contractual checkpoints stand. For a start, one line you draw yourself is
enough: What you would not send to an external service provider without a contract does not belong in
a filing area Copilot sees either — nor in an input. Section 6 turns that into a list.
1.5 Who tidies up?
One person, by name. Once a month they check who has a licence, which shares have been added, and
whether what was decided three months ago still holds. In a one-person business that is you — so put an
appointment in your calendar, otherwise it will not happen. Section 8 says what this person looks at.

Whoever has the shares in order has Copilot in order.

SECTION 2
Licence, roles and access
With Copilot there is no "create an account". Your business already has its
Microsoft 365 account — the tenant, as the provider calls it — with
mailboxes, filing and administration. Copilot is added as a licence per
person. That is more convenient than with a standalone tool, and at the
same time the reason why the questions in this section are different: not
"which plan" but "which prerequisites, which roles, which way in and which
way out".
2.1 Two routes: add-on licence or bundle
According to the provider’s pages, the licensed Copilot is an add-on plan to an existing plan. The prerequisite
is, among others, Microsoft 365 Business Basic, Business Standard or Business Premium; every person
needs the Copilot licence and a matching base licence.
Source: Microsoft Learn, "License Options for Microsoft Copilot", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/microsoft-365-copilot-
licensing (search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026: "Before your users can use Microsoft Copilot, they must be assigned a Microsoft
Copilot license along with a qualifying pre-requisite license").
For businesses with at most 300 users and one of the three Business plans, the provider lists the edition
Copilot Business: available only as an add-on to these plans, with the same capabilities as the large Copilot,
limited to 300 seats per tenant, purchasable in the admin center, through a partner or directly from the
provider, as an annual commitment with monthly or annual billing. According to the same excerpt, the large
Copilot requires a minimum purchase of 300 seats — so for a small business it is not the route.
Source: Microsoft Learn, "Microsoft Copilot Business FAQ", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/copilot-business-faq (search
excerpt, accessed 14 and 15 September 2026: "The Copilot Business add-on delivers the same capabilities as the Microsoft Copilot offering";
"Copilot Business supports up to 300 seats per tenant"; "annual commitment with either monthly or annual billing").
Since 1 July 2026 there have also been bundles, "Microsoft 365 Business Standard with Copilot" and
"Business Premium with Copilot", which contain the apps and Copilot in one plan.
Source: Microsoft 365 Blog, "Introducing Microsoft 365 Business with Copilot: The new standard for small business", 28 May 2026,
microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/blog/2026/05/28/introducing-microsoft-365-business-with-copilot-the-new-standard-for-small-
business/ (read in full, accessed 14 September 2026).
Which route suits your business is a question for your invoice and your commitment period, not for this guide.
For everything that follows it makes no difference: in the end a person has a Copilot licence or not.

2.2 What has to be in place beforehand
As minimum requirements the provider names: accounts in Microsoft Entra ID (that is, the business accounts
of your tenant), the Copilot licence plus base licence per person, a OneDrive account for some features, the
installed Microsoft 365 apps on the update channel "Current Channel" or "Monthly Enterprise Channel" —
not the semi-annual channel — and, for the web apps, a modern browser in which third-party cookies are
allowed.
Source: Microsoft Learn, "Minimum requirements to deploy Microsoft Copilot in your organization", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-
365/copilot/microsoft-365-copilot-minimum-requirements (search excerpt, accessed 15 September 2026) · Microsoft Learn, "App and

For a small business this usually means: nothing to do, if Word and Outlook were installed the normal way and
are up to date. Two things you should check. First, whether every person who is to receive a licence has a
OneDrive and uses it — because Copilot in Word only sees files from OneDrive or SharePoint (Section 1.3).
Second, the update channel, in case your apps were set up by a service provider: in Word under "File",
"Account", the item on updates states which channel the app runs on. If it shows a semi-annual channel, talk
to the service provider before buying licences.
2.3 Who administers: the roles
In the admin center you assign roles, not only licences. For Copilot four of them matter.
The Global Administrator may do everything. The provider recommends assigning this role to fewer than five
people and limiting it to cases for which no other role suffices; in general the recommendation is to use roles
with the fewest permissions and to keep the number of people with administrative permissions small.
Source: Microsoft Learn, "About administrator roles in the Microsoft 365 admin center", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-
365/admin/add-users/about-admin-roles (search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026: "Microsoft recommends that you assign the Global
Administrator role to fewer than five people in your organization").
The AI Administrator is the role with which the Copilot page in the admin center is viewed and changed.
Source: Microsoft Learn, "Manage Microsoft Copilot Scenarios", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/microsoft-365-copilot-
page (search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026: "To view and make changes to the Copilot scenarios in the Microsoft 365 admin center,
sign in with the AI Administrator role").
The SharePoint Administrator manages sharing settings, sites and the reports that Section 4 is about. The
License Administrator assigns licences. Which rights these two roles comprise in detail could not be read on
the pages checked; the appendix lists this as an open point.
For a business without an IT department, this guide’s recommendation is simple, and it is its own, not the
provider's: One person administers, a second one can in an emergency. The second person is your fallback
— in a one-person business a second administrator account whose credentials are kept where somebody
can reach them if it matters. Whoever locks themselves out of the admin center does not get back in without
the provider.
The path to assign a role: in the admin center, on the left "Users", then "Active users"; click the person; in the
side panel open the tab for account and roles, choose "Manage roles"; tick the role there and save.
Afterwards the role is shown in the person’s side panel.

licence tick.

2.6 When somebody leaves the business
The step everyone forgets, and the only one with a deadline: on the last working day, not the week after.
With Copilot it consists of two parts. The first is the licence (2.5) — it keeps costing as long as it is assigned.
The second is the account itself: block sign-in, hand over mailbox and OneDrive, check the person’s shares.
This second part is the same as without Copilot and is not part of this guide; how a business handles access
on departure is covered in Volume 2, Section 6.
One thing does belong here, because it is new with Copilot: what the departed person shared stays shared.
A link "to everyone in the business" on a file in their OneDrive keeps working as long as the OneDrive exists —
and Copilot finds the file for everyone who never saw the link. The monthly check in Section 8 therefore looks
at the sharing reports after a person leaves, not only at the licence list.

3.4 Web search
Copilot can search the web for an answer. According to the provider, the queries go to the search engine
without user and tenant identifiers and are not used for training — but they leave the data boundary (3.2).
The switch sits under "Copilot", "Settings", "Data access", "Web search" and is called "Allow web search in

SECTION 4
Filing and sharing: what Copilot sees
Up to here you have a licence and checked switches. Now comes the part
this is all about. With a chat tool you ask "what may I enter"; with Copilot
you additionally ask "what sits in my business in such a way that the
assistant finds it". The second question is the larger one, and it has a
property you need to know: Copilot changes nothing about the answers. It
only asks the question for the first time.
4.1 The principle: Copilot shows only what the person may see anyway
The provider says so in several places: Copilot shows a person only data on which they have at least view
permission, with the same access controls as the other services; the index the provider builds for search also
respects the identity boundary of the person.
Source: Microsoft Learn, "Data, Privacy, and Security for Microsoft Copilot", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/microsoft-
365-copilot-privacy (search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026: "presents only data that each individual can access") · Microsoft Learn,
"Microsoft Copilot architecture and how it works", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/microsoft-365-copilot-architecture
(search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026: "only surfaces organizational data to which individual users have at least view permissions") ·
Microsoft Learn, "Semantic indexing for Microsoft 365 Copilot", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoftsearch/semantic-index-for-copilot
(search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026: "the grounding process only accesses content that the current user is authorized to
access").
That is the good news, and it is the whole consolation: Copilot does not extend rights. The bad news sits in
the same sentence. What until now was found only by those who knew the folder is now found by everyone
whose share reaches — with a question in everyday language. The provider itself describes the introduction
of Copilot as remediating oversharing and calls this the first step of a secure foundation.
Source: Microsoft Learn, "Configure a secure and governed foundation for Microsoft Copilot", learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-

September 2026: "establishing a secure and governed foundation for Copilot by remediating oversharing").

Oversharing does not arise from intent. It arises from defaults, from links that go "to everyone" because that
was the quickest way, and from folders that were opened once for a project and never closed again. This
section goes through them in the order in which they occur in a small business.
4.2 The three link types — and the one that is the trap
When somebody shares a file in OneDrive or SharePoint, they choose a link type. The provider describes
three:
"Anyone": the link works for anyone who has it, without signing in; it can be forwarded.
"People in your organization" ("Only people in your organization"): the link gives everyone in the
business who has it access — not only the person it was sent to.
"Specific people": the link works only for the people named when sharing.
Source: Microsoft Learn, "How shareable links work in OneDrive and SharePoint in Microsoft 365", learn.microsoft.com/en-
us/sharepoint/shareable-links-anyone-specific-people-organization (search excerpt, accessed 14 September 2026: "Specific people links
only work for the people that users specify when they share the item"; "Only people in your organization links provide access to the file or
folder to anyone in your organization who has the link").
The middle one is the trap for Copilot. It sounds harmless, it is the convenient choice, and it does something
hardly anyone knows: as soon as such a link exists, the file has a share "to everyone in the business" —
regardless of whom the link was sent to. For the assistant that means: the file is findable for every person in
the business, including those who never received the link and do not know the folder. The staff list that went
"only to accounting" two years ago is, with this link type, an answer to the question "What does my colleague
earn?" — for everyone.
THE "PEOPLE IN YOUR ORGANIZATION" LINK
It does not share with the person you send it to. It shares with the business. Whoever
wants to give something to one person chooses "Specific people". Everything else is, with
Copilot, a share with everyone.

4.4 Shared places and private places
It helps to think of the filing as three rings.

member list is correct. A team "Management" with a member who left management a year ago is, with

SECTION 6
What never goes in
This section is the shortest and the most important. It does not answer the
question of what is permitted — that is decided by your business and by
what applies to you, and Volume 1 is there for that. It answers the question
where there is no weighing up.
The list is substantiated in Volume 1, Section 11 (data protection, especially 11.1 and 11.2) and Section 12.3
(AI-specific risks). This guide lists, it does not substantiate.
6.1 With Copilot, "in" means two things
With a chat tool, "putting in" means: typing, pasting, uploading. With Copilot a second way is added that
needs no typing at all: a file sits in a filing area the asking person can access. The staff list nobody would
ever have entered into an AI tool is, with Copilot, an answer — if it sits in a folder that is shared with the

<!-- Seite 26 -->
person.
The list below therefore applies to both: to what somebody enters, and to what sits in a filing area not
restricted to "Specific people". For the filing, Section 4 is the path; here stands only what must not lie open
there.
6.2 The list
Credentials. Passwords, keys, recovery codes, access links. Not even "just for formatting", not even in a
picture — and not in a file "Passwords.docx" on a team site.
Health data. Diagnoses, findings, sick leave with names — including those of your own employees, including
in a list you "just want summarised", including the sick-leave list in the office manager’s folder if that folder is
shared with the business.
Personnel data. Applications, appraisals, salaries, warnings, dismissals. The temptation is great because
precisely these texts are tedious to write. The text may be created — the person in it stays anonymous until
the paper leaves the business. And the folder in which such things sit is the first one you check in Section 4.
Customer data without a basis. The most frequent case in practice, and the only one with a condition:
without a basis. Whether there is one is not stated here but in Volume 1, Sections 11.1 and 11.2.
What is subject to a duty of confidentiality. Client, professional or contractual secrets. If you would not be
allowed to send it to a service provider without a contract, you may not enter it here — nor place it in a filing
area Copilot searches for others.
Third-party documents without permission. Competitors' quotations, forwarded contracts, everything
that belongs to somebody else and merely lies in front of you.
6.3 The rule of thumb for everything not on the list
Would you feel uncomfortable if the person concerned learned that you entered this text
here — or that everyone in the business can find it with one question?
If yes: do not enter it, or remove the names first; and set the folder to "Specific people". The question
replaces no legal examination; it only catches the cases where one already has an uneasy feeling and pushes
it aside.
6.4 If something got in after all
No drama, but no shrug either. In order, and with Copilot with one step more than usual:
1. Delete what was entered. The Copilot activity history via "My Account", "Data and Privacy" (Section 3.8).
What Copilot wrote into a file remains, according to the provider, in the file — so look at the file separately.
2. Find where the source lies. That is the step that exists only with Copilot: if the answer contained
something that should not have come, there is a file somewhere with a share that is wrong. In the file
"Share", "Manage access", and go through the links and people. The log from Section 3.9 says which files
Copilot read for the answer.
3. Check whether it lies elsewhere too — in the assistant’s memory (3.5), in a saved transcript (3.7), in a
second copy of the file.
4. Note what happened. One sentence with a date is enough. Whoever notes nothing cannot say later
whether it was once or constant.

SECTION 7
When staff use it
As soon as a second person has a licence, the question changes. Until then
it was about what you enter and what you find. Now it is about what
somebody enters and finds whom you do not watch — and who assumes it is
in order because you gave them the licence. That is not distrust. It is the
simple observation that nobody can guess what you were thinking when
setting up.
7.1 Four rules, one page
More is not needed for a start. One page everyone reads works better than ten pages nobody reads.
First: what never goes in. The list from Section 6, unchanged. Not summarised, not "in substance" — the list.
Second: who reads before something goes out. Every result that leaves the business — to customers, to
authorities, to suppliers — has been read by a human beforehand. Who, you decide: the author, or for some
things a second person. Section 5.4 says what to look for.
Third: where to go when unsure. One name. Without this rule people guess, and they guess towards "it'll be
fine".
Fourth, only with Copilot: whoever shares a file shares it with Copilot. The "People in your organization"
link gives everyone in the business access, and Copilot finds the file for everyone. Whoever wants to give
something to one person chooses "Specific people". Whoever finds something they should not find tells the
name from rule three — they did nothing wrong, the share was.
These four rules cannot be stored in the interface itself; on the provider’s pages checked there is no place
where a business shows all users a text at start-up. Paper is the way, or a page in the team site everyone has
access to — which is then deliberately one that Copilot may see.
7.2 The conversation before the licence
Five minutes, not fifty, and before the licence, not after. What belongs in it:
What we use it for — the two or three tasks from Section 1. A clear purpose prevents more nonsense than
any prohibition.
The four rules, read aloud, not handed out.

<!-- Seite 28 -->
The test from Section 4.7. Show once what Copilot answers to a question about salaries — on your
account, after you have tidied up. Whoever has seen that understands rule four without further
explanation.
The assurance that questions are not a weakness. Whoever does not dare to ask tries it secretly — and
then you do not find out.
7.3 What you should not do
Do not announce surveillance you do not carry out. The log from Section 3.9 exists, and it shows who used
Copilot when. If you say "I'll look at that" and do not, you have lost both: control and credibility. If you do, staff
must know beforehand — whether and how a business may evaluate such a log is not a question for this
guide; Volume 1, Section 7.3, covers co-determination, Section 11 data protection.
No prohibition without an alternative. "No customer data" alone leads to somebody doing it anyway and
saying nothing. "No customer data — if you need it, come to me" leads to them coming.
No licence "to try out" for everyone. With a chat tool that is a cost problem. With Copilot it is the day ten
people ask for the first time what sits in the filing.
7.4 Where this guide ends
What stands here are four rules for a start, not a set of rules. A complete usage policy with approval
procedure, training material and records is a separate product: Volume 2, "AI Policies for the Workforce",
says what belongs in a policy and why (Section 5 on approving tools, Section 8 on training and evidence); the
AI Evidence Kit of the same series delivers the ready-to-fill files. This guide sets up; the volume and the kit
regulate.
SECTION 8
Checking and keeping track
A setup nobody keeps track of is a guess after half a year. This is the
countermeasure, and it is deliberately kept small: thirty minutes a month.
Whatever demands more does not take place. With Copilot it is ten minutes
more than with a chat tool, because the shares come on top — and because
shares change by themselves, every time somebody clicks "Share".
8.1 The monthly check
Who has a licence? Admin center, "Billing", "Licenses", Copilot entry: look at the list of people and compare it
with reality. Somebody left? Somebody added without anyone having had the conversation from Section 7?

invented names

links report anew (Section 4.5). Look at the sites at the top of the list; check "Anyone" and "People in the
organization" links created since last time. That is the point that is new with Copilot and that yields the most.

Are the member lists right? The teams and groups from Section 4.4, especially those with "management",
"personnel" or "finance" in the name. Whoever no longer belongs goes out.
Are the switches still as set? Copilot page in the admin center: flex routing, AI providers, web search, chat
pinned (Section 3). Defaults can change with changes by the provider; one look a month costs two minutes.
What did Copilot access? Purview portal, "Audit", activity "Interacted with Copilot" (Section 3.9): do not read

Does what you decided in Section 1 still hold? The purpose changes, and that is fine — as long as you
notice and adjust the rules instead of letting them quietly lapse.
8.2 Four signs that things are getting out of hand
Copilot answers questions it should not answer. The test from Section 4.7, once every month: "Which files
about salaries are there?" If the answer differs from last time, somebody has shared.
Results go out unread. Recognisable by phrasings nobody in the business writes like that, or by details that
are wrong. If that happens twice, the second rule from Section 7 is just a sentence on paper.
The sharing links report grows upwards. A site on which more "People in the organization" links appear
every month is a site where somebody does not know the fourth rule — or where the default link type has
been reset (Section 4.3).
Nobody asks any more. Sounds like success, but is usually the opposite: at the beginning there are
questions. If they stop entirely, either nobody has doubts any more — or nobody dares.
8.3 What you note
One sheet, one line a month: date, who checked, what was noticed, what was changed. No form, no software.
The purpose is not documentation for its own sake — it is the only way to say in half a year whether
something has got better or worse. And it is the list on which the emergency brakes from Section 4.6 stand
with a date.
If you have to prove something beyond that, that is a different question. What a business has to prove is set
out in Volume 1, Section 8.4 (the deployer’s obligations at a glance); on documented instruction in particular
Section 7.5. The usage reports the provider makes available for administrators are a place where you see
whether licences are used; what they show in detail could not be read on the pages checked.
8.4 Once a quarter: the interface
Interfaces age. This guide describes them as of the date in the preface. Once a quarter go through the
operating paths from Sections 2 to 4 once and note what is called differently or sits elsewhere. If a switch
from Section 3 has disappeared, the question is not whether the guide is wrong but where the switch has
moved — and whether it still exists.

Glossary
From the glossary of Volume 1 of this series the following are adopted and
not explained anew here: data processing agreement, deployer, provider
(in the sense of the AI Act) and data protection impact assessment.
Outside the glossary of Volume 1, the word "provider" in this guide simply
denotes the company that supplies Microsoft 365 and Copilot.
Admin center
The administration interface of Microsoft 365 in which users, licences, roles and the Copilot page are
managed. Alongside it there are separate admin centers for SharePoint and for Teams as well as the Purview
portal; all are linked from the Microsoft 365 admin center.
AI Administrator
Role in the admin center with which the Copilot page is viewed and changed (Section 2.3).
Update channel
The setting determining the rhythm in which the installed Microsoft 365 apps receive new features.
According to the provider, Copilot requires "Current Channel" or "Monthly Enterprise Channel" (Section 2.2).
Copilot Business
The edition of the licensed Copilot for businesses with at most 300 users on a Business plan (Section 2.1).
Copilot Chat
The chat with web search that comes with every business account without a Copilot licence and does not
access the business’s files (Preface, Section 3.6).
EU Data Boundary
The provider’s commitment to process and store certain services for customers in the EU within the EU; with
the exceptions web search and third-party models (Section 3.2). What it comprises in detail, see
Uncertainties no. 6.
Flex routing
Switch that allows processing by the language model outside the EU Data Boundary at times of peak demand
(Section 3.2).
Sharing link
A link to a file or folder in OneDrive or SharePoint with one of three types: "Anyone", "People in your
organization", "Specific people" (Section 4.2).
Restricted Content Discovery
Setting per SharePoint site that makes the site invisible to Copilot and organisation-wide search without
changing access (Section 4.6).
Tenant
A business’s Microsoft 365 account as a whole: all users, mailboxes, filing areas and settings under one
administration.
Microsoft Graph
The interface through which, according to the provider, Copilot accesses the business’s mailbox, files and
conversations — with the same rights as the person asking (Section 4.1).
OneDrive

<!-- Seite 36 -->
Each person’s personal cloud storage in the business; private as long as nothing is shared from it (Section
4.4).
Purview portal
The provider’s interface for retention, search and logging ("Audit", "eDiscovery", "Retention policies")
(Sections 3.8, 3.9).
SharePoint
The business’s shared cloud storage; every team site is a SharePoint site (Section 4.4).
SharePoint Advanced Management
A set of administration features for sharing reports and access reviews which, according to the provider,
SharePoint Administrators receive as soon as a Copilot licence is assigned in the business (Section 4.5).
Default link type
The link type OneDrive and SharePoint propose when somebody clicks "Share"; configurable at business
level (Section 4.3).
Oversharing
The state in which more people have access to a file than anyone ever decided; according to the provider the

Site access review
A process by which the owner of a site is asked to go through its access; to be started from a sharing report
