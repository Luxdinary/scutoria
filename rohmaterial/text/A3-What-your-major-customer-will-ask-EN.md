PRELIMINARY NOTE
Purpose and structure
This handbook is addressed to the management of small and medium-sized
companies that have received from a customer a questionnaire on
information security, a contract clause or a request for evidence — without
an IT department of their own, without a security officer and without
anyone in the building who has ever answered such a thing before. The
customer refers to NIS2, to DORA or to the Cyber Resilience Act. The reader
of this handbook is the supplier, not the entity covered by the law.
It answers four questions, in this order: Why does your company of all companies receive this requirement, although it does
not itself fall under the law? What exactly is being asked, and where do the questions come from? What is a usable answer —
and why is a "yes" without foundation more expensive than an honest "not yet, by the end of the quarter"? And what do you
write down beforehand so that the next request no longer costs you a weekend?
The order has been chosen deliberately. Accounts of this subject frequently begin with the obligations of the entities
covered and thereby lose the reader they actually mean: the company that does not have the obligation and receives the
requirement anyway. The legal part comes first here, but only as far as it explains the mechanism; the answer is the centre.
Structure
The handbook is divided into four parts. Part I explains why you are being asked: the supply chain mechanism in the NIS2
Directive, the special cases of a financial customer and a manufacturer customer, and — only as a signpost — the question
whether your own company might be covered. Part II shows what is asked: the topics of the questionnaires, the contract
clauses, the evidence, and the registers for which your customer needs your master data. Part III says what a usable answer
is, which minimum every answer rests on, and what remains a matter for negotiation. Part IV contains a self-assessment, the
collected uncertainties, the glossary and the list of sources.
Scope
Three legal acts of the European Union govern: Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2), Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (DORA) and
Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 (the Cyber Resilience Act). The two regulations apply directly in all Member States. The directive
is transposed by each Member State into its own law; this handbook takes the Austrian transposition, the Network and
Information System Security Act 2026 (NISG 2026), as its example and marks every Austrian passage as such. For companies
in other Member States: the mechanism is the same, the deadlines and authorities are not. Where something applies only to
Austria, that is stated; the transposition of other States is not presented. Switzerland is not the subject of this handbook.
Handling of sources
Every time limit and every legal statement is supported by a source and an access date. Source lines appear immediately
beneath the statement in question; the complete list is found in Section 16. Verbatim quotations are set as such and carry
their reference.
Where a statement could not be substantiated, this is expressly noted — as not determinable, with the reason given, and not
as an estimate. These points are collected in Section 14. Two limitations concern the whole handbook and are therefore
stated here. First, the Union texts were read for this handbook from copies of the Official Journal that are not official; the
reading against the official text is still outstanding. Second, the sections of the Austrian act that contain the catalogue of
obligations of covered entities could not be read in their wording; for that catalogue the handbook therefore relies on the
directive itself and says, at every point where the Austrian act is meant but was not read, that this is so. Third, and specific to
this English edition: the official English wording of the NIS2 Directive, of DORA and of the Implementing Regulation could be
confirmed only through search excerpts; quotations from those acts are given in that wording and are marked accordingly in
Section 14.
A distinction is drawn throughout between what a law requires of your customer, what an authority or chamber
recommends, and what is customary in practice. A questionnaire is practice. It is not a statutory command addressed to you.

DELIMITATION
This handbook is a guide. It is not a legally binding document, is no substitute for legal advice and is not
a legal commentary. It does not examine whether your own company falls under a law; instead it names
the questions you have to ask yourself and the place where the answer is found. It contains no model
questionnaire and no model answers; that is the task of Scutoria’s supplier answer kit. And at no point
does it say "you must" where in truth only your customer is asking for something — because precisely
that distinction is the reason it was written.

SECTION 1
Three laws, one mechanism
The questionnaire does not arrive because your customer distrusts you,
and not because a law obliges you to anything. It arrives because your
customer has to demonstrate something they cannot demonstrate without
you: that they know the security risks emanating from their suppliers, and
that they manage them. This section shows the mechanism using the NIS2
Directive, because it is formulated most clearly there. The two other legal
acts — DORA for financial customers, the Cyber Resilience Act for
manufacturer customers — follow the same pattern and are dealt with in
Sections 3 and 4.
1.1 Whom the directive addresses — and whom not
Directive (EU) 2022/2555 addresses the Member States and, through them, "essential and important entities". These are
companies and organisations from certain sectors above a certain size, plus some kinds of entities regardless of their size.
Which ones is the subject of Section 2. For this section one sentence suffices: as a rule, your customer is such an entity, and
you are not.
The central provision is Article 21. Its first paragraph reads:
"Member States shall ensure that essential and important entities take appropriate and proportionate
technical, operational and organisational measures to manage the risks posed to the security of
network and information systems which those entities use for their operations or for the provision of
their services, and to prevent or minimise the impact of incidents on recipients of their services and on
other services."
Quelle: Article 21(1), first subparagraph, of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 14 December 2022, OJ L 333, 27.12.2022, p.
80, eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022L2555 (accessed 14 September 2026; wording from a copy of the Official Journal and
search excerpts, Appendix Section 14 nos. 1 and 18).
The addressee of every sentence is the entity. Nowhere in Article 21 is there a sentence that obliges a supplier of that entity.
What you receive is the effect of someone else’s obligation — and that is the first sentence you should keep in mind for every
conversation with your customer: the requirement has an addressee, and it is not you.
1.2 The ten measures, and the one that concerns you
Paragraph 2 lists what the entity’s measures must "at least" include. There are ten points, and they read like the table of
contents of every questionnaire you will ever receive: policies on risk analysis and information system security (a); incident
handling (b); business continuity, such as backup management and disaster recovery, and crisis management (c); supply
chain security (d); security in the acquisition, development and maintenance of network and information systems, including
vulnerability handling and disclosure (e); policies and procedures to assess the effectiveness of the measures (f); basic
cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training (g); policies and procedures regarding the use of cryptography and,
where appropriate, encryption (h); human resources security, access control policies and asset management (i); and the
use of multi-factor authentication or continuous authentication solutions, secured communications and, where
appropriate, secured emergency communication systems (j). Point (d) reads in full:
"supply chain security, including security-related aspects concerning the relationships between each
entity and its direct suppliers or service providers;"
Quelle: Article 21(2)(a) to (j) of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; points (a), (b), (c), (d) and (i) confirmed verbatim
from search excerpts, the others rendered in substance — Section 14 no. 18).

<!-- Seite 7 -->
Point (d) is the reason for this handbook. "Supply chain security" means: the entity has to treat the relationship with its
direct suppliers — that is, with you, if you supply it directly — as a security matter. The other nine points are the reason for
what is asked: if the entity itself has to demonstrate backups, access control, training and incident handling, it wants to
know whether its supplier has a hole in the same places. A customer who asks you about backups, accounts and an
emergency plan is essentially asking you points (c), (i) and (b) of their own catalogue of obligations.
THE MECHANISM IN ONE SENTENCE
Your customer has to be able to do ten things and prove them. One of them is knowing you. The other
nine determine what they want to know about you.
1.3 What the entity has to know about you
Paragraph 3 says what the entity has to consider with regard to the supply chain: when considering which supply chain
measures are appropriate, entities are to
"take into account the vulnerabilities specific to each direct supplier and service provider and the overall
quality of products and cybersecurity practices of their suppliers and service providers, including their
secure development procedures".
Quelle: Article 21(3), first sentence, of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; wording confirmed from search excerpts,
Section 14 no. 18).
Three things are contained in it, and from each follows a form of request you will come to know.
First, the "vulnerabilities specific to each direct supplier and service provider". The entity has to know them — per supplier,
not in general. Hence the questionnaire: it is the tool with which a purchasing department records a hundred suppliers one
by one.
Second, the "overall quality of products and cybersecurity practices". The entity has to assess how you work, not only what
you deliver. Hence the questions about written security rules, about updates, about the person responsible.
Third, "including their secure development procedures". That concerns software houses and suppliers of devices: the entity
wants to know whether what you develop is developed securely. Hence the questions about code review, about third-party
components, about the handling of reported vulnerabilities.
And proportionality? It is in paragraph 1, but it applies to the entity, not to you: when assessing the proportionality of the
measures, due account is to be taken of the degree of the entity’s exposure to risks, the entity’s size and the likelihood of
occurrence of incidents and their severity, including their societal and economic impact.
Quelle: Article 21(1), second subparagraph, of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; German wording read in the copy,
rendered here in substance — Section 14 no. 18).
The yardstick is therefore the size and the risk of the entity. A hospital may ask more of its maintenance contractor than a
logistics company of its cleaning service — not because the maintenance contractor matters more, but because the
customer carries a different risk. That is also the explanation for why two customers of the same size can send you very
different questionnaires.
1.4 The contract as a tool: what the directive "encourages"
The directive does not say how the entity is to secure its supply chain. It names no form, no questionnaire and no certificate.
But its recitals give a direction, and that direction is the reason for the clauses in your contracts: essential and important
entities
"should in particular be encouraged to incorporate cybersecurity risk-management measures into
contractual arrangements with their direct suppliers and service providers".
Those entities could also consider risks stemming from other levels of suppliers and service providers.
Quelle: Recital 85 of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; the quoted sentence confirmed from search excerpts, the
following sentence rendered in substance from the German copy, which at this point shows a visible transcription error to be checked against the Official Journal
— Section 14 nos. 1 and 18).

<!-- Seite 8 -->
A recital is not a legal rule; it gives the reasons for what the articles order. But it shows where the practice of writing security
requirements into purchasing conditions comes from: the legislator expressly encouraged it. And the second sentence
shows why you are also asked about your suppliers — the "other levels". For your customer, your subcontractors are the
second level of their supply chain.
For the digital providers to which a Commission Implementing Regulation applies, the "should be encouraged" has become a
list: eight points that belong in the contract with the provider. Section 6 deals with them. They are the most precise evidence
of what a major customer should write into the contract with its supplier — and thus the best indication of what will be put in
front of you.
1.5 Two sentences that explain the rest
Two further passages of the directive explain why customers are unyielding on certain questions.
The first concerns incidents. Article 23 obliges the entity to notify significant incidents within short time limits — and to
inform the recipients of its services. Where appropriate, the entities concerned inform the recipients of their services
without undue delay of significant incidents that are likely to adversely affect the provision of those services.
Quelle: Article 23(1), second sentence, of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; German wording read in the copy,
rendered in substance). The entity’s notification deadlines — an early warning within 24 hours, an incident notification within 72 hours — are in Article 23(4); for
Austria in § 34(2) NISG 2026 (Section 10.2).
An entity that itself has to issue an early warning within 24 hours can only do so if its service provider informs it immediately
when something happens on the provider’s side that touches the entity’s systems. That is the reason for the clause you will
see most often: the duty to report incidents without undue delay. It is not harassment. It is the only way for your customer to
keep their own deadline.
The second passage concerns corrections. If an entity finds that it does not comply with its measures, it has to take, without
undue delay, all necessary, appropriate and proportionate corrective measures.
Quelle: Article 21(4) of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance from the German copy).
So if an entity learns that a supplier does not deliver what they ticked in the questionnaire, it is obliged to correct — and the
obvious correction is to change the supplier. That is the sober reason why Section 9 places so much weight on the honest
answer: a "yes" that is not true is, for the customer, a deficiency they must remedy.
1.6 What the chambers observe
The Austrian Federal Economic Chamber describes the practice on a page of its own on supply chain security: service
providers and suppliers of affected entities are being "contractually obliged to risk-management measures"; entities are
"already now, in preparation, demanding corresponding evidence" or writing requirements "into their contracts and
purchasing conditions"; suppliers are "indirectly affected".
Quelle: Wirtschaftskammer Österreich, "Die Sicherheit der Lieferkette im NISG 2026", wko.at/it-sicherheit/sicherheit-lieferkette-nis2 (accessed 14 September
2026, readable only through a search excerpt; chamber page, not a legal source; German original, publisher’s translation; Section 14 no. 9).
"Indirectly affected" is the phrase that carries this handbook. It says both things: you are not the addressee of the law — and
you receive the requirement anyway. The rest of this handbook deals with what follows from that in practice.
NOT LIKE THIS
A supplier reads the clause "The contractor undertakes to comply with the NIS2 Directive", searches the
web for "NIS2 obligations", finds the list of the ten measures and the amount of the fines — and out of
worry signs everything put in front of them. The clause in truth means the customer’s obligations; the
fines fall on the entity, not on its supplier; and what the supplier can promise depends on its size. The
right reaction is to ask what the customer concretely needs (Section 12), not to sign.

SECTION 2
Whether you are covered yourself — a signpost
Before you answer a questionnaire, you should have settled one question
for yourself: might my company itself be an essential or important entity?
The answer changes everything — whoever is covered has obligations of
their own and needs a different book. This handbook does not examine the
question; that would be a legal consequence for an individual company that
no guide can determine. It does show which building blocks the answer
consists of, so that you know what to look for. Scutoria’s free NIS2 self-
check (scutoria.com/werkzeuge/nis2) leads through the same building
blocks with a reference under every statement; it is likewise no substitute
for a legal examination, but it says what remains open.
2.1 Building block one: the sector
The directive covers entities of the kinds listed in its Annexes I and II. Annex I is headed "Sectors of high criticality" and
contains eleven sectors, Annex II "Other critical sectors" with seven. Austria adopts both as Annex 1 and Annex 2 to the NISG
2026 and replaces the references to Union law with Austrian statutes wherever a national equivalent exists.
Quelle: Annexes I and II to Directive (EU) 2022/2555, eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022L2555 (accessed 10 September 2026) · §
2 and Annexes 1 and 2 NISG 2026, Federal Law Gazette (BGBl.) I No. 94/2025, ris.bka.gv.at/Dokumente/BgblAuth/BGBLA_2025_I_94/BGBLA_2025_I_94.html
(accessed 10 September 2026).
The eighteen sectors, in the order of the Austrian act: energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructures, health,
drinking water, waste water, digital infrastructure, ICT service management (business-to-business), public administration,
space (Annex 1); postal and courier services, waste management, manufacture, production and distribution of chemicals,
production, processing and distribution of food, manufacturing, digital providers, research (Annex 2).
Quelle: § 2 NISG 2026, reference as above (accessed 10 September 2026).
What matters is not the name of the industry but the type of entity in the third column of the annexes. A food business is
covered only if it is engaged in wholesale distribution and industrial production and processing; gastronomy and retail are
not named. A waste business is exempt if waste management is not its principal economic activity. And in the sector "ICT
service management" stands the entry that is closest to the readers of this handbook: providers of managed services
(managed service providers, MSP), "except in those cases where these services are operated and used exclusively by the
affected entity".
Quelle: Annex 1 no. 9 NISG 2026, BGBl. I No. 94/2025,
ris.bka.gv.at/Dokumente/BgblAuth/BGBLA_2025_I_94/Anlagen_0001_EAE421E2_DE4E_4B71_BD36_81BFBE39AF53.pdf (accessed 10 September 2026).
German original, publisher’s translation.
Whoever runs IT operations for other companies — system houses, IT service providers that look after their customers'
servers, networks or workstations — belongs to this type of entity. A company’s own IT department does not. The right
question is therefore not "Do we have IT?" but "Do we operate IT or security services for others?".
2.2 Building block two: size
For most types of entity, size decides. Austria formulates the thresholds thus: an entity is deemed a "medium-sized
enterprise" if it employs at least 50 staff, or if it has an annual turnover of more than ten million euros and an annual balance
sheet total of more than ten million euros, unless it already counts as a large enterprise.
Quelle: § 25(3) NISG 2026, BGBl. I No. 94/2025, ris.bka.gv.at/eli/bgbl/I/2025/94 (accessed 10 September 2026). German original, publisher’s translation. Large
enterprise under § 25(2): at least 250 staff, or annual turnover above 50 million euros and balance sheet total above 43 million euros.
Headcount acts alone; turnover and balance sheet total only together — that follows from the "or" and the "and" in the
wording. The calculation follows the rules of Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC: on the basis of the latest
accounts, in annual work units, apprentices are not counted, and a change of size class only occurs when the threshold is
exceeded or fallen below in two consecutive financial years.
Quelle: § 25(1) NISG 2026 in conjunction with Articles 2, 4 and 5 of the Annex to Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC of 6 May 2003, OJ L 124, 20.5.2003, p.
36, eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32003H0361 (accessed 10 September 2026).

An entity from Annex 1 that operates a large enterprise is essential; an entity from Annex 1 or 2 that operates a large or
medium-sized enterprise is important, unless it is already essential.
Quelle: § 24(1) no. 3 and (2) no. 1 NISG 2026, reference as above (accessed 10 September 2026).
For the readers of this handbook that means, as a rule: whoever has fewer than 50 employees and stays below ten million
euros in turnover or balance sheet total is not covered by size — and is asked anyway, because their customer is covered.
That is precisely the situation Section 1 describes.
2.3 Building block three: the cases without a size threshold
Some types of entity are covered regardless of their size. Deemed essential "regardless of the size of the undertaking" are
qualified trust service providers, top-level domain name registries, DNS service providers, federal public administration
entities and entities identified as critical entities under the directive on the resilience of critical entities; deemed important
regardless of size are providers of public electronic communications networks or services and trust service providers.
Quelle: § 24(1) no. 1 and (2) no. 3 NISG 2026, reference as above (accessed 10 September 2026). German original, publisher’s translation.
Added to this is a case that no company can determine itself: the cybersecurity authority shall classify an entity from Annex 1
or 2 that would not be covered because of its size by administrative decision, for instance if it is the sole provider of an
indispensable service in Austria or if a disruption could have a significant impact on public order, public security or public
health.
Quelle: § 26(1) and (3) NISG 2026, reference as above (accessed 10 September 2026).
2.4 What a form cannot decide
Three questions remain open even after the building blocks, and any guide that answers them smoothly overestimates itself:
The group question. The data of partner or linked enterprises are in principle to be added — but not if the entity is
"organisationally, technically and operationally independent" of them with regard to its network and information systems.
That is an assessment, not an arithmetic operation.
Quelle: § 25(4) NISG 2026, reference as above (accessed 10 September 2026). German original, publisher’s translation.
The main establishment in the case of digital services. For cloud, data centre, content delivery, MSP, MSSP, marketplace,
search engine and platform services, what matters is where the main establishment is, and that is the Member State in
which the decisions on the risk-management measures are predominantly taken — not necessarily the registered office.
Quelle: § 28(2) no. 2 and (3) NISG 2026, reference as above (accessed 10 September 2026).
The administrative decision under § 26. It is issued by the authority; a company cannot anticipate it.
Whoever arrives at "probably not covered" after the building blocks is in the situation of the readers of this handbook.
Whoever arrives at "possibly covered" needs more than a handbook — and should clarify the result with a professional or
legal adviser before deadlines start running.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR A COMPANY WITH TWELVE EMPLOYEES
An installation company with twelve employees maintains the building services of a hospital. The
company is not covered by size; the hospital is. The hospital has to register by early 2027 and declare by
early 2028 which measures it has implemented — among them how it deals with its suppliers. So the
questionnaire to the installation company does not come because it did anything wrong, but because
the hospital has a date. Whoever knows that can answer more calmly: the customer needs a true, dated
answer they can file. They do not need a miracle.
2.6 Other Member States
The directive was to be transposed by 17 October 2024; Austria brought the substantive part into force on 1 October 2026,
around two years later. Other Member States have transposed earlier or differently, with their own authorities, their own
deadlines and in part their own categories of entities. For Germany, the sources of this handbook contain only a secondary
source according to which a transposition act has been in force since December 2025 and uses its own terms; it was not
checked against the Federal Law Gazette (Section 14 no. 6). For all other States the state of transposition was not surveyed.
Quelle: Article 41(1) of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (transposition deadline), reference as in Section 1 (accessed 10 September 2026). Germany: secondary source,
not checked against the primary text (Section 14 no. 6).
For the mechanism of this handbook that plays no role: Article 21 of the directive applies in every transposition, and the
questionnaire of your German, Italian or Dutch customer asks the same ten points. What differs are the customer’s deadlines
— and thus the moment at which they ask you.
SECTION 3
If your customer is a financial entity
Whoever supplies software, hosting, maintenance, operations or other
digital services to a bank, an insurer, an investment firm, a payment service
provider or an insurance intermediary receives their questionnaires and
clauses not from NIS2 but from Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 on digital
operational resilience for the financial sector, DORA for short. It has applied
directly in all Member States since 17 January 2025, and it is more precise

<!-- Seite 12 -->
than the NIS2 Directive: it says in words what must be in every contract.
This section explains who is covered, which obligations fall on the
customer, and what of that arrives at your door. Section 6 deals with the
contract clauses in detail.
Quelle: Article 64 of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 14 December 2022, OJ L 333, 27.12.2022, p. 1, eur-
lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2554 (accessed 14 September 2026; wording from a copy of the Official Journal and search
excerpts, Section 14 nos. 1 and 18): it applies from 17 January 2025.
3.1 Who is a "financial entity"
Article 2 lists twenty-one kinds of undertakings to which the regulation applies: credit institutions, payment institutions,
account information service providers, electronic money institutions, investment firms, crypto-asset service providers,
central securities depositories, central counterparties, trading venues, trade repositories, managers of alternative
investment funds, management companies, data reporting service providers, insurance and reinsurance undertakings,
insurance intermediaries, reinsurance intermediaries and ancillary insurance intermediaries, institutions for occupational
retirement provision, credit rating agencies, administrators of critical benchmarks, crowdfunding service providers,
securitisation repositories — and, as the last point, "ICT third-party service providers".
Quelle: Article 2(1)(a) to (u) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
The circle is wider than the words "financial sector" suggest. An insurance broker with five employees is a financial entity
within the meaning of the regulation; its software supplier is its ICT third-party service provider.
3.2 Who is an "ICT third-party service provider"
The definition is so short that it covers almost everyone:
"'ICT third-party service provider' means an undertaking providing ICT services"
Quelle: Article 3(19) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; wording confirmed from search excerpts, Section 14 no.
18).
And ICT services are:
"digital and data services provided through ICT systems to one or more internal or external users on an
ongoing basis, including hardware as a service and hardware services which includes the provision of
technical support via software or firmware updates by the hardware provider, excluding traditional
analogue telephone services"
Quelle: Article 3(21) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; wording confirmed from search excerpts, Section 14 no.
18).
There is no size threshold. A sole trader who looks after a regional bank’s website, a two-person software house with a
specialist application for insurance brokers, a system house that maintains a payment service provider’s workstations: all are
ICT third-party service providers. Obligations of their own under the regulation fall on them essentially only if the European
supervisory authorities designate them as critical ICT third-party service providers — that concerns large providers and is not
the subject of this handbook. The small service provider feels DORA through the contracts its financial customer has to
shape.
In Austria, the NISG 2026 makes two things clear: in relation to entities within the scope of DORA, DORA’s provisions prevail;
and ICT third-party service providers within the meaning of the regulation are also subject to the NISG 2026 — which
changes nothing for a service provider below the size threshold, but can mean both sets of rules for a larger system house.
Quelle: § 24(7) and (8) NISG 2026, BGBl. I No. 94/2025, ris.bka.gv.at/eli/bgbl/I/2025/94 (accessed 10 September 2026).
3.3 The principle: your customer remains responsible
The core of Article 28 is one sentence that explains every clause that seems one-sided to you: financial entities remain at all
times fully responsible for compliance with, and the discharge of, all obligations under the regulation and applicable financial
services law.
Quelle: Article 28(1)(a) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance from the German copy).

<!-- Seite 13 -->
Outsourcing relieves the customer of nothing. So if they write audit rights, reporting duties, return obligations and
termination rights into your contract, they do it because towards their supervisor they answer for your performance as if it
were their own. Proportionality is expressly provided for: the regulation requires ICT third-party risk to be managed with due
consideration of the nature, scale, complexity and importance of ICT-related dependencies.
Quelle: Article 28(1)(b) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance).
3.4 Before the contract: due diligence and standards
Before a financial entity enters into a contract on ICT services, it has to do five things. It has to assess whether the contract
concerns the use of ICT services supporting a critical or important function; whether the supervisory conditions for
contracting are met; identify and assess all relevant risks of the arrangement, including concentration risk; "undertake all
due diligence on prospective ICT third-party service providers and ensure throughout the selection and assessment
processes that the ICT third-party service provider is suitable"; and identify and assess conflicts of interest.
Quelle: Article 28(4)(a) to (e) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; point (d) confirmed verbatim from search
excerpts, the rest rendered in substance — Section 14 no. 18).
Point (d) is the questionnaire. "Due diligence" and "suitable" can only be demonstrated to a supervisory authority with
documents — and the documents come from you. Added to this is a requirement as to quality:
"Financial entities may only enter into contractual arrangements with ICT third-party service providers
that comply with appropriate information security standards. When those contractual arrangements
concern critical or important functions, financial entities shall, prior to concluding the arrangements,
take due consideration of the use, by ICT third-party service providers, of the most up-to-date and
highest quality information security standards."
Quelle: Article 28(5) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; wording confirmed from search excerpts, Section 14 no.
18).
"Appropriate standards" is not a norm and not a certificate; which standard is appropriate, the customer decides according
to their risk. For critical or important functions the yardstick becomes stricter — and that brings you to the question you
should ask first.
3.5 The first question back: critical or important?
The regulation distinguishes throughout between ICT services supporting a "critical or important function" and all others. A
critical or important function is a function the disruption of which would materially impair the financial performance of a
financial entity, or the soundness or continuity of its services and activities, or the discontinued, defective or failed
performance of which would materially impair the continuing compliance of a financial entity with the conditions and
obligations of its authorisation.
Quelle: Article 3(22) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; first half confirmed from search excerpts, remainder
rendered in substance from the German copy — Section 14 no. 18).
Whether your service supports such a function, the customer decides — that is the assessment under Article 28(4)(a). On
that classification depends whether you receive the nine minimum clauses of Article 30(2) or additionally the six of
paragraph 3, among them unrestricted audit rights and exit strategies (Section 6). The first usable answer to a DORA
questionnaire is therefore not an answer but a question: Do you classify our service as supporting a critical or important
function? The answer determines the scope of everything that follows.
RULE OF THUMB
The service provider does not decide whether it is critical. But it may ask — and it should, before filling in
a questionnaire designed for critical functions.
3.6 After the contract: register, monitoring, terminability, exit
Four further obligations of the customer explain the rest of what you will see.
The register of information. Financial entities

<!-- Seite 14 -->
"maintain and update at entity level, and at sub-consolidated and consolidated levels, a register of
information in relation to all contractual arrangements on the use of ICT services provided by ICT third-
party service providers",
distinguish in it between critical and other functions, report at least yearly to the authority and make the register available on
request.
Quelle: Article 28(3), first to fourth subparagraphs, of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; quoted part confirmed
from search excerpts, Section 14 no. 18).
Hence the master data request: legal entity, seat, kind of service, subcontractors, locations. Section 8 deals with what
belongs in that register.
Monitoring. The customer determines, on a risk-based approach and in advance, the frequency of audits and inspections
and the areas to be audited.
Quelle: Article 28(6) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance from the German copy).
That is the basis of the audit rights — and at the same time their limit: the frequency is to be determined in advance on a risk
basis, not at will.
Terminability. The customer has to ensure that the contract may be terminated, among other things, in the event of a
significant breach by the provider of applicable laws, regulations or contractual terms; in circumstances identified
throughout monitoring that are deemed capable of altering the performance of the functions, "including material changes
that affect the arrangement or the situation of the ICT third-party service provider"; and in the event of the provider’s
"evidenced weaknesses pertaining to its overall ICT risk management".
Quelle: Article 28(7)(a) to (c) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; wording confirmed from search excerpts, Section
14 no. 18).
The exit strategy. For ICT services supporting critical or important functions, "financial entities shall put in place exit
strategies", and they must be able to transfer services and data securely and completely to alternative providers or bring
them back in-house.
Quelle: Article 28(8) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; first sentence confirmed from search excerpts, remainder
rendered in substance).
Hence the return and transition clauses that often seem one-sided to small service providers. They are — but not because
the customer wants it so, rather because they must.
3.7 Concentration and subcontracting
Article 29 obliges the customer to consider, before contracting, whether it is contracting an ICT third-party service provider
"that is not easily substitutable", or having in place multiple contractual arrangements for critical or important functions with
the same provider or with closely connected providers. Where subcontracting is envisaged, benefits and risks are to be
weighed, in particular where the subcontractor is established in a third country, and it is to be assessed how potentially long
or complex chains of subcontracting affect the ability to monitor.
Quelle: Article 29(1) and (2) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; paragraph 1 confirmed from search excerpts,
paragraph 2 rendered in substance). According to secondary sources, Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/532 specifies the requirements on
subcontracting for critical functions; its wording was not read (Section 14 no. 7).
For you this means: you will be asked whom you yourself use — hosting, data centre, software suppliers, freelancers — and
where. A service provider that cannot say counts as hard to monitor. The supplier list of Section 11 is therefore not an extra
for financial customers, but a precondition.
3.8 What DORA is not for you
DORA imposes on you as a small service provider no registration of your own, no reporting duty of your own and no audit of
your own. Everything you receive comes through the contract. That has a practical consequence: what is in the contract is
negotiable — within what the customer must at least write into it under Article 30. Where that boundary runs and what goes
beyond it, Section 12 shows. And the regulation itself encourages both sides to consider the use of standard contractual
clauses developed by public authorities for specific services.
Quelle: Article 30(4) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance). The Austrian Financial Market
Authority and the German financial supervisor have published aids on contract design; their content could be read only by title (Section 14 no. 8).

SECTION 4
If your customer manufactures products with digital elements
Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, the Cyber Resilience Act, is the third set of
rules that questionnaires invoke. It is addressed to manufacturers,
importers and distributors of "products with digital elements" — software
and connected hardware. A company that buys and uses such products has
no obligations under it. It notices the act nonetheless, at three points that
this section takes in turn. The quotations in this section are in the official
English wording, read from a copy of the Official Journal (Section 14 nos. 1
and 3).
4.1 What a product with digital elements is
"'product with digital elements' means a software or hardware product and its remote data processing
solutions, including software or hardware components being placed on the market separately;"
Quelle: Article 3(1) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 October 2024, OJ L, 20.11.2024, eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-
content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R2847 (accessed 14 September 2026; wording from a copy of the Official Journal, Section 14 no. 1).
The regulation applies to such products "the intended purpose or reasonably foreseeable use of which includes a direct or
indirect logical or physical data connection to a device or network" — that is, to everything that can be connected:
controllers, routers, sensors, apps, operating systems, specialist software, including separately sold components of them.
Excluded are, among others, medical devices, motor vehicles, aviation and marine equipment, for which rules of their own
apply.
Quelle: Article 2(1) to (7) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
4.2 Who has obligations: the economic operator
Obligations fall on "economic operators" — manufacturers, authorised representatives, importers and distributors. The
manufacturer is whoever develops or manufactures a product or has it designed, developed or manufactured, "and markets
them under its name or trademark, whether for payment, monetisation or free of charge". The distributor is whoever makes a
product available on the Union market "without affecting its properties". And "making available on the market" means "the
supply of a product with digital elements for distribution or use on the Union market in the course of a commercial activity,
whether in return for payment or free of charge".
Quelle: Article 3(12), (13), (19), (22) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
The converse is the most important sentence of this section for the readers of this handbook: whoever buys a product and
uses it in their own business makes nothing available on the market and is not an economic operator. According to its
recitals, the regulation applies to economic operators "only in relation to products with digital elements made available on
the market, hence supplied for distribution or use on the Union market in the course of a commercial activity". For public
administration it says expressly that products developed or modified "exclusively for its own use" are not to be considered
made available; whether it says the same expressly for private companies with in-house developments, the text does not
state — it follows from the definition of making available, but is to be marked as interpretation and checked in case of doubt
(Section 14 no. 10).
Quelle: Recitals of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 on the scope in respect of economic operators and on public administration, reference as above (accessed 14
September 2026); the numbers of the recitals could not be taken from the copy (Section 14 no. 3).
4.3 Three ways a small company feels the CRA
As a distributor. Whoever resells devices or software — including as a sideline, such as an electrical contractor who supplies
routers and controllers, or a system house that passes on licences — is a distributor. Distributors have to "act with due care"
and, before making a product available, verify

<!-- Seite 16 -->
"that: (a) the product with digital elements bears the CE marking; (b) the manufacturer and the importer
have complied with the obligations set out in Article 13(15), (16), (18), (19) and (20) and Article 19(4), and
have provided all necessary documents to the distributor."
Quelle: Article 20(1) and (2) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
Upon becoming aware of a vulnerability in the product, a distributor has to inform the manufacturer "without undue delay".
Quelle: Article 20(4), second subparagraph, of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
As a manufacturer, without noticing. An importer or distributor is considered a manufacturer "where that importer or
distributor places a product with digital elements on the market under its name or trademark or carries out a substantial
modification of a product with digital elements already placed on the market". The same applies to anyone else who
substantially modifies a product and passes it on.
Quelle: Articles 21 and 22(1) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
That concerns companies that sell devices under their own name that someone else built, or have an app offered under their
own brand. Whoever does that should know the manufacturer’s obligations — and with that question belongs not in a
handbook for suppliers but with professional advice.
As a supplier to a manufacturer. That is the case this handbook is about. Manufacturers have to exercise diligence when
integrating purchased components:
"manufacturers shall exercise due diligence when integrating components sourced from third parties so
that those components do not compromise the cybersecurity of the product with digital elements,
including when integrating components of free and open-source software that have not been made
available on the market in the course of a commercial activity."
Quelle: Article 13(5) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
A machine builder that obtains control software from a ten-person software house therefore has to demonstrate that it
examined this component with due diligence. The corresponding recital names as indications whether the component bears
the CE marking, whether it receives "regular security updates", whether it is free of vulnerabilities registered in the European
vulnerability database, and whether additional security tests have been carried out. Hence the questions to the component
supplier: How long do you supply updates? How do you report vulnerabilities to us? Do you have a bill of materials of the
software contained?
Quelle: Recital of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 relating to Article 13(5), reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026; recital number not determined, Section
14 no. 3).
4.4 What the manufacturer customer has to do itself — and therefore wants to know from you
Four obligations of the manufacturer determine the questions to its suppliers.
Secure development. Manufacturers ensure that a product has been "designed, developed and produced in accordance
with the essential cybersecurity requirements set out in Part I of Annex I", and carry out a risk assessment to be taken into
account "during the planning, design, development, production, delivery and maintenance phases".
Quelle: Article 13(1) and (2) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
Vulnerabilities over the support period. Manufacturers ensure "for the support period" that vulnerabilities of the product,
"including its components", are handled effectively. The support period is the time during which the manufacturer has to
handle vulnerabilities; it reflects the expected time in use, and:
"Without prejudice to the second subparagraph, the support period shall be at least five years. Where
the product with digital elements is expected to be in use for less than five years, the support period
shall correspond to the expected use time."
Quelle: Article 13(8), first and third subparagraphs, and Article 3 (definition of "support period") of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14
September 2026).

<!-- Seite 17 -->
Whoever supplies a component is therefore asked whether they will provide it with security updates for at least as long as
the manufacturer has to support the end product. That is the question with the greatest economic weight in a CRA
questionnaire — and one that is not answered with "yes" without having costed it.
Notification of exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents. Manufacturers notify "any actively exploited vulnerability"
and "any severe incident having an impact on the security of the product" to the competent CSIRT and to the European
Union Agency for Cybersecurity: an early warning "within 24 hours", a notification "within 72 hours", and a final report.
Quelle: Article 14(1) to (4) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
A manufacturer that has to notify within 24 hours wants to hear from its component supplier immediately if the supplier’s
part has an exploited vulnerability. Here too, the reporting clause is not arbitrary but the condition for the customer keeping
their deadline.
Documentation. Microenterprises and small enterprises may provide the technical documentation "by using a simplified
format"; Member States are to support small manufacturers with awareness raising, training and assistance in conformity
assessment.
Quelle: Article 33(1) and (5) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
4.5 From when
"This Regulation shall apply from 11 December 2027. However, Article 14 shall apply from 11 September
2026 and Chapter IV (Articles 35 to 51) shall apply from 11 June 2026."
Quelle: Article 71(2) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, reference as above (accessed 14 September 2026).
The manufacturers' notification obligation under Article 14 therefore already applies; the other manufacturer obligations,
including diligence for components, apply from 11 December 2027. Manufacturer customers preparing their supply chain for
that are asking accordingly now. On fines this handbook deliberately says nothing except one note: according to the wording
of the copy, they do not apply to microenterprises and small enterprises "for a failure to meet the 24-hour deadline for the
early warning notification"; the exact extent of that relief could not be read in the article itself (Section 14 no. 11).
A SOFTWARE HOUSE WITH SIX EMPLOYEES
The software house supplies a data evaluation module that a manufacturer of heat pumps builds into
its control unit. The software house itself places nothing on the market under its own name; the
manufacturer is the heat pump maker. The questionnaire that arrives asks three things: how long the
module receives security updates, by what route vulnerabilities can be reported, and whether a list of
the third-party libraries contained exists. None of the three questions rests on an obligation of the
software house. All three rest on Article 13(5) and (8) of the customer — and all three decide whether
the module is still built into the next product.

The questionnaire: fourteen recurring topics
No law and no authority has published a questionnaire for suppliers. For this

Sicheres Österreich, from which the label "Cyber Trust Austria" derives (Section 7.3).
Quelle: BSI, "CyberRisikoCheck nach DIN SPEC 27076", bsi.bund.de (accessed 14 September 2026, search excerpt; in detail Volume 1, Section 12.2, accessed 5

5.3 What the questions really want to know
Behind the fourteen topics stand four worries of the customer, and knowing them helps when answering.
Can an attacker reach me through you? That is the worry behind access, malware, network, updates and cloud. The
customer knows that attacks run through service providers — the directive expressly justifies the supply chain obligation
with the fact that incidents in which entities have been the victim of cyberattacks through vulnerabilities affecting third-
party products and services have become more frequent. They are not asking whether you are perfect, but whether the
ground rules that apply to them also apply to you.
Quelle: Recital 85 of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, reference as in Section 1 (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance from the German copy).

and it is the most urgent, because the customer’s own notification deadlines hang on it (Section 1.5).
Will I stay operational if you fail? That is the worry behind business continuity, backup, termination and return of data. For
financial customers it is anchored in the exit strategy (Section 3.6).
Can I show this to my supervisor? That is the worry behind evidence, certificates and audit rights. The customer has to
demonstrate its diligence; a certificate is, for them, a piece of evidence they can file without an examination of their own.
Whether it is the only one is dealt with in Section 7.

5.4 How to recognise a poor questionnaire
Not every questionnaire is well thought out. Some are sent to all suppliers alike, from the data centre operator to the florist.
Three signs that a form is not tailored to your service:
It asks about things that have nothing to do with your service — for instance about securing development processes at a
company that develops no software. The right answer is then "not applicable" with a reason (Section 9.3), not a strained
"yes".
It demands certificates regardless of size and risk. The regulation for financial entities requires "appropriate information
security standards", the directive the consideration of proportionality according to the entity’s risk and size — both allow the
customer to ask something different of a small supplier than of a data centre. Whether they do is their decision; whether you
ask, yours.
Quelle: Article 28(5) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554; Article 21(1), second subparagraph, of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, references as in Sections 1 and 3 (accessed
14 September 2026).
It leaves no room for "partly" or "by when". A form that knows only yes and no forces answers that are not true. Then a
covering letter belongs with it that puts the answers in context (Section 9).

6.6 Reading clauses: an order
Whoever receives a contract with security clauses can classify it in four steps before asking a lawyer — and afterwards talk to
the lawyer better:
First: which set of rules does the customer come from? Financial entity — then Article 30 is the yardstick. Digital provider —
then the Annex to the Implementing Regulation. Other covered entity — then Article 21 of the directive without a binding list
of clauses. Manufacturer customer — then Article 13 of the Cyber Resilience Act.
Second: which clause corresponds to a point on the list, and which goes beyond it? What is on the list the customer must
agree; the form is negotiable. What is not on the list is the customer’s wish.
Third: which clause requires a deadline you must be able to keep? Reporting deadlines, notice periods, return deadlines. A
promised deadline is a promise.
Fourth: which clause requires something you do not have today? That is the list for Section 9 — partly, by when.
NOT LIKE THIS
A service provider strikes out the reporting clause because "without undue delay" is too vague for
them, and sends the contract back. The customer cannot strike the clause: for financial entities and for
the digital providers under the Implementing Regulation it is mandatory. The contract does not come
about. Better would have been to keep the clause and insert the reporting route and a deadline the
company can keep — that is the answer the customer needs (Section 10.2).

7.1 What the law says on evidence — and what not
None of the three legal acts requires a certificate from a supplier. The Implementing Regulation names among the contract

7.2 The forms, ordered by effort
The supplier statement. A form filled in, dated and signed by you that answers the topics of Section 5 and names the

customers it is sufficient — above all if it has attachments that are more than assertions: a backup log with a date, an
account list, an emergency card, a supplier list. Section 9 deals with how it is built. It is not a certification and establishes no
legal consequence; that belongs in it as a sentence.
Self-declaration against a scheme. Some labels rest on a self-declaration against a fixed catalogue of criteria, which is
checked by a body and provided with a mark. The difference from the free supplier statement: the customer knows the
catalogue and does not have to read it themselves. The Austrian example is in 7.3.
The audit report. A report by an independent body — an IT service provider, an auditor, an expert — describing what was
examined and what was found. It is the form that the Implementing Regulation names as an alternative to the audit right, and
for DORA customers the form with which you can turn audit rights into something manageable (Section 12.2). A report from
the German testing procedure under DIN SPEC 27076 is an example of such a form for small companies; it is expressly not a
certification, but it has a date, an examiner and a catalogue.
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.2, with source bsi.bund.de (accessed 5 September 2026).

security ISO/IEC 27001. It is the form the customer can file most easily, and the form a small company reaches with most
difficulty: it presupposes a management system with documentation, internal audits and regular surveillance audits.

Whether it is proportionate for your size depends on how many customers demand it and what it costs; neither was surveyed
for this handbook.
The industry assessment. In the automotive supply chain the TISAX procedure under the catalogue of the German

From all of this follows an order that this handbook proposes for companies without an IT department — as a professional

SECTION 9
Three levels of answer
Most questionnaires are built so that you can tick "yes" everywhere. The
temptation to do so is great: the customer matters, the deadline is short,
and nobody checks anyway. This section explains why that is the most
expensive answer a company can give, and sets against it three answers
that hold. It is the most important section of this handbook, because it is
the only one addressed not to your customer but to you.
9.1 Why a "yes" without foundation is more expensive
A ticked "yes" is a declaration to your customer. What follows from it in civil law depends on the contract and the applicable
law and is not the subject of this handbook. But what follows from the customer's rules can be stated with a reference — and
it is reason enough.
The customer has to correct. If a covered entity finds that it does not comply with its measures, it has to take, without
undue delay, all necessary, appropriate and proportionate corrective measures. A supplier whose "yes" turns out to be false
is, for the customer, a point at which they do not comply with their measures. The obvious correction is the change of
supplier.
Quelle: Article 21(4) of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, reference as in Section 1 (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance from the German copy).
The financial customer has to be able to terminate — and may. DORA requires that contracts can be terminated, among
other things, in the event of a significant breach by the provider of applicable laws, regulations or contractual terms, and in
the event of the provider's
"evidenced weaknesses pertaining to its overall ICT risk management and in particular in the way it
ensures the availability, authenticity, integrity and, confidentiality, of data, whether personal or
otherwise sensitive data, or non-personal data".
Quelle: Article 28(7)(a) and (c) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as in Section 3 (accessed 14 September 2026; wording confirmed from search excerpts,
including the punctuation shown there — Section 14 no. 18).
A "yes" on backups that falls apart at the first incident is such an evidenced weakness. An honest "partly, tested restoration
by the end of the quarter" is not — it is a state the customer knows and can classify.
The financial customer has to be informed of developments. For critical functions, the contract includes reporting
obligations, including the notification of any development that might have a material impact on the provider’s ability to
provide the services effectively.
Quelle: Article 30(3)(b) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as in Section 3 (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance).
Whoever says "yes" today and tomorrow has a state that does not match the "yes" has such a development — and has to
report it. Whoever states the actual state from the outset has nothing to report.
And the most sober reason: the customer does not ask only once. Questionnaires come back annually, audit rights are
exercised, audit reports demanded. A "yes" has to be true the second time, and the third. An honest "partly" with a date only
has to have improved the second time.
THE SENTENCE FOR MANAGEMENT
A "yes" is a promise to a customer who has to document it, check it and, in the worst case, use it
against you. A "not yet, by then" is information they can work with.

<!-- Seite 31 -->
9.2 Level one: "We have it" — with an attachment
"Yes" is the right answer if three things are true: the state actually exists, not only in intention. It is recorded — there is a
document with a date that shows it. And you could present it to the customer on request.
The third criterion is the touchstone. A "yes" without an attachment is an assertion; a "yes" with an attachment is evidence.
Therefore every "yes" in a supplier statement should carry a reference to an attachment: "Yes — backup plan of [date], log of
the last restoration test of [date], attachment 3." Which attachments a company without an IT department can produce
itself is in Sections 10 and 11.
A "yes" may also be qualified, as long as the qualification is stated: "Yes for all workstations; for the warehouse terminal no,
because it has no connection to the outside." That is not a weakness but proof that you know your systems.
9.3 Level two: "Partly, by when"
This is the answer for everything that has begun but is not finished — and it is the most frequent honest answer of a small
company. It has three parts:
What exists: "Backups run daily to a separate drive."
What is missing: "A restoration has not yet been fully rehearsed."
By when, and who: "Rehearsal by [date], responsible [role]."
The date is not a promise into the blue but a promise you can keep — and therefore it should be set so that you keep it. A
customer who keeps a register enters the date and asks after it. Whoever keeps it has a "yes" with an attachment at the next
questionnaire. Whoever does not keep it has a development they have to report.
For the role: a function, not a personal name, at least in the copy that stays with the customer. Names change; the
responsibility stays.
9.4 Level three: "Not applicable, because"
Some questions do not concern your company. A cleaning company develops no software; a company without access to
customer systems has no remote accesses to secure; a supplier of spare parts without digital elements has no support
period. The right answer is then "not applicable" — with the reason, in one sentence.
The reason is the decisive part. "Not applicable" without a reason reads like an evasive "no"; "not applicable, because we
develop no software and introduce no third-party components into your systems" is a statement the customer can file and
adopt next time. It also shows that you understood the question.
Two warnings. First: "not applicable" is no answer to questions that concern every company — accounts, backup, updates,
reporting route. Whoever writes "not applicable" there has not understood the question or does not want to answer it, and
the customer sees both. Second: whether a question is actually not applicable sometimes depends on the customer’s
classification; the DORA points for critical functions are not applicable if the customer does not classify your service so —
and that is the question back from Section 3.5.
9.5 The supplier statement as a form
The three levels need a vessel. For companies without a procedure of their own that is the supplier statement: a document
you set up once, send along with every questionnaire and update annually. It has a fixed structure, which this handbook
describes without pre-empting it — the ready-to-fill form is part of Scutoria’s supplier answer kit.
Header. Company name, date, version, who is responsible for it (role), and one sentence that says what it is: This supplier
statement describes the state on the date given. It is not a certification and establishes no legal consequence.
Company and role. The master data from Section 8.4: what you supply, whether you have access to the customer’s
systems, where data are processed, your size class.
The topics. For each topic from Section 5, an answer at one of the three levels, with a reference to the attachment. For
companies supplying software or devices, additionally the manufacturer customer’s questions (Section 4.4); for financial
customers, additionally the locations and the return of data (Section 6.4).
List of attachments. The sheets that substantiate the answers: security rules, responsibilities, reporting route, backup log,
account list, update rule, training record, supplier list, where applicable insurance confirmation and certificates. Each
attachment with a date.

<!-- Seite 32 -->
Signature. Of the management. Not because a law requires it, but because a supplier statement is a declaration by the
company, and management should know it before it lies with the customer.
A supplier statement built like this answers most questionnaires before they arrive: the customer receives it as an
attachment, and in their form every question says "see supplier statement, section X". That spares you the repetition and
gives the customer a document they can file.
9.6 The covering letter
Some questionnaires know only yes and no. Then a short covering letter belongs with the answers, explaining the levels the
form does not know: which "yes" are qualified, which "no" are a "partly, by when", which questions are not applicable and why.
The letter is half a page, and it is the place where you ask the questions back that the form leaves open — first of all, with
financial customers, the classification of the service.
NOT LIKE THIS
A company ticks yes at "Is restoration of the backup tested regularly?" because the backup does run. A
year later, malware encrypts the servers, the backup cannot be restored, the service for the customer
is down for two weeks. The customer has to report the incident and document that their supplier had
promised a tested restoration. The right answer a year earlier would have been: "Partly — daily backup,
restoration rehearsal by [date]." It would not have prevented the incident. But it would have triggered
the rehearsal, and the rehearsal would have shown the problem.

Training, staff, subcontractors, locations, termination
The five sheets from Section 10 answer the questions on technology. A
second part of the questions is aimed at people and relationships: who
works for you, what do those people know, whom do you yourself use,
where, and what happens when the contract ends. These questions are
harder to answer because they are not in an administration console — and
they are the ones where a "yes" without foundation occurs most often.
11.1 Training: do your people know what they are doing
What the customer asks. Are your employees trained in information security? When last, who, with what content? Have
they signed a confidentiality undertaking?
Where the question comes from. Basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training are one of the customer’s ten
points; the Implementing Regulation names requirements regarding skills and training of the provider’s employees as a
contract point; DORA provides for the participation of service providers in the financial entity’s training programmes.
Quelle: Article 21(2)(g) of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 · point 5.1.4(b) of the Annex to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 · Article 30(2)(i) of Regulation (EU)
2022/2554 (all accessed 14 September 2026).
The sheet. A training record: date, topic, participants (roles in the customer copy), who trained, in one sentence what. For a
company of eight employees, one hour a year with a list of who attended is a record; a certificate per person is not, and is not
demanded either. Volume 2 of this series describes in Section 8 how such a record is kept for the use of AI; the same record
can be extended by the security topics — passwords and second factor, recognising fraudulent messages, reporting
incidents, handling customer data.

The confidentiality undertaking is in many companies part of the employment contract. Whether and how it is formulated is
an employment law question this handbook does not answer; for the questionnaire it suffices to state whether one exists
and since when.
What the sheet answers. Training and staff; part of the requirements on staff under the Implementing Regulation.
11.2 Background verification: the question you do not answer with "yes"
What the customer asks. Are persons with access to our systems or data checked for reliability?
Where the question comes from. The Implementing Regulation names requirements regarding background verification of
the suppliers' and service providers' employees as a contract point.
Quelle: Point 5.1.4(c) of the Annex to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, reference as in Section 6 (accessed 14 September 2026).
The answer. What a background verification comprises in detail, the regulation does not say; what an employer may check
about employees is governed by the employment and data protection law of the State of establishment and is not the
subject of this handbook. For the questionnaire this means: describe what you actually do — say, checking references and
certificates on recruitment, a confidentiality undertaking, access to customer systems only for named persons — and do not
write "yes" if you would thereby promise a check you neither carry out nor may carry out. A customer who demands more

What the customer asks. Which subcontractors and services do you use that touch our data — hosting, data centre,
software, freelancers, maintenance contractors? In which country? Have you agreed security requirements with them? Will

you cannot promise, and the contract should say so.
What the answer answers. Termination; for financial customers the core of the exit strategy.

SECTION 12
What is negotiable — and when "not applicable" is the right answer
What the customer must agree under Section 6 is settled; how it is agreed
is not. Between "the customer’s obligation" and "clause in the contract" lies
a margin the laws themselves grant — "where appropriate", "appropriate
standards", "proportionate" — and that margin is the place where a small
company negotiates. This section names the five points where that is worth
doing in practice, says what the legal acts give for it, and draws the line to
what a lawyer should examine. It gives no legal advice; it shows where the
questions lie.
12.1 The margin the laws leave
Three passages provide it. The directive requires of the entity measures appropriate to the risk and ties proportionality to
the degree of the entity’s exposure to risks, the entity’s size and the likelihood of occurrence of incidents and their severity.
Quelle: Article 21(1), second subparagraph, of Directive (EU) 2022/2555, reference as in Section 1 (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance).
The Implementing Regulation requires the eight contract points "where appropriate".
Quelle: Point 5.1.4 of the Annex to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, reference as in Section 6 (accessed 14 September 2026).
And DORA requires of the financial customer that it manage third-party risk according to the nature, scale, complexity and
importance of ICT-related dependencies, determine the frequency of audits in advance on a risk-based approach, and
encourages the use of standard contractual clauses developed by public authorities.
Quelle: Article 28(1)(b), (6) and Article 30(4) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, reference as in Section 3 (accessed 14 September 2026; rendered in substance).
None of these passages gives you a right to refuse anything. They give your customer the right to demand less if their risk
allows it. Negotiating therefore does not mean resisting, but proposing to the customer a form that fulfils their obligation
and that you can deliver.
12.2 Audit rights
What is demanded. The right to audit you — on site, at any time, also by the authority, with the right to copy documents.
What the law provides. For the digital providers under the Implementing Regulation the contract point is the right to audit or
the right to receive audit reports — the regulation itself names the audit report as an equivalent form. For financial
customers, for critical functions, "unrestricted rights of access, inspection and audit" are mandatory content; the only relief
applies if the customer is a microenterprise. For non-critical functions Article 30(2) requires no audit rights but cooperation
with the authorities.
Quelle: Point 5.1.4(e) of the Annex to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 · Article 30(2)(g), (3)(e) and last subparagraph of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554
(accessed 14 September 2026).
What a small company can propose. First, the question back about the classification — is the service critical? If not,
unrestricted audit rights are not mandatory content. Second, where audit rights are mandatory: conditions for their exercise
that the regulation itself suggests — frequency determined in advance, announced dates, audits during business hours,

<!-- Seite 38 -->
confidentiality towards other customers whose data are in the same systems. Third, the audit report as the rule and the on-
site audit as the exception on cause. The right itself remains; its exercise is put in order.
12.3 Background verification of staff
What is demanded. The promise to check all persons with access to customer systems — sometimes with requirements
such as a criminal record extract or a credit check.
What the law provides. The Implementing Regulation names the point but does not say what a background verification
comprises; and it stands under "where appropriate". What an employer may check is national employment and data
protection law.
Quelle: Point 5.1.4(c) of the Annex to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 (accessed 14 September 2026).
What a small company can propose. The description of what it actually does (Section 11.2), and the promise to restrict
access to customer systems to named persons who are communicated to the customer — for the customer that is often
worth more than a blanket promise to check, because they then know who has access. Whether a particular check is
permissible belongs with a lawyer.
12.4 Reporting deadlines
What is demanded. Reporting of incidents within a few hours, around the clock.
What the law provides. The customer itself has to issue an early warning within 24 hours; the Implementing Regulation
requires contractually notification "without undue delay"; DORA assistance in incidents. A particular number of hours for the
service provider stands in none of the texts.
Quelle: § 34(2) no. 1 NISG 2026 (accessed 10 September 2026) · point 5.1.4(d) of the Annex to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 · Article 30(2)(f) of
Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (accessed 14 September 2026).
What a small company can propose. A deadline it can keep, in two stages: immediately via an emergency number for
recognisably severe incidents, and within a defined time on working days for everything else. What "without undue delay"
means for a company without an on-call service can be described in the contract; a number of hours nobody can keep is of
no use to the customer, because they rely on it. And assistance in incidents may be provided "at a cost that is determined
ex-ante" — whoever does not regulate that provides it for free.
12.5 Liability, subcontractors, insurance
What is demanded. Liability for subcontractors as for one’s own acts; unlimited liability for security incidents; evidence of a
cyber insurance with a particular sum insured.
What the law provides. On liability the three legal acts say nothing that binds the service provider; they require contract
contents on subcontractors (Section 11.3), not a particular allocation of liability. On insurance they say nothing; the question
is pure practice (Section 5.2).
What a small company can propose. On liability: nothing without a lawyer — liability clauses are the point where this
handbook stops and legal review begins, because the consequences fall on the company itself and depend on national law.
On insurance: the honest statement whether one exists and what it covers, and the question to your own insurer whether
the cover includes damage at customers arising from security incidents — Volume 1 advises in Section 12.5 to put that
question in writing and keep the answer. Whether a cyber insurance makes sense for your own company depends on
customers, service and premium; a market standard is not determinable.
Quelle: Volume 1 of this series, Section 12.5 (sources there accessed 5 September 2026).

Economic operator

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847). A company that only uses products is not one.
Entity
The term of the NIS2 Directive and of the NISG 2026 for the covered companies and organisations; divided into essential and

Essential entity, important entity
The two categories of covered entities. Essential are, among others, entities of Annex 1 operating a large enterprise;

Evidence

Exit strategy
Plan of a financial entity for how it can end an ICT service for a critical or important function and transfer it to another

Financial entity
Collective term of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 for the twenty-one kinds of undertakings within its scope, from credit

ICT concentration risk
The risk of a financial entity of depending on a provider that is not easily substitutable or on multiple contracts with the same

ICT services
Digital and data services provided through ICT systems on an ongoing basis, including hardware as a service and technical
support via software or firmware updates, excluding traditional analogue telephone services (Article 3(21) of Regulation
(EU) 2022/2554).
ICT third-party service provider
An undertaking providing ICT services (Article 3(19) of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554). Without a size threshold; every IT service
provider of a financial entity.
Implementing regulation
Act of the Commission that specifies a directive or regulation for particular cases. Here: Implementing Regulation (EU)

Making available on the market
The supply of a product with digital elements for distribution or use on the Union market in the course of a commercial

uses a product does not make it available.
Manufacturer
In the Cyber Resilience Act, whoever develops or manufactures a product with digital elements, or has it designed,
developed or manufactured, and markets it under its own name or trademark (Article 3(13) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847). A
distributor or importer also becomes a manufacturer if it places a product on the market under its own name or substantially
modifies it (Articles 21, 22).

Login that requires, in addition to the password, a second, independent proof; one of the ten measure points of the covered

Register that a financial entity keeps of all contracts with ICT third-party service providers, reports to the authority yearly

The notification of a covered entity to the cybersecurity authority with name, address, sector, type of entity, Member States

Sheet of a supplier listing all services and subcontractors that touch a customer’s data: provider, country, purpose, contract

Support period

Direct supplier or service provider
The supplier who supplies a covered entity directly; subject of the supply chain measures under Article 21(2)(d) of Directive
