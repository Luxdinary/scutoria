PRELIMINARY NOTE
Purpose and structure
This guide shows how a small business sets up Claude and brings it into
everyday work without doing itself harm: which kind of access fits, who
manages it, which settings to look at before the first real task, how
recurring tasks are filed, what one piece of work looks like from the enquiry
to the checked result, and what never goes in. It is written for owners,
managing directors and office managers — for businesses from the one-
person company to the medium-sized firm without its own IT department.
This guide describes the interface as of 15 September 2026
Interfaces go stale. A menu item is renamed, a switch moves, a new kind of account appears. That is why this
guide carries a date, and why it is maintained differently from a handbook in the series: once a quarter it is
gone through, changed screenshots are replaced, the line above is moved forward. Readers using online
access see the new version immediately; readers who downloaded the PDF see it the next time they
download.
The provider’s statements — kinds of account, roles, default settings, retention, connections — come from its
own pages and each carry the page, its stated version and the retrieval date. The paths through the interface
were walked on screen and are documented with screenshots. Where a statement does not appear on the
pages checked, the guide says so; it does not fill the gap with a guess. The annex “Uncertainties” brings
together everything that remains open.
What this guide is not
Not a guide to the law. Whether your business may enter a particular document into an AI tool, which
contractual assurances you need from the provider, and what to do in the event of an incident is not decided
at the interface. Those questions belong to the handbook “AI in the Company” (Volume 1 of the series); the
rules towards staff belong to “AI Policies for the Workforce” (Volume 2). At such points this guide refers there
by section number and invents nothing. It is not legal advice.
No statement about the provider that is not on the provider’s page. Every statement about kinds of
account, use of data, retention or connections carries a source and a retrieval date. What the provider
changes applies from the day of the change — not from the day this guide catches up. For the most important
setting, what happens to your inputs, section 3 therefore says explicitly: look for yourself, today.
No substitute for a set of rules. Section 7 gives three rules for the start, no more. A full usage policy with
approval procedure, briefing and records is the subject of Volume 2 and of the AI evidence kit in this series.
This guide sets up; the kit regulates.
Structure
Part I is a single page: the four decisions that come before the first account. Part II sets up — account and
access (section 2), the settings (section 3), projects, instructions and connected folders (section 4). Part III
is everyday use: one complete piece of work (section 5), the exclusion list (section 6), the rules once staff
join (section 7), and the monthly check (section 8). The annex contains the checklist to print out (section 9),
the uncertainties (10), the glossary (11) and the sources (12).
Every setting gets three things: where it sits, what it does, why it matters to a business. The third is the
one this guide was written for.

Choice of words
Business account means, in this guide, an account that the business manages and in which several people
work — with the provider, these are the Team and Enterprise plans. Individual plan means an account of a
single person (Free, Pro, Max). Connection means Claude’s access to another service, such as a file store or
a mailbox; the provider calls this a “connector”. Project is the place for recurring tasks with permanent
instructions and stored knowledge. Interface terms are given as they appear on the provider’s pages,
because the provider’s help pages are in English and the interface may be labelled differently depending on
the language setting.
The example business
The examples in this guide refer to a fictitious joinery with eight employees: an owner, an office manager, a
master joiner, four journeymen and an apprentice. There is no IT department; the office manager looks after
the computers on the side. All screenshots show an account created specifically for this fictitious business;
no real customer, staff or business data, no names.

“business account”.

SECTION 1
Before you begin
Most businesses create an account first and think afterwards about who
may use it. That is the wrong order, and you only notice when the first file
ends up in the wrong place. Four decisions belong beforehand. Together
they take twenty minutes and save you the clean-up.
1.1 Who may use it?
Not “everyone” and not “just me, let’s see”. Write down two or three roles: the office manager and me. The
reason is practical: every further access is one more place where something can get in that does not belong
— and one more person you have to see off when they leave.
This decision determines the kind of account, and it does so already here. An individual plan is an account for
exactly one person: for the individual plans the provider lists “project sharing and collaboration”, “central
billing and administration” and “role-based access” as “No”. A business account starts at two people. So
anyone who wants to work as a pair does not choose between “an account” and “a bigger account”, but
between two separate individual accounts without shared administration and a business account. Section 2
sets this out.
Source: Anthropic, “Plans & Pricing”, German-language version, tabs “Einzelperson” (Individual) and “Team & Enterprise” (retrieved 8
September 2026), claude.com/pricing.
Whoever starts small can expand. Whoever starts wide has to gather everything back in.
1.2 What for?
Two or three concrete tasks, not a wish list. Prepare quotation texts. Summarise long enquiries. Turn minutes
into tasks.
That is not bureaucracy; it is the yardstick by which you measure in three months whether it was worth it.
Without it, only a feeling remains. Volume 1 describes in section 6 a four-week approach — decide, apply,
evaluate — that begins with exactly this list.
Source: Volume 1 “AI in the Company”, section 6 (approach to introduction), section 4.6 (getting started); reference, no new evidence.
1.3 With which data?
This is where it gets serious, and where this guide stops being an operating manual. What may go into an AI
tool and what may not is not decided at the interface but by your business and by what applies to you.
What to consider is in Volume 1, section 12.3 — where data leakage through inputs is described as a risk — and
in section 2.4, which lists the contractual points to check: data processing agreement, place of processing,
training with business data, liability for third-party rights.
Source: Volume 1 “AI in the Company”, sections 2.4 (contractual points to check) and 12.3 (AI-specific risks); reference, no new evidence.
For the start, one single line you draw for yourself is enough: What you would not send to an external
service provider without a contract does not go in here either. Section 6 of this guide turns that into a list.

<!-- Seite 7 -->
1.4 Who tidies up?
One person, named by role. Once a month, this person checks who has access, what is in the projects, and
whether what was decided three months ago still holds. In a one-person business that is you — so put an
appointment in your calendar, or it will not happen.
Section 8 says what this person looks at.
THE FOUR DECISIONS AT A GLANCE
Who may use it (two or three roles) · What for (two or three tasks) · With which data (your
own line, section 6) · Who tidies up (one person, one appointment). Only then an account.
This section deliberately has no screenshot. Anyone who sees screenshots here starts clicking instead of
deciding.

Account and access

Does this run under my name or the business's? And: What happens to

provider’s statement and the screenshot from the walkthrough.

2.2 Under whose name
Access on a private address is convenient until that person leaves the company or is unavailable. Then the
access hangs on a mailbox nobody can reach any more. Therefore, regardless of the kind of account:
register on a business address that more than one person can reach. Volume 2 explains in section 6.1 why
business access comes before private access; this guide does not repeat the reasoning.
Source: Volume 2 “AI Policies for the Workforce”, section 6.1 (business access instead of private); reference, no new evidence.
In the business account this question has a second level. The provider has four roles — User, Admin, Owner
and Primary Owner — and there is exactly one Primary Owner: “Team and Enterprise organizations can only
have one Primary Owner.” This role occupies a seat and is the only one that may request data exports and
transfer ownership. The provider says explicitly that it can be bound to a service account: “Your organization’s
Primary Owner can be a service account that isn’t tied to an individual user.”
Source: Anthropic, Claude Help Center, “Roles and permissions”, page version “June 18, 2026” (retrieved 8 September 2026),
support.claude.com/en/articles/9267276-roles-and-permissions.
For the business this means: the Primary Owner is a single point. Whoever binds it to the private address of a
person who becomes unavailable has the same trap as with the private mailbox — only with the whole
account. In the example business the Primary Owner is therefore a business address whose mailbox the
owner and the office manager can both reach; the owner works with her own access as Owner.
Whether a business may register an individual account on a business address is a question of the provider’s
terms of use; they were not read for this guide (annex, Uncertainties).
2.3 Who manages
One person manages; at least a second can reach the mailbox in an emergency. That is not a question of
size: in a one-person business the second person is your own fallback — a stored password in a place
someone can reach when it matters.
In the business account, management is spread across the four roles. From the provider’s roles table:

2.6 What remains of this person
This is where a practical guide earns its money, and the provider’s statement on it is unambiguous. When a

Their chats are gone for the others. “When a user is removed from your Team or Enterprise organization,

SECTION 3
The settings to check first
Default settings are made for the average case, not for your business. That
is no reproach to the provider — it only means that you have to go through
once what applies to you. Every setting gets three things here: where it
sits, what it does, why it matters to a business. The third is the one
someone buys this guide for.
3.1 What happens to your inputs
The most important setting of all, and the only one where this guide says: look for yourself, today. What a
provider does with entered content can change, and what is printed here is always a snapshot.
Business account (Team, Enterprise). The provider writes: “By default, we will not use your inputs or
outputs from our commercial products (e.g. Claude for Work, Anthropic API, Claude Gov, etc.) to train our
models.” The pricing page says it in the Team column: no model training on your content by default. Exception
per the same page: if you explicitly give feedback (“thumbs up/down feedback button”) or opt into a partner
programme.

3.2 Who sees what you create
In the individual plan there is no second person; the question arises only in the business account. There the
separation has two levels, and you need to know both.
Projects have two visibilities. When creating a project in the business account you choose between
“Public” — “Everyone in your organization can view and use the project” — and “Private” — “Only invited
members can view and use the project.” A private project is initially seen only by its creator; everything in it
(“chats, project knowledge, and artifacts”) remains inaccessible to others until they are expressly invited.
Inviting has two levels: “Can view” (see, work within it, not change) and “Can edit” (change instructions,
knowledge and members).
Chats are not part of the project sharing. “Even if a project is public, your chats within that project will be
private and inaccessible to other members of your organization unless you manually share them.” A chat is
shared only expressly, via the “Share” button at the top right; it creates a link to the state up to that moment.
To undo: set the chat from “Shared” to “Private”.
Source: Anthropic, Claude Help Center, “Manage project visibility and sharing”, page version “Updated over 3 weeks ago” (retrieved 8
September 2026), support.claude.com/en/articles/9519189-manage-project-visibility-and-sharing.
The trap in a business therefore lies not with the chats but with project knowledge and uploaded files:
whoever puts a file into a public project has released it to everyone in the business account. Which of the two
visibilities is preselected on creation is not stated on the page checked. Check on screen (walkthrough B2):
the preselection on creation and the labels of the two visibilities are recorded in the walkthrough.

3.3 Retention and deletion
You need this point twice: when something has got in by mistake, and when someone demands that their
data disappear. Both times the same distinction counts.
“Removed” and “deleted” are two steps. The provider describes deleting a chat for business accounts like
this: “Removed from your chat history immediately · Deleted from our back-end storage systems within 30
days.” For individual plans the same two steps appear on the consumer page. In plain words: removed from
view happens immediately; deleted from the provider’s storage systems happens within 30 days. In between
lie up to thirty days in which the chat still exists at the provider.
Source: Anthropic Privacy Center, “How long do you store my organization’s data?” (commercial products), page version “July 1, 2026”

are wrong. You do not see that in the tool but in the post that goes out. If it happens twice, the second rule

SECTION 11
Glossary
Listed are the terms of the interface and of the provider’s pages as this
guide uses them. Provider, deployer, data processing agreement, AI system
and confabulation are explained in the glossary of Volume 1; functional
address, usage policy and instruction in the glossary of Volume 2. None of
these terms is defined differently here.
Admin
Role in the business account that may invite and remove members but does not change roles (provider page
“Roles and permissions”, retrieved 8 September 2026).
Business account
In this guide, an account that the business manages and in which several people work with their own access;
with the provider, the Team and Enterprise plans.
Connection (connector)
Claude’s access to another service — file store, mailbox, calendar — reaching as far as the rights of the
signed-in person in that service. In the business account the Owner enables the connection first, then each
person signs in.
Connectors Directory
The list of available connections within the application; this guide does not print it.
Data export
The retrieval of a business account’s data by the Primary Owner; may, according to the provider, contain
chats, uploaded files and usage patterns.
Feedback (thumbs)
The “thumbs up / thumbs down” button under an answer; transmits, according to the provider, the whole
associated conversation to the provider, regardless of the training switch.
Incognito chat
A chat that, according to the provider, is not used for improving the models and is deleted in the business
account within 30 days.

<!-- Seite 34 -->
Individual plan
An account for a single person (Free, Pro, Max); without shared administration, without roles, without project
sharing.
Instructions, permanent
Text in the project that applies to every chat within it; with the provider “project instructions”. May be
changed by whoever has the level “Can edit”.
Levels “Can view” / “Can edit”
The two rights with which someone is invited into a private project: see and work within it, or in addition
change instructions, knowledge and members.
Model training / model improvement
The provider’s use of inputs and outputs to improve its models; not by default in the business account,
controlled in the individual plan by the switch “Help Improve our AI models”.
Owner
Role in the business account that, in addition to the Admin, may change roles, invite or remove Admins and
Owners, and enable connections for the business.
Primary Owner
The one role per business account that may request data exports and transfer ownership; can, according to
the provider, be a service account.
Project
The place for recurring tasks: permanent instructions, stored knowledge (files), the associated chats. In the
business account with the visibility “Public” or “Private”.
Project knowledge
Files and texts stored in the project and drawn on in chats within it; with the provider “project knowledge”. In a
shared project visible to all who are invited.
Rate chats
Setting in the business account under “Organization settings > Data and Privacy” with which the Owner
switches off thumbs feedback for all members.
Removed / deleted
The two steps of deletion: removed from view immediately, deleted from the provider’s storage systems
within 30 days (provider pages on retention, retrieved 8 September 2026).
Sharing (Share)
The express release of a single chat via a link to the state up to that moment; undone by setting it from
“Shared” to “Private”.
User
The basic role in the business account without administrative rights.
Visibility
On creating a project in the business account, the choice between “Public” (everyone in the business
account) and “Private” (invited people only). Chats are not covered by the project’s visibility.
