P R E L I M I N A R Y  N O T E
Purpose and structure
This handbook is addressed to the management of small and medium-sized enterprises
which use, or intend to use, generally available AI tools in their business — without an IT
department of their own and without prior technical training.
It answers four questions, in this order: what does the term “artificial intelligence” denote, and which tools are available?
What operational benefit can they provide, and where do their limits lie? By what routes can they be integrated into an
existing business? And which legal and normative requirements are to be observed in doing so?
The order has been chosen deliberately. Accounts of this subject area frequently begin with the regulatory framework and
thereby lose their connection to operational practice. The legal part comes last here, but is dealt with in detail and with
references.
Structure
This handbook is divided into four parts. Part I deals with the term, a market overview and the capability and limits of the
technology. Part II describes fields of application, integration stages and a procedure for introduction. Part III sets out the
legal and standards framework. Part IV contains an instrument for maturity self-assessment as well as a glossary,
delimitations and the list of sources.
Scope
The governing legal framework is the law of the European Union, in particular the Artificial Intelligence Act and the
General Data Protection Regulation. Both apply directly in all Member States. Where implementation or supervision is
arranged differently from one Member State to another, this is stated, without presenting individual States in full.
In addition, Section 13 deals with internationally recognised standards and frameworks which serve as a common point of
reference irrespective of the State of establishment. For companies outside the Union, the territorial scope of the AI Act is
also of significance; it is dealt with in Section 9.5.
Handling of sources
Every figure, every time limit and every legal statement is supported by a source and an access date. Source lines appear
immediately beneath the statement in question; the complete list is found in Section 19.
Where a statement could not be substantiated, this is expressly noted — as not determinable, with the reason given, and not
as an estimate. These points are marked by boxes with a dashed border and are summarised in Section 17. A handbook that
smooths over unresolved legal questions is less useful than one that names the gap.
A distinction is drawn throughout between settled law, official interpretative guidance and expert interpretation. Non-
binding guidelines are identified as such.
DELIMITATION
This handbook is no substitute for legal advice and is not a legal commentary. It is not addressed to manufacturers of
AI systems, but to companies which obtain and use finished tools. Where providers are named, this is done with a
source and a date and without any recommendation.

The term and its delimitation
The term “artificial intelligence” is used differently in everyday language, in law and in
official statistics. Where the levels are mixed, misjudgements arise as to the extent of
one’s own obligations.
1.1 Three levels of meaning
In everyday language, “AI” usually denotes a text-based chat system such as ChatGPT. This usage is widespread, but has
neither legal nor statistical significance.
In law, the definition in Article 3(1) of the AI Act applies. Under that provision, an AI system is a machine-based system
that is operated with varying degrees of autonomy, that may be adaptive after deployment and that infers, from the input it
receives, how to generate outputs — predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or
virtual environments.
Source: Article 3(1) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, reproduced via the AI Act Service Desk of the European Commission, ai-act-service-
desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-3 (accessed 5 September 2026).
The decisive element of the definition is inference. A system that derives from inputs how it is to respond falls within the
definition. A system that executes exclusively rules laid down in advance does not, as a rule.
Official statistics, in turn, measure technologies: text analysis, image recognition, speech recognition, language generation,
machine learning. The survey unit is enterprises with ten or more employees. This classification is not a legal definition and
was never intended as one.
TEST QUESTION
What is not decisive is whether a provider advertises its product as “AI”. What is decisive is whether the system
infers from data how it is to respond, or whether it executes exclusively rules that a human being has laid down. The
legal boundary also runs along this line.
1.2 What is regularly not an AI system
In July 2025 the European Commission published guidelines on the definition of the term. According to those guidelines,
the following are typically not regarded as an AI system: rules laid down exclusively by human beings and executed
automatically; simple filtering or sorting; analytical overviews without a recommendation for action of their own; simple
average-based forecasts. Knowledge-based expert systems, by contrast, may be AI systems, provided that they infer on a
logic basis.
Source: European Commission, Leitlinien zur Definition eines KI-Systems, C(2025) 5053 final of 29 July 2025, digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu
(accessed 5 September 2026). The guidelines are legally not binding; binding interpretation is a matter for the Court of Justice of the
European Union.
For practice it follows that a payroll accounting system does not become an AI system by virtue of calculating
automatically. A merchandise management system does not become one by virtue of the manufacturer’s product
designation. Conversely, a chat system on a company website is one, even if it is not designated as such internally.

1.3 How generative language models function
Large language models — the technical basis of the widely used chat systems — generate text by repeatedly selecting the
most probable next sequence of characters. The German Federal Office for Information Security describes this process as
stochastic: no examination of substantive correctness takes place. What is generated is what is linguistically plausible.
Source: BSI, Generative KI-Modelle — Chancen und Risiken für Industrie und Behörden, version 2.0 of 17 January 2025, bsi.bund.de (accessed
5 September 2026). Relied upon as a technical description of how the technology functions, not as a source of law.
Two effects of considerable operational significance follow from this:
Confabulation. The model generates substantively incorrect information in a linguistically convincing form. References
are affected in particular: case numbers, standards, studies, prices and contact details are output in the correct format but
entirely invented.
Automation bias. A fluently formulated output is adopted without examination, precisely because it appears assured.
This is a documented effect, not individual misconduct — and it is the actual risk factor in operational use.
BASIC RULE
A language model is a drafting tool, not an information system. All work products which leave the company or
which support a decision are to be examined by a person with the relevant expertise. Observance of this one rule
prevents the greater part of the risks dealt with in this handbook.
1.4 Prevalence
According to the harmonised survey by Eurostat, in 2025 20.0 per cent of enterprises in the European Union with at least
ten employees used AI technologies; in the previous year the figure was 13.5 per cent. The spread between the Member

Poland 8.4 per cent and Bulgaria 8.5 per cent.

1.5 Obstacles
More informative than the rate of use are the reasons given by enterprises which considered use but did not implement it.
Across the Union, 70.89 per cent cite a lack of expertise, 52.52 per cent legal uncertainty and 48.83 per cent concerns
regarding data protection.
Source: Eurostat Statistics Explained, “Use of artificial intelligence in enterprises”, as at December 2025 (accessed 5 September 2026).
Reference base: enterprises which considered use — not all enterprises.
It is notable which reasons do not come first: costs and doubts about the benefit. The three strongest obstacles are
knowledge, law and data protection — and thus the subject matter of this handbook.

2.4 Contractual points to be examined
The choice is decided not by the quality of the text, but by four contractual questions. They are to be clarified before
conclusion of a contract and documented in writing.
1. Are the inputs used for training? For business plans, the larger providers predominantly answer this in the negative.
For free private accounts the answer is frequently different.
2. Is a data processing agreement offered? Without such a contract, the processing of personal data in a professional
context is not lawful (Section 11.2).
3. Where does the processing take place, and is there a reliable assurance to that effect?
4. Who bears the risk in the event of third-party claims? That is to say: does the provider indemnify the company if a
third party asserts that a work product infringes its rights?

2.5 Open and closed models
Two forms of operation are to be distinguished. In the closed form, the service of the provider is used; the provider operates
the technology, and billing is per user. There is no set-up effort, but the data leave the company, and its protection rests on
the assurances of the provider.
With open model weights, the model runs on the company’s own hardware. No transfer takes place; what is required,
however, is suitable equipment, ongoing support and an arrangement for cover in the event of failure. As an order of
magnitude, approximately 16 gigabytes of main memory are cited for smaller models and, for larger ones, a workstation
graphics card with at least 24 gigabytes; in place of ongoing fees come acquisition, energy and maintenance.
Source: specialist article “Lokale Open-Source-KI für KMU” of 27 June 2026, ki-pruefstand.de (accessed 5 September 2026). Secondary
source, not a statement by a manufacturer or an authority.
INTERPRETATION
Operation in-house resolves the transfer question and at the same time creates an operational task: updating, backup,
contingency planning and succession. For companies without IT staff of their own, a business plan with a robust
contract is as a rule the more proportionate solution. A robust figure for the prevalence of in-house operation in small
and medium-sized enterprises was not determinable.
2.6 Voluntary commitments by providers
The European Commission maintains a voluntary code of practice for providers of general-purpose AI models. Signature
comprises commitments on transparency, copyright and safety. It is neither an authorisation nor a quality mark, but it is a
usable distinguishing feature.
Signatories include OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, IBM, Mistral AI, Aleph Alpha, Cohere and Black
Forest Labs; xAI exclusively for the safety chapter. Meta, DeepSeek and Alibaba have not signed.
Source: European Commission, Unterzeichnerliste zum Verhaltenskodex für Allzweck-KI, page as at 31 July 2026, digital-
strategy.ec.europa.eu (accessed 5 September 2026).

Capability, limits and rights in the output
There are types of task for which these tools reliably deliver good results, and others for
which they systematically fail. The difference follows a rule that can be stated.
3.1 Language tasks and truth tasks
Language models are capable where language is the task. They are unreliable where
correctness is the task.
Rephrasing, summarising, translating, structuring and the production of a first draft are language tasks. Here the tool as a
rule surpasses the result that is produced by hand under time pressure.

3.4 Common misconceptions
No substitute for specialist knowledge. The tool increases the performance of those who have mastered the subject
matter. Where specialist knowledge is absent, the result is text that is formally impeccable and substantively erroneous,
and whose defects go unnoticed.
No substitute for legal or tax advice. Suitable for preparing a question, not for answering it.
No knowledge of internal business matters. A chat system does not know the history of a matter unless that history is
supplied to it. Procedures for connecting a company’s own documents are described in Section 5.4; they involve more
effort than the product presentations suggest.
No substitute for staff. The robust studies show a shift in activities, not the disappearance of occupational profiles.

ON THE ECONOMIC BENEFIT
An official indicator of the economic return of the use of AI in small and medium-sized enterprises does not exist.
The OECD observes that companies using AI are frequently more productive, but expressly points out that this
circumstance “may not be entirely credited to AI”; once qualifications and existing infrastructure are controlled for,
the relationship weakens.
Source: OECD, Fostering an inclusive digital transformation, November 2024, doi.org/10.1787/5876200c-en (accessed 5 September
2026).
3.5 Examining suitability in the company’s own operations
Before a decision on a tool is taken, the following examination is more informative than any product presentation:
1. Select a task that has already been completed — a quotation, a refusal, a set of minutes. The correct result is thereby
known.
2. Supply the tool with the same source material, without personal data. Describe the task as precisely as it would be
described to a person newly inducted into the work.
3. Compare. The yardstick is not the first impression, but the question of how long the reworking takes until the result can
be used.
4. Repeat the procedure three times with the same task. Pronounced fluctuations in quality show that the task is not
sufficiently stable for this tool.
Where the reworking exceeds the effort of completing the task independently, this is as a rule due to the selection of the
task, not to the tool. Changing provider is then not the correct conclusion to draw.
3.6 Rights in the work product
The question of the rights in an AI output is rarely asked before that output is used. It has three parts.
Protection under copyright law
According to the case-law of the Court of Justice of the European Union, protection under copyright law presupposes an
author’s own intellectual creation in which the personality of its author is expressed. Purely machine-generated content
without a substantial human creative contribution does not, according to the prevailing view, meet this requirement. The
copyright regimes of the Member States implement this accordingly; German law requires in § 2(2) UrhG a “persönliche
geistige Schöpfung”, Austrian law in § 1 UrhG an “eigentümliche geistige Schöpfung”.
Practical consequence: where an AI-generated advertising motif is taken over by a competitor, there is as a rule no remedy
under copyright law. Whoever wishes to keep a motif exclusive requires either a substantial creative contribution of their
own or a photograph.
Interpretation: Prevailing view in the literature on the basis of the wording of the statute. There is no decision of a supreme court. Lower
courts follow this line; on 13 February 2026 a German local court denied protection to simple machine-generated figurative marks (case no.
142 C 9786/25) — based on a single source, not verified against a second. Source: § 2 UrhG, gesetze-im-internet.de (accessed 5
September 2026).
Use and liability
All the larger providers grant commercial use of the output. The only question regulated differently is who answers for
third-party claims; on this see Section 2.4.

A company’s own content as training material
Article 4 of Directive (EU) 2019/790 permits text and data mining on lawfully accessible works, provided that the
rightholder has not reserved the use in a machine-readable format. The Member States have transposed this, in Germany in
§ 44b UrhG.
For companies this means: their own websites, specialist articles and product descriptions may be analysed for the training
of models as long as no reservation has been declared. The reservation is usually made by way of the file robots.txt
together with an express declaration in the terms of use of the website. Inaction operates here as consent; the declaration is a
task for the technical support of the website and can be implemented in a short time.
Sources: Article 4 of Directive (EU) 2019/790; § 44b UrhG, gesetze-im-internet.de (accessed 5 September 2026).
LEGAL DEVELOPMENT — NOT CONCLUDED
On 11 November 2025 a German regional court held that song lyrics may be contained in a model in reproducible
form and that the exception for text and data mining is not applicable to this (case no. 42 O 14139/24); on 31 July
2026 a decision to the same effect was handed down against a music generator (case no. 42 O 763/25). Neither
decision is final; an appeal has been lodged against the first. On 10 March 2026 the Court of Justice of the European
Union heard oral argument on generative AI and copyright for the first time; no decision has been given. For the use
of finished tools, no altered obligations arise from this at present. Sources: gema.de, justiz.bayern.de, lto.de,
twobirds.com (accessed 5 September 2026).

ON THE SEQUENCE
Failed introduction projects in small companies regularly display the same cause: the start was made at Stage 3 or 4.
Without several weeks of experience at Stage 1, the basis is lacking on which to determine the scope of a Stage 3
project at all.
5.2 Stage 1 — chat system in the browser
What is required is a business account, a limited group of users and binding rules. The difference between successful use
and use that peters out lies not in the software, but in three organisational points:
Quality of the task description. An unspecific instruction delivers an average result. Where the role, the addressee, the
occasion, the tone, the length and the relevant factual particulars are supplied, a usable draft is produced. This can be
learned in a short time.
Reuse. Task descriptions that have proved themselves are collected in a document accessible to all. Herein lies the
actual gain in productivity, not in the choice of model.
Binding requirements on the permissible use of data; on this see Section 7.
STRUCTURE OF A TASK DESCRIPTION
Role: the specialist background of the treatment desired. Task: the document to be produced. Factual particulars:
the relevant facts. Tone: register and addressee. Form: length and structure. Five lines suffice and alter the result
considerably.
5.3 Stage 2 — integration into existing applications
This route is frequently the most practicable, since it requires no change in working habits: the function is available where
the work is done in any event. Two points are to be clarified before an order is placed:
Scope of access. An embedded function as a rule sees everything that is accessible to the person in question. An orderly
authorisation concept is therefore a precondition. Existing defects in the allocation of rights take effect for the first time
through the integration.
Exceptions to the data protection assurance. With one of the large providers, the assured processing within the Union
expressly does not apply to web search queries (Section 2.4). Exceptions of this kind are found in the technical
documentation, not in the offer.
5.4 Stage 3 — connection of the company’s own documents
From this stage onwards the undertaking has the character of a project. The system is given access to the company’s
documents — price lists, work instructions, contracts — and answers questions stating the reference. The procedure is
termed retrieval-augmented generation: a search is made first, and only thereafter is an answer given.
This solves a real problem, namely dependence on individual holders of knowledge. Three conditions are, however,
presupposed:
Orderly filing. Several versions of the same document in different folders do not become better through the connection,
but more dangerous: the system answers from the incorrect version.
Authorisations. Where confidential documents are held in the same filing system, it is to be determined who is able to
inspect them by the indirect route of a search.
Maintenance. A knowledge base that is not kept up to date becomes a source of error within a year.

A COST-FREE INTERMEDIATE STEP
Before such an undertaking is commissioned, it is advisable to place the ten most important documents in an orderly
folder and to supply them to the chat system directly as an attachment. This intermediate step causes no costs and
shows within a week whether the expected questions actually arise.
5.5 Stage 4 — agent systems
An agent system carries out several steps of work independently: reading an incoming item, extracting the particulars,
transferring them into a system, generating a reply. The distance between the demonstration and everyday operations is
greatest in this category. Three conditions are to be met:
1. The workflow is already documented. A workflow that cannot be described in ten steps cannot be automated.
2. The frequency of repetition justifies set-up and maintenance — as a guide, several times a day, not monthly.
3. Errors are recognisable and reversible. Operations with an effect on payments or a binding external effect receive a
prior human approval.
QUESTION TO BE EXAMINED BEFORE AUTOMATION
What would be the consequences if this workflow were carried out incorrectly a hundred times in succession without
anyone noticing? If the consequences are immaterial, the workflow may run automatically. Otherwise an approval is
to be provided for, even if this reduces the time gained.

6.3 Week 4 — assessment
At the end there is a written assessment on the basis of five questions:
1. What gain in time has actually occurred — measured against the baseline figure of the first week?
2. In what proportion of cases was substantial reworking necessary? If this is the rule, the selection of the task is
incorrect.
3. Have incidents occurred which ought to have been avoided — data in the wrong place, erroneous particulars in
dealings with customers, assurances that were not covered?
4. Are the persons involved willing to continue? An instruction is no substitute for that willingness.
5. What next step is envisaged — extension of the group of persons for the same task, or a second task? Not both at once.

A successful first month is inconspicuous: three persons save a measurable amount of time on a recurring task, a
collection of task descriptions that have proved themselves is in place, the permissible use of data is known to all, and
no incident with external effect has occurred. That suffices as a basis for all further steps.
WHERE THE OUTCOME IS NEGATIVE
Discontinuation after four weeks and a small outlay of resources is a usable result; it prevents an annual contract and
a consultancy fee. Repetition in unchanged form with another provider is not recommended — the cause lies
overwhelmingly in the selection of the task.

9.5 Territorial scope
The Regulation applies not only to companies established in the Union. Article 2(1)(c) also covers
“providers and deployers of AI systems that have their place of establishment or are located in a third country, where
the output produced by the AI system is used in the Union”.
For companies established outside the Union it is therefore decisive whether the work results are used in the Union. Point
(b), by contrast, presupposes an establishment in the Union and does not apply to deployers established exclusively in a
third country.
INTERPRETATION OPEN
The text of the Regulation does not specify in more detail under what conditions output “is used in the Union” — by
whom, with what frequency, and whether mere receipt by a recipient in the Union suffices. An official aid to
interpretation on this point was not determinable. The present handbook derives no duty from it. Companies outside
the Union with regular supply relationships into the Union should have this question examined from a legal point of
view.
9.6 Prohibited AI practices under Article 5
Article 5 prohibits certain uses entirely. Two sets of circumstances are of significance for general use in a business.
INFERENCE OF EMOTIONS IN THE WORKPLACE
The use of systems which infer the emotions of employees on the basis of biometric data — that is to say from the
face, the voice or physical characteristics — is prohibited. The prohibition has applied since 2 February 2025 and
expressly covers use as well, not only the placing on the market. Offerings for “mood analysis in customer
conversations” or for “motivation measurement” are therefore to be examined before use as to the data basis on
which they rest.
A mere analysis of the tone of written texts without a biometric basis does not, according to the wording, readily fall
within this. This is an expert interpretation and not a statement by an authority.

10.2 Deepfakes
A deepfake within the meaning of the Regulation is machine-generated or machine-modified image, audio or video content
which resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to be authentic.
As to the manner of disclosure, the Commission states that it must take place at the latest upon the first exposure, and in a
clear and distinguishable manner — perceptible to natural persons and without special technical aids, for example by means
of a visible or audible marking.
Source: European Commission, Fragen und Antworten zu den Transparenzpflichten nach Artikel 50, digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu (accessed
5 September 2026).
For artistic, satirical, fictional and comparable works the duty of disclosure is attenuated: it is to be complied with in an
appropriate manner and must not impair the presentation.
10.3 Published text
The duty presupposes that three conditions are cumulatively met. If one is absent, no duty arises.
1. The text is published. A message to a single recipient is not a publication.
2. It serves to inform the public. A quotation or an invoice does not fulfil this.
3. The subject matter is a matter of public interest.
The Commission describes the last point by reference to the fields of politics and democratic processes, public
administration and services, justice and law enforcement, fundamental rights, public safety, public health, protection of the
environment and consumer safety, as well as economic, financial, political, scientific and cultural developments capable of
giving rise to a public debate.
Source: ibid., accessed 5 September 2026.
Exception where there is editorial responsibility
Even where all three conditions are met, the marking is dispensed with if the text has undergone human review or editorial
control and a natural or legal person bears the editorial responsibility.
The Commission gives concrete form to the requirement: what is required is a deliberate examination of the content by one
or more natural persons with relevant expertise. Editorial control presupposes the power to approve, to amend or to reject
the content. Expressly insufficient are superficial, purely formal or procedural checks such as a spelling or grammar check.
Source: ibid., accessed 5 September 2026.
PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE
Where a machine-produced draft is examined as to its content, assessed on the merits, amended and approved under
named responsibility, the exception applies. This procedure corresponds in any event to the standard of care set out in
Section 1.3 and Section 7. Proper processing and compliance with the rules coincide here.

Data protection
As soon as personal data reach an AI tool, the General Data Protection Regulation applies
in full. Five points are decisive.
11.1 The triggering circumstance
Personal data are all particulars which can be attributed to an identifiable natural person — name, address, customer
number, electronic address, image, voice. They can reach a tool by three routes: through the input, through uploaded
documents and through the output, in so far as the latter concerns persons.
As long as no personal data are processed, the Regulation is not affected. This is the simplest way of avoiding the
requirements set out below, and in drafting work it is regularly possible.
11.2 The five requirements
1. A legal basis for every processing step. In the view of the supervisory authorities, a separate legal basis must exist for
each individual step in which an AI application processes personal data; which basis is applicable depends on the
context.
2. Data processing agreement. Where an external service is used for the company's own purposes, a contract with the
provider is as a rule required under Article 28 GDPR. The larger providers make one available (Section 2.4); it is to be
concluded and documented.
3. Data protection impact assessment. Required under Article 35 GDPR where a high risk is likely. The supervisory
authorities state that this “will frequently be the case” where AI applications are used. Frequently does not mean always
— but it does mean that the examination is to be carried out.

<!-- Seite 35 -->
4. Business accounts and internal requirements. Provision of devices and accounts by the employer; documented
instructions on permissible use (Section 7).
5. Rights of data subjects. Access, rectification and erasure extend also to data held at a service provider. This is a further
reason not to deposit personal data there.
Source for 1 to 4: Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe Künstliche Intelligenz und Datenschutz of 6 May 2024, datenschutzkonferenz-
online.de (accessed 5 September 2026). The orientation guide formulates recommendations and assessments in several places; it
reproduces the supervisory authorities' interpretation of the GDPR, which applies throughout the Union, not the wording of the statute. In
addition, orientation guides on technical and organisational measures (June 2025) and on retrieval-based generation (October 2025) are

11.3 Automated individual decisions
A decision which significantly affects a person and is based solely on automated processing is in principle impermissible
under Article 22 GDPR. Exceptions exist where the decision is necessary for a contract, where it is permitted by law and
where there is explicit consent; the supervisory authorities interpret the contract exception narrowly in application
procedures. Where an exception applies, a right to the intervention of a person, to the presentation of one's own point of
view and to contestation, as well as transparency as to the underlying logic, are to be ensured.
Source: specialist account of Article 22 GDPR, fieldfisher.com (accessed 5 September 2026). Secondary source, not a publication by an
authority.
ON THE EFFECTIVENESS OF HUMAN INVOLVEMENT
A person who merely confirms a prepared decision does not turn an automated decision into a human one. What is
required is an independent examination with the power and the expertise to decide otherwise. The yardstick is the
same as that for editorial control under Section 10.3.

12.2 Basic measures
Before any AI-specific measure, five points are to be put in order. They are unspectacular and cover the predominant part of
the incidents which actually occur.
1. Data backup, held separately from the network, with restoration successfully tested at least once.
2. Up-to-date software on all devices — operating systems, browsers, servers, including rarely used devices.
3. Accounts: accounts tied to individuals, two-factor login, immediate blocking upon departure.
4. Responsibility: a person designated by name as responsible, even where external support is called in.
5. Emergency arrangement: established contact points for the event of an outage.

ISO/IEC 42001 for small companies — comparable to the guidance notes on other management system standards — was
not determinable.
ORIENTATION AND CERTIFICATION
Two courses of action are to be distinguished. In the case of orientation, a company takes the structure and contents
of a standard as an internal basis, without external examination; no evidential effect vis-à-vis third parties arises. In
the case of certification, an independent, accredited body examines the management system and issues a certificate
which can be presented to customers, partners and authorities.
For a small company without a corresponding market requirement, orientation towards the structure of the standard is
regularly the proportionate route. Certification becomes expedient where clients require it.

14.3 Realistic assessment of the risk
For a company which uses generally available tools, the fine under the AI Act is not the most probable risk. More probable
are:
a personal data breach, because personal data were processed in a service without a contract — with the separate
framework of penalties of the GDPR of up to €20 million or 4 per cent as the maximum;
a case of liability on account of an incorrect particular in an offer or in information supplied;
damage to standing through the publication of recognisably machine-generated, erroneous content;
a complaint under competition law on account of an unexamined advertising statement.
All four cases have the same countermeasure: a written rule, business accounts, a duty of examination and approval. This is
the subject matter of Section 7 and establishes its central position in this handbook.

Statements not made
Widespread assertions about the use of AI in companies which this handbook expressly
does not make, in each case with reasons and reference.
1. No statement on economic return. An official causal indicator for the productivity contribution of AI in small and
medium-sized enterprises does not exist. The OECD states that users of AI are frequently more productive, but points
out that this “may not be entirely credited to AI”; once qualification and infrastructure are controlled for, the connection
weakens. (3.4)
Artificial Intelligence in the Company 46

<!-- Seite 47 -->
2. No disappearance of occupational profiles. The robust studies show a shift in activities, not the replacement of entire
occupations. A calculation that assumes staff reductions rests on no substantiated basis. (3.4)
3. No uniform rate of use. The percentages in circulation are based on differing populations and definitions. For the
Union, Eurostat records 20.0 per cent among enterprises with ten or more employees, with a spread of 5.2 to 42.0 per
cent between the Member States. Values from association surveys cannot be set off against official surveys. (1.4)
4. No exemption by category for small companies. Article 4 of the AI Act covers providers and deployers; the wording
contains no exemption according to company size. The reliefs provided for concern fees, AI regulatory sandboxes and
the assessment of penalties, not the applicability of Articles 4, 5 and 50. (8.4, 14.1)
5. No provider role through mere use. The duties of Chapter V fall on providers of models. Conformity assessment, CE
marking and registration concern high-risk AI systems; the requirements under Annex III do not apply until 2 December
2027. (8.1, 9.2)
6. No general duty to mark. Article 50(4) requires disclosure in the case of deepfakes and in the case of published text on
matters of public interest, with an exception where there is human review and editorial responsibility. A message to a
single recipient is not a publication. (10.3, 10.4)
7. No duty to carry out an impact assessment without exception. The supervisory authorities formulate this as
something that “will frequently be the case”. What is required is an examination, not automatic performance. (11.2)
8. No presumption of conformity through international standards. The European Commission expressly states that
ISO/IEC 42001 is not sufficient for this purpose. The presumption is reserved to harmonised standards, of which, as at
the date of this handbook, none is cited in the Official Journal. (13.2)
9. No automatic fine running into the millions. The amounts named are maxima. In the case of small and medium-sized
enterprises, under Article 99(6) the lower of the absolute amount and the percentage applies in each case. An official
empirical value does not exist. (14.1)
10. No official figure on AI-supported phishing messages. The widely circulated statement of more than 80 per cent rests
on the formulation “reportedly” and hence on accounts by third parties. The identical figure from a national situation
report designates the proportion of reported attacks against small and medium-sized enterprises — a different matter.
(12.1)
11. No copyright protection for machine-generated content. The output may be used, but for want of human creation is
as a rule not protected. Clarification by a supreme court is outstanding. (3.6)
12. No general indemnification by the providers. Only some of the providers give an assurance of indemnification, in
part exclusively under the business plan, in part only against additional payment, in part not at all. No indemnification
covers claims arising from the customer's inputs or from subsequent alterations by the customer. (2.4)
13. No preference for operating open models in-house. Operation in-house resolves the transfer question and at the same
time creates a permanent operational task. A robust figure for the prevalence in the segment of small and medium-sized
enterprises was not determinable. (2.5)
14. No adoption of manufacturers' particulars on productivity gains. Neither the OECD nor the statistical offices nor
the national security authorities confirm particulars of that kind. (3.4)

Glossary
Agent system AI tool which carries out several working steps independently, one after another.
AI management system Organisational control system consisting of policies, responsibilities and procedures;

AI system Legal term under Article 3(1) of the AI Act. What is decisive is that the system infers from
inputs how outputs are to be generated.
Automation bias Tendency to adopt a convincingly formulated machine output without checking it.
Confabulation Generation of substantively incorrect particulars, including invented references, in a
linguistically convincing form. Also widespread: hallucination.
Data processing agreement Contract under Article 28 GDPR with a service provider which processes personal data
on the company’s behalf.
Deepfake Machine-generated or machine-altered image, sound or video content which replicates
real persons, objects or events and appears genuine.
Deployer Legal term of the AI Act: whoever uses an AI system in a professional capacity under
their own responsibility.

widespread chat systems. English: General Purpose AI, GPAI.

High-risk AI system AI system in a particularly sensitive context of use. Requirements under Annex III from 2

Impact assessment Prior examination under Article 35 GDPR where a high risk to the rights of the persons
concerned is likely.
Indemnification Contractual assurance by a provider to hold the customer harmless against claims by
third parties.
Injected instruction Instruction concealed in a document or on a web page which the tool executes without
the person using it perceiving it. English: Prompt Injection.
Language model Statistical model for the processing and generation of natural language; technical basis
of the widespread chat systems.
Open model weights Models which can be downloaded and operated on one’s own hardware.
