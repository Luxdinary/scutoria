PRELIMINARY NOTE
Purpose and structure
In brief: What this kit is, what it is not, in which order it is filled in, and how
you can tell it is finished.
This kit is for the management of a business in which more than one person uses AI tools — and which wants
not only to regulate but to show what has been regulated, which tools have been reviewed and who has been
briefed. It supplies five records as editable files: a usage policy with a one-page summary for posting, a tool
register with an approval form, a training record, a labelling checklist and a plan for entry into force. This guide
says, for each file, what it does, which decisions the business must take in it, and what each decision means.
What the kit is
A set of forms in which the passages where the business has to decide are highlighted in yellow and set in
square brackets. Next to each of these passages stands one sentence in italics: what the decision means
and where in the handbook “AI Policies for the Workforce” (Volume 2 of the series) the basis is given. That is
the rule which sets this kit apart from a collection of templates: every gap is marked and explained. A
template in which you only enter the company name decides, in the business’s place, things it cannot see.
The kit does not presuppose Volume 2. Anyone who only wants to fill in the forms gets through with this
guide; anyone who wants to be able to explain why a point is in the policy finds the reasoning, with source
and date, in the handbook at the place named next to the gap.
What the kit is not
It is not legal advice and not a legal opinion. No file claims that a business complies with a rule by using it. Nor
does the kit establish any legal position anew: where it names a provision, it cites the handbook by section
number or the source given there with its date; where date and source are missing, it says “check current
status”. What the handbook deliberately does not say — for instance how long a training record must be kept
— the kit does not say either; it leaves the passage as a gap and explains why.
The kit contains no names of tools or providers and no completed approval list: a supplied list of names
would be out of date the moment it is printed. It supplies the empty register and the procedure by which the
business keeps it itself, with a date in every row.
The order
1. Open the entry-into-force plan (inkraftsetzung-en.xlsx). The sheet is the plan for everything else;
the responsible person and the intended effective date are entered there first.
2. Fill in the usage policy (nutzungsrichtlinie-en.docx). Nine areas of regulation, each with one or more
gaps. This is where the decisions are taken; everything else depends on them.
3. Create the tool register (tool-register-en.xlsx, freigabeformular-en.docx). Every tool actually
used in the business gets a row; every tool to be approved gets an approval check.
4. Post the one-page summary (kurzfassung-aushang-en.docx or .pdf). One page with what must never
go into a tool, and with the route to take in case of doubt.
5. Hold the briefing and record it (schulungsnachweis-en.docx, schulungsnachweis-en.xlsx). The policy
is gone through with staff; date, participants and content are recorded.
6. Create the labelling checklist (kennzeichnungs-checkliste-en.docx) as soon as the business
publishes AI content or lets an AI system speak directly to customers; a business that does neither
records that with a date.

7. Complete the entry into force. Tick the last rows, enter the follow-up.
How you can tell it is finished
The kit is finished when a folder — on paper or as a file store — contains five things that together answer one
question: What have we regulated, since when, for which tools, and who knows about it?
The usage policy with effective date, version line and management’s signature; no yellow passage left
open.
The tool register, in which every tool in use has a row with review date and follow-up, and a completed
approval form for every approved tool.
The training record with one signature per person who uses AI tools for work, and the list of content
covered.
The labelling checklist per publication or per system — or a dated note that the business currently
publishes nothing and lets no system speak directly to persons.
The entry-into-force plan, in which every row carries a date and the follow-up is entered.
The example business
The examples in this guide refer to a fictitious electrical installation business with twelve employees: an
owner, who is also the management, an office manager, two further office staff, seven fitters and an
apprentice. There is no IT department and no data protection officer; the office manager looks after the
computers on the side. Three people in the office use an AI tool for quotation texts, summaries and
translations; two fitters have set one up privately on their phones. The business is located in Austria. People
appear by their role, never by name.
Choice of words
The files and this guide say tool; the handbook says the same thing at the same places. What is meant is an
AI system that the business uses as deployer and did not develop itself. Area of regulation means one of the
nine subjects the handbook names in section 7 for a usage policy. Record here means a sheet that shows,
with date and signature, that something was regulated, reviewed or carried out — not that it is legally
sufficient.

SECTION 1
The usage policy and the one-page summary
In brief: The main document with nine areas of regulation, the gaps in each
and what they mean; plus the one page that actually gets read.
The usage policy is the written form of decisions that management has taken beforehand. The handbook
puts it in one sentence: whoever drafts a policy before deciding writes down wishes. That is why the file
nutzungsrichtlinie-en.docx contains, for each area of regulation, not a finished paragraph but a paragraph
with one or more yellow passages — and next to each passage a sentence on what the decision means.
Source: Volume 2 “AI Policies for the Workforce”, sections 4 and 7; reference, no new evidence.
1.1 Where the nine areas come from
The policy follows the handbook, not the other way round. Section 7 of the handbook goes through the
subjects that belong in a usage policy and states for each where the requirement comes from and what
happens if the point is missing. The selection rests on three published sources: the guidance of the German
Data Protection Conference, which recommends clear internal instructions, their documentation and
concrete examples of permitted and prohibited scenarios; measure M16 of the German Federal Office for
Information Security, under which users should be told which applications they may use for which purposes,
which inputs they may make and how they may reuse outputs; and the template of the Austrian Economic
Chamber with its nine paragraphs.
Source: Datenschutzkonferenz (Germany), Orientierungshilfe Künstliche Intelligenz und Datenschutz, version of 6 May 2024, section 2.2,
pages 9 and 10, datenschutzkonferenz-online.de (retrieved 8 September 2026).
Source: Bundesamt für Sicherheit in der Informationstechnik (Germany), Generative KI-Modelle — Chancen und Risiken für Industrie und
Behörden, version 2.0 of 17 January 2025, measure M16, page 49, bsi.bund.de (retrieved 8 September 2026).
Both authorities are German bodies; their recommendations are professional guidance, not a legal duty, and
not binding in other Member States. The handbook records in section 3 that no express legal duty to keep an
AI usage policy is apparent in the AI Act or in data protection law. The policy in this kit is therefore not a
mandatory document but the means by which the business passes its decisions on to staff and can later
show that it took them.
The nine areas, in the order of the handbook: permitted tools and access · permitted and prohibited uses ·
prohibited inputs · handling unavoidable personal data · review before use · release of documents leaving the
business · labelling · reporting incidents · responsibility for doubtful cases.
WHAT DOES NOT BELONG IN THE POLICY
No legal lecture: a policy that explains the AI Act is not read; the reasoning belongs in the
handbook. No tool names without a date: they go stale silently — names belong in the
register, which carries a date in every row. No promises the business cannot keep: a
commitment that every input is checked cannot be honoured without logging. (Volume 2,
section 7, last subsection.)

1.2 The gaps, area by area
Header. Name of the business, effective date, version line, responsible person. The effective date should
come after the briefing, because a policy nobody knows is, according to the handbook, worse than none — it
feigns security (section 1). The version line carries a date so that, after a later change, it remains visible which
version applied when (section 9).
Area 1 — Permitted tools and access. Two gaps: the reference to the register as the place where permitted
tools are listed, and the decision whether private accounts are permitted for work. The supervisory authority
recommends business devices and accounts because private accounts can create profiles of individual
employees; the handbook concludes that a policy must name tool and access path — naming only the tool
permits use through the private access as well (sections 6 and 7). In the example business, the two fitters
with the privately set-up tool are the reason this gap cannot be filled with “as before”: either they get access
through a business account, or use on the private phone for work ends on the effective date.
Source: Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe of 6 May 2024, section 2.4, pages 10 and 11 (retrieved 8 September 2026); Volume 2,
section 6.
Area 2 — Permitted and prohibited uses. The gap for permitted uses asks for activities that actually occur in
the business, not categories from a template. The gap for prohibited uses is pre-filled with the one case the
handbook derives from Annex III of the AI Act — final decisions about persons are not based on an AI result —
and leaves room for cases specific to the business. Uses in the personnel area are listed in Annex III as high-
risk cases; the policy is the place where a business rules them out before the obligations for them become
applicable (sections 3 and 7). The prohibition names uses, not tools. In the example business the permitted
uses are: drafts of quotation texts, summaries of manufacturers' documents, translations for two fitters
whose first language is not German, help with wording complaint letters. Prohibited: anything concerning job
applications or the assessment of individual employees.
Source: Annex III No. 4 Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, consolidated version 02024R1689 — EN — 27.07.2026 — 001.001, eur-lex.europa.eu
(retrieved 8 September 2026); Volume 2, sections 3 and 7.
Area 3 — Prohibited inputs. The gap is a list of types of data that occur in the business, plus a rule for
doubtful cases. The kit pre-sets five types found in practically every business — details of customers,
employees and applicants; login data and keys; calculations, prices, contracts and customer lists; health
data; third-party documents received in confidence — and leaves room for more. The rule for doubtful cases
is the decisive part: a list without it is incomplete, because no catalogue covers every case. The strongest
reason for this area is the trade secret: under Directive (EU) 2016/943 information is a trade secret only if it
has been subject to reasonable steps to keep it secret. Whether an AI policy is recognised as such a step is
undecided — the handbook says so expressly — but without a rule there is in any case no indication that the
business took any step at all. The example business adds: the costing sheets per job and the access codes
to customers' premises.
Source: Art. 2(1)(c) Directive (EU) 2016/943, CELEX 32016L0943, eur-lex.europa.eu (retrieved 8 September 2026); Volume 2, sections 1, 7 and
12.
Area 4 — Handling unavoidable personal data. The gap asks which processes involving personal data may
go into a tool at all, and with what preparation. The handbook relays the supervisory authority: removing
names and addresses is regularly not enough, because the reference can be reconstructed from the
context. The rule must therefore say when a process does not belong in a third-party system at all (section
7). The example business decides: customer data does not go into the tool; the complaint letter is drafted
with placeholders and the office manager inserts the details afterwards.
Source: Volume 2, section 7; Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe of 6 May 2024, section 3.1 (retrieved 8 September 2026).

<!-- Seite 8 -->
Area 5 — Review before use. The gap names what is to be checked — figures, names, deadlines, references,
standard designations — not only that a check is due. A general duty to check is read as a formality; the BSI
requires that users be enabled to question outputs critically, adapt them and restrict their reuse. The
example business adds references to standards in quotations, because the tool invents standard numbers in
correct format.
Source: Bundesamt für Sicherheit in der Informationstechnik, Generative KI-Modelle, version 2.0 of 17 January 2025, M16, page 49 (retrieved
8 September 2026); Volume 2, section 7.
Area 6 — Release of documents leaving the business. The gap names a person or role, not a department.
The handbook says openly that it found no source expressly requiring release by a second person; it justifies
the point as the organisational counterpart of the duty to check (sections 7 and 13). Deleting this area places
the check on the author alone. The example business enters the office manager, in her absence the owner.
Area 7 — Labelling. The gap requires a responsible person and refers to the labelling checklist (section 4 of
this guide). The handbook names two opposite mistakes: labelling nothing where required, or labelling
everything as a precaution. A sentence such as “where required by law” hands the decision back to staff and
is therefore not a rule.
Area 8 — Reporting incidents. The gap names who is to be told. The sentence that the report itself has no
consequences is not set as a gap in the file but fixed — with the note that a business which deletes it learns
of incidents last. None of the sources consulted expressly requires such a reporting channel; the
recommendation follows from the incentives (sections 7 and 10).
Area 9 — Responsibility for doubtful cases. The gap names a person or role and a way of reaching them. The
supervisory authority recommends involving the data protection officer; in businesses without that role —
like the example business — a person must be named (section 7).
Source: Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe of 6 May 2024, section 1.12, page 8 (retrieved 8 September 2026).
End. Announcement, follow-up, place, date, management’s signature. The handbook names three triggers
for a review — a new or changed tool, changed provider terms, a changed legal situation — plus a fixed date
so that the review does not depend on someone noticing the change (section 9). How often is not
documented in the handbook and is therefore a gap in the kit.
1.3 The one-page summary for posting
The file kurzfassung-aushang-en.docx (and the same page as PDF) is the one page that actually gets read.
It does not contain any area in full but three things: what must never go into a tool, with drawn tick boxes so
that the page can be gone through point by point at the briefing; what is checked before use; and who to
ask in case of doubt and who to report an incident to. The gaps on this page are the same as in areas 1, 3, 5,
8 and 9 of the policy; they are taken from the policy, not decided anew. Filling in both files independently risks
the notice and the policy contradicting each other.
The design follows the rules of the self-assessment sheet accompanying Volume 1: drawn tick boxes,
enough line spacing, no dark areas, so that the sheet remains legible on any office printer.
THE RULE FOR DOUBTFUL CASES
It comes first on the notice because it covers the case no catalogue covers: in case of
doubt, nothing is entered — ask instead, the person named in area 9 of the policy.

1.4 What the example business ends up with
Three pages of policy, signed by the owner, effective two weeks after the briefing, follow-up in twelve months
and with every new tool. The notice hangs next to the printer and sits as a PDF in the fitters' vehicle folder.
Nobody had to read a law for it — but every line of the policy can be justified with a section number in the
handbook.
SECTION 2
The tool register and the approval form
In brief: One row per tool, one check per approval — with date, reference,
and a note wherever nothing could be found.
The register answers the question nobody can answer without a rule: which tools are used in the business,
by whom, for what, through which account, with which data — and which of them have been reviewed?
Without this overview, what the handbook describes in section 2 happens: private access without a contract,
unknown tools, incidents that management learns of only through the damage. The register is the record
that the business knows its situation; the approval form is the record that it has decided per tool.
Source: Volume 2, sections 2, 5 and 6; reference, no new evidence.
2.1 Sheet 1 — the register
The file tool-register-en.xlsx has three sheets. The first is the list: one row per tool with type of tool,
purpose, users, account type, types of data, result of the approval (yes, no, unclear), date of review and
follow-up. Three columns have drop-down lists so that entries remain comparable: account type (business,
shared, private, unknown), types of data (no personal data, personal data, trade secrets, third-party data)
and approval.
Why “type of tool” and not “tool”. The first column asks for the type — text assistant, translation, image
generator, transcription, assistant in office software —, the second for the designation used in the business
together with the provider. The handbook names no tool because a named approval becomes wrong the
moment the provider changes its terms (section 5); in the register the name is permitted because the date
stands next to it.
The entry “unclear”. It is a finding, not an omission: a tool that is used but not reviewed stands in the register
as “unclear” and gets a follow-up. The example business starts with four rows: the text-assistant tool in the
office (business account, approval: unclear), the same tool on the two private phones (account type: private,
approval: no, reason: area 1), a translation service (account type: unknown) and the AI assistant that is
switched on in the office software without anyone having set it up.
The gaps on sheet 1. The decisions are taken on sheet 2 and in the approval form; yellow here are only the
columns for approval, review date and follow-up, with the note that a row without a date is not evidence.
2.2 Sheet 2 — the approval check per tool
The second sheet checks a tool against six questions. The first four are the criteria by which Volume 1
assesses tools and which the handbook turns into a repeatable procedure in section 5:
1. Training on business data — are inputs used to train the models?
2. Data processing agreement — is one offered, and for which services?
3. Place of processing — is there a reliable commitment to processing within the Union?
4. Third-party rights — does the provider indemnify against third-party claims, and with which exceptions?

<!-- Seite 10 -->
Questions five and six do not come from the four criteria but from the handbook’s section on access,
accounts and default settings (section 6), and the sheet says so:
5. Deletion and input history — can it be set so that no input history is stored beyond the session and
inputs are not processed for training? The supervisory authority recommends choosing both when a
business account is set up.
6. Access and account type — is there a business account without personal names, with a functional
address, and who in the business administers it?
Source: Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe of 6 May 2024, sections 2.4 and 2.5, pages 10 and 11 (retrieved 8 September 2026);
Volume 2, section 6.
Each question has three columns: the answer as a selection (yes, no, no statement on the page reviewed),
the reference — which page or document of the provider, with date — and a field for the wording. The
handbook is strict here: an empty field is a finding; a filled field without a source is not. And it says what the
review is based on — the provider’s page, not a commentary about the provider, and what counts is the
signed contract, not a product page (section 5).
The gaps on sheet 2. Three decisions are yellow: who reviews — a named person, not “IT” and not “whoever
proposes it”. What is required for approval — the sheet proposes nothing; a business may decide that a tool
without a data processing agreement is not approved for personal data but is for tasks without personal
data. The handbook gives the range of answers to each question, not a recommended one, because it
depends on the business (section 4). When the review is repeated — a fixed date and additionally as soon as
the provider changes its terms.
THE FIFTH CHECK THE SHEET ONLY NAMES
Next to the four contractual criteria the handbook lists a fifth point, expressly marked as
interpretation: whether there is an official or judicial finding on the lawfulness of the
provider’s model development. The sheet has a row for it, with the note that this question
cannot be answered from provider pages and that a business without a legal department
usually leaves it as “not assessed”. That is more honest than a tick. (Volume 2, section 5.)
2.3 Sheet 3 — the instructions in the sheet
The third sheet repeats in ten lines what is written here, so that the file remains usable without this guide.
2.4 The approval form
The file freigabeformular-en.docx is the individual decision on one tool on one page — for businesses that
prefer to review on paper, and as evidence belonging to a row in the register. It has the same structure as
sheet 2: tool, provider, purpose, users, account type, types of data; then the six questions with answer,
reference and date; then the decision — approved without restriction, approved with restriction (which), not
approved — with reasons, date, signature of the reviewing person and follow-up.
What the form does not contain: an assessment of which answers suffice for approval, and no completed
review of a particular provider. Anyone looking for such a compilation finds it as a dated supplement to the
handbook — with the caveat that it is unverified once its review date has passed.
In the example business the office manager fills in the form for the text-assistant tool. Result: training on
business data excluded in the business plan according to the provider page (reference and date entered);
data processing agreement offered; place of processing — no statement on the page reviewed; third-party
rights — indemnity with exceptions for own inputs; input history can be switched off; business account with

functional address set up. The owner’s decision: approved with restriction — no personal data, no
calculations; follow-up in six months. The row in the register changes from “unclear” to “yes, with restriction”,
with the same date.
2.5 What the example business ends up with
A register with four rows, one of them approved with restriction, one not approved (private account), one
with a follow-up in two weeks (the translation service, once the account type is clarified) and one “unclear”
with the note that the assistant in the office software stays switched off until reviewed. Plus one signed
approval form. Every row carries a date. Anyone asking in a year what applied then can read it.
SECTION 3
The training record
In brief: Attendance sheet and overview per person — the record that a
briefing took place, with date, participants and content.
Since 2 February 2025 there has been an obligation regarding AI literacy, and it is narrower than often
presented: providers and deployers take measures to support the development of the AI literacy of their
staff and other persons dealing with AI systems on their behalf; a particular level is expressly not to be
guaranteed. The article prescribes no format, minimum duration or certification. To the question of how this
is to be shown, the European Commission answers: there is no need for a certificate; organisations can keep
an internal record of trainings and other guiding initiatives.
Source: Art. 4(1) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, consolidated version 02024R1689 — EN —
27.07.2026 — 001.001, eur-lex.europa.eu (retrieved 8 September 2026); Art. 113(a) of the same version; Volume 2, section 8.
Source: European Commission, AI Literacy — Questions & Answers, digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu, page status 27 July 2026 (retrieved 8
September 2026); Commission guidance, not a legal act.
The training record in this kit is exactly that internal record. It shows that a briefing took place, when, with
whom and on which content. It does not show that the briefing was sufficient — none of the sources
consulted says that, and the kit does not claim it.
3.1 The attendance sheet (Word)
The file schulungsnachweis-en.docx is one sheet per briefing: business, date, duration, person conducting
it, form (meeting, walk through the policy, one-to-one), then the list of content covered to tick and below it
the attendance list with name, role, signature. The handbook names, as a workable form for a business
without a specialist department, a documented briefing in which the business’s own policy is gone through, a
few use cases are worked on together and the typical failure patterns are shown — recording date,
participants and content; that is marked as interpretation, not as a requirement (section 8).
The content of the briefing. The list in the sheet follows the handbook and is built so that it can be delivered
with the other files of the kit:
1. The business’s own usage policy, area by area (file 1).
2. The permitted tools and access, and why private accounts are not used for work (register, area 1).
3. What must never go into a tool, and the rule for doubtful cases (notice, area 3).
4. Two or three use cases from the business itself, worked through together (area 2).
5. The typical failure patterns — invented figures, names, deadlines and references in correct format — and
what is checked before use (area 5).

<!-- Seite 12 -->
6. The reporting channel, and that the report itself has no consequences (area 8).
7. When and how to label, if the business publishes AI content (area 7, checklist).
8. Who is asked in case of doubt (area 9).
Plus a gap for content specific to the business: the obligation requires taking into account the persons'
prior knowledge and context of use; a briefing identical for everyone fits only if everyone does the same. In
the example business the office gets a longer briefing with use cases, the fitters a shorter one on areas 1 and
3.
The sheet also names, as a suggestion, what the Austrian Economic Chamber lists as training content in its
template — understanding of AI, selection and use of suitable tools, technical limits, ethical and legal
aspects, security. The content is usable; the description of the obligation at the same place is outdated
(section 6 of this guide).
Source: Wirtschaftskammer Österreich, KI-Guidelines für KMU, 3rd edition, April 2026, paragraph #9, pages 25 and 26, wko.at (retrieved 8
September 2026); Volume 2, section 8.
3.2 The overview per person (Excel)
The file schulungsnachweis-en.xlsx has two sheets. The first is the overview: one row per person with role,
whether they use AI tools for work, date of first briefing, date of last, content covered, acknowledgement of
the policy (date) and a column for remarks — for instance that a person is a new joiner and the briefing is still
due. The second sheet is a list of briefings: one row per session with date, person conducting it, form,
duration, number of participants and reference to the signed attendance sheet.
Who belongs on the list. The gap asks for the group of persons. By its wording the obligation covers not only
employees but also other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on behalf of the business
— freelancers, agency workers, contracted service providers. How an internal policy binds these persons is
contract law and national employment law and not covered by the handbook (section 13). The kit therefore
advises including them in the overview as soon as they work with the business’s tools, and clarifying the
question of binding with a professional. The example business enters the apprentice and the self-employed
bookkeeper who has access to the office software.
Two gaps the kit does not fill.How often to repeat: that a briefing should be repeated when the tool
changes is plausible and formulated as a duty in none of the sources consulted. The column “next briefing” is
therefore yellow, with the note that the business sets the cycle itself and that a new or changed tool is a
trigger. How long to keep: none of the sources consulted says how long the internal record must be kept. The
sheet has a line in the header for that, which the business fills — and the note that there is no documented
minimum.
Source: Volume 2, sections 8 and 13; reference, no new evidence.
3.3 What the record does, and what it does not
It does what the Commission describes as sufficient: an internal record. It does not make an employee
competent — the sheet asks about attendance and content, not understanding. Anyone who wants more
lets each person carry out a use case themselves during the briefing and notes it in the “remarks” column;
there is no duty to do so under the sources consulted.
For high-risk systems stricter rules apply: the Commission makes clear that the obligation to train staff for
human oversight remains in place there. A business using an AI system for a purpose listed in Annex III does
not get by with this sheet; the kit is built for general use, and the policy rules out the high-risk cases in area 2.
Source: European Commission, AI Literacy — Questions & Answers, page status 27 July 2026 (retrieved 8 September 2026); Art. 26(2)
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, consolidated version as above; Volume 2, section 8.

3.4 What the example business ends up with
Two signed attendance sheets — one for the office, ninety minutes, one for the fitters, thirty minutes at the
morning meeting —, an overview with thirteen rows (twelve employees and the bookkeeper), two of them
marked “not for work, no briefing needed” for two fitters who use no tool, and a “next briefing” line with the
date on which the register is next reviewed.
SECTION 4
The labelling checklist
In brief: Nine points with article and paragraph to tick — per system and per
publication; and a sentence in the header that says where the wording
comes from.
Since 2 August 2026 Article 50 of the AI Act has applied, with transparency obligations for providers and
deployers of certain AI systems. By its wording, a business using third-party tools is mainly concerned by two
cases: image, audio or video content that is a deep fake, and text published with the purpose of informing the
public on matters of public interest. The notice that one is interacting with an AI system, and the machine-
readable marking of outputs, address the provider of the tool. The checklist classifies these cases for the
business; it does not determine that an obligation applies and names no legal consequence.
Source: Art. 50(1), (2), (4) and (5) and Art. 113 Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Official Journal L of 12 July 2024, CELEX 32024R1689, eur-
lex.europa.eu. Wording from copies of the Official Journal (retrieved 14 September 2026); check current status. Volume 2, section 7, referring
to Volume 1, section 10.
4.1 The sentence in the header
The file kennzeichnungs-checkliste-en.docx carries under the header the sentence: Wording from copies

of Article 50 could not be read at EUR-Lex for this kit, but was taken from publicly accessible copies,
compared with each other and with the quotations on an Austrian authority’s page. The copies agree in
paragraphs 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6; one phrase in paragraph 3 differs and is of no relevance to everyday business. The
Commission’s guidelines on Article 50 were consulted only through an extract with paragraph numbers. The
checklist therefore names article and paragraph but no guideline paragraph number: what has been checked
against the Official Journal stands; what comes only from an extract is marked “according to the guidelines”.
4.2 The nine points
Points 1 to 3 are the baseline — once per business, with follow-up. Points 4 to 9 are completed per system or
per publication.
1 — Roles. Record for each tool: the provider, and that the business is deployer; who in the business decides
on deployment and use. A deployer is whoever uses an AI system under its own authority; employees acting
on instruction are not deployers themselves (Art. 3(3) and (4)). Anyone who puts a system into service under
their own name or substantially modifies a third-party system may be a provider — then additional obligations
apply outside this kit, and the sheet refers to a professional.
2 — Inventory of types of use. Four lines to tick: an AI system interacts directly with persons (para. 1); the
business generates image, audio or video with AI (para. 4, first subparagraph); the business publishes AI text
(para. 4, second subparagraph); the business uses emotion recognition or biometric categorisation (para. 3).
What is in the register decides which of points 4 to 9 arise at all.

<!-- Seite 14 -->
3 — Provider evidence. For each tool that generates content or interacts directly with persons: confirmation
or documentation by the provider that its system builds in the notice of interaction (para. 1) and marks
outputs in a machine-readable format (para. 2); date of review. Providers of systems placed on the market
before 2 August 2026 have until 2 December 2026 for machine-readable marking (Art. 111(4) as amended by
Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) — the business then enters “no statement on the page reviewed” and sets a
follow-up.
4 — Chatbot or voice assistant. Where an AI system answers customers or other persons directly: notice
text, place of the notice (before the first reply in the chat window; audible at the start of the call), screenshot
or recording, date (para. 1, para. 5). According to the guidelines, a notice only in terms and conditions, privacy
policy or footer does not suffice. Whether a business embedding a third-party chatbot has its own duty to
keep the notice visible is not clear from the wording; the checklist therefore only requires checking that the
provider’s notice appears before the first reply.
5 — Deep-fake check per image, audio or video. Does the content depict real persons, objects, places,
entities or events, and would it falsely appear authentic to a person (Art. 3(60))? Decision “yes/no” with a
one-sentence reason. If “yes”: disclosure that the content was artificially generated or manipulated — visible
or audible, placement, date (para. 4, first subparagraph, para. 5). Anyone relying on the exception for
evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional works enters the reasons; in advertising it generally does not
apply. The example business: an AI-generated picture of a fitter in front of a switch cabinet on the website
falls under the definition because it shows a real scene that never happened; a stylised graphic of a lightning
bolt does not.
6 — Text check per publication. Two questions: is the text publicly accessible, and does it inform the public
on matters of public interest? If both yes: either disclosure (wording, place) or the exception with evidence —
the text has undergone a process of human review or editorial control, and a natural or legal person holds
editorial responsibility (para. 4, second subparagraph). The sheet asks for the name of the reviewing person,
type of review, date, whether the AI changed nothing after the review, and where editorial responsibility is
publicly stated. What “public interest” means for a small business is a matter of interpretation; the sheet
gives as indications topics such as law, health, safety, environment and subsidies, and leaves out pure offers
and product texts. The example business: the article on the website about the new inspection duty for
electrical installations is such a text; the owner reviews it for content, she is named in the legal notice, and
the sheet records that.
7 — Timing and form. The information is provided at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure,
in a clear and distinguishable manner, and conforms to the applicable accessibility requirements (para. 5).
8 — Key dates. Article 50 has applied since 2 August 2026 (Art. 113). There is no transitional period for the
business as deployer; according to the extract of the guidelines, content generated before 2 August 2026
need not be labelled retrospectively, but text generated before and published afterwards must be — to be
verified against the original. The sheet has two date fields per item: generated on, published on.
9 — Delimitation. Labelling does not make the use lawful; data protection, unfair-competition law,
personality rights and copyright remain unaffected (para. 6). And: authority and penalties in the own
Member State are to be checked for current status. The checklist names no authority and no penalty; for
Austria, no official source with status after 1 August 2026 naming the competent authorities could be read as
of 14 September 2026. The sheet has a gap for that with the date of the check.
Source: Art. 3(3), (4) and (60), Art. 50(1) to (6), Art. 111(4), Art. 113 Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744;
wording from copies of the Official Journal (retrieved 14 September 2026); check current status. European Commission, Guidelines on the
implementation of the transparency obligations for certain AI systems under Article 50, C(2026) 5054 final, 20 July 2026, consulted only as
an extract (retrieved 14 September 2026).

4.3 Who does not need the sheet — and files it anyway
A business that lets no AI system speak directly to persons, publishes no AI images, audio or video, publishes
no AI text on matters of public interest and uses no emotion recognition currently has no case under points 4
to 9. It ticks “no” four times in point 2, enters the date and files the sheet. That is a record: it shows that the

SECTION 5
Entry into force
In brief: The plan by which the files become a policy in force — steps,

A policy does not apply because it is written. The handbook calls this the part most often left undone in
practice and derives from the supervisory authority two requirements that are easily read as one:
instructions should be issued and documented. A filed paper meets only the second. According to the
handbook, four details turn a text into a policy in force — an effective date, a named responsible person, a
traceable way of announcement and an acknowledgement of receipt; none of the sources consulted
expressly requires these four, they follow from the fact that otherwise neither issuing nor documentation
can be shown (section 9).

SECTION 6
The difference from the Economic Chamber's free template
In brief: What the free template can do, what it does not do, and why this kit
names it instead of rebuilding it.
In its KI-Guidelines für KMU the Austrian Economic Chamber publishes, from page 14, an explicit template:
“guidelines for employees on dealing with artificial intelligence”, in nine numbered paragraphs with blanks,
each with an explanation and practical tips. It is free, carefully made and comes from the body an Austrian
business turns to anyway. Anyone looking only for a policy to fill in will find it there. This kit says so openly,
because a kit that concealed a free competitor would not be honest — and because what makes this kit is not
the policy but the evidence.
Source: Wirtschaftskammer Österreich, KI-Guidelines für KMU, 3rd edition, April 2026, 41 pages, page 14 and table of contents page 2,
wko.at/noe/digitalisierung/ki-guidelines/ki-guidelines.pdf (retrieved 8 September 2026).
6.1 What the template can do
According to its table of contents it covers the framework for use, the limits of AI and the human as the
decisive instance, customer-related and company-related data, confidentiality, the legal framework, security
and quality of data, ethics, transparency and labelling, training and external communication. Those are, in a
different order and a different cut, the same subjects the handbook treats in section 7 and this kit gathers
into nine areas. The handbook draws on the template as a named third-party model; the training content
from paragraph #9 appears as a suggestion in this kit’s training record.
6.2 What the template does not do
It does not explain the gaps. The template has blanks, but next to a blank there is nothing on what the
decision means and where the requirement comes from. A business filling it in thereby decides on private
accounts, on the approval of tools and on the reporting channel — without being told that it is doing so. The
policy in this kit says it at every yellow passage.
It is a policy, not a record. The template contains no register, no approval form, no attendance sheet, no
overview per person, no checklist per publication and no plan with follow-up. It answers the question “what
applies here?”, not the question “how do we show that it applies, for which tools, and who knows about it?”.
The four records next to the policy are the part of this kit the template does not have.
It is outdated in two places. The third edition is dated April 2026 and is thus older than amending Regulation
(EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026. Two statements no longer hold under the consolidated version: page 5 says
high-risk AI systems apply “from August 2026” — under Article 113 the obligations for Annex III systems apply
from 2 December 2027 and for Annex I systems from 2 August 2028. Page 26 says the AI Act expressly obliges
companies to “ensure” AI literacy and that the requirements apply “since the end of 2025” — Article 4 requires
supporting the development of AI literacy, expressly excludes guaranteeing a level, and has applied since 2
February 2025. The handbook cites both places in section 9 as an example of how quickly a good source
becomes outdated — not as an attack on the Chamber, but as evidence for the sentence that every
publication relied on must first be checked for its date. That applies to this kit too: which is why every file
carries a version line with a date.
Source: Wirtschaftskammer Österreich, KI-Guidelines für KMU, 3rd edition, April 2026, pages 5 and 26 (retrieved 8 September 2026); Art. 4
and Art. 113(a) and (c) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, consolidated version 02024R1689 — EN — 27.07.2026 — 001.001, eur-lex.europa.eu
(retrieved 8 September 2026); Volume 2, section 9.

SECTION 7
Uncertainties
In brief: What this kit cannot substantiate, why, and when it was last
searched.
The following points are open. They are named and not replaced by an assumption. Where the handbook
(Volume 2) carries the same point, the section is given.
1. The wording of Article 50 comes from copies. The official text at EUR-Lex could not be read for the
labelling checklist; publicly accessible copies of the Official Journal were used, compared with each other
and with the quotations on an Austrian authority’s page. They agree in Art. 50(1), (2), (4), (5) and (6); one
phrase in paragraph 3 differs. Before any use beyond the checklist, the wording must be checked against
the Official Journal. Last searched: 14 September 2026.
2. The Commission’s guidelines on Article 50 were consulted only as an extract. The paragraph
numbers on which the checklist relies for two recommendations (no notice only in terms and conditions;
text generated before but published afterwards) were not checked against the original. The checklist
therefore cites no paragraph numbers. Last searched: 14 September 2026.
3. The German wording of the new Art. 50(7) and Art. 111(4) as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744
was not read verbatim; the amendment is documented through a secondary source quoting the English
text verbatim. The checklist relies on nothing in paragraph 7; Art. 111(4) is named only as a deadline for
providers. Last searched: 14 September 2026.
4. Authority and penalties in Austria. As of 14 September 2026 no official source with status after 1 August
2026 naming the market surveillance authorities competent for Article 50 in Austria could be read. The
checklist therefore names no authority and no penalty and has a gap with the date of the check. Other
Member States were not searched.
5. The deployer’s own duty for an embedded third-party chatbot. Whether a business embedding a
third-party chatbot on its website has its own duty under Art. 50(1) to keep the notice visible is not clear
from the wording; paragraph 1 obliges the provider. The checklist requires only checking that the
provider’s notice appears before the first reply. Last searched: 14 September 2026.
6. What “matters of public interest” are for a small business. The indications in the checklist (law, health,
safety, environment, subsidies; not pure offers and product texts) come from the extract of the
guidelines and are interpretation. Mixed texts are not settled.
7. The nine areas are an interpretation, not an official catalogue. The handbook says so itself (section 7);
the grouping into nine areas follows its structure. A business may merge or add areas without missing
anything a source requires.
8. Release by a second person and a no-consequence reporting channel. None of the sources consulted
expressly requires either; the handbook justifies them from the incentives and as the organisational
counterpart of the duty to check (sections 7 and 13). Areas 6 and 8 of the policy therefore rest on the
handbook’s interpretation, not on a provision.
9. Repetition and retention of the training record. A duty to repeat briefings and a retention period for the
internal record are formulated in none of the sources consulted (handbook, sections 8 and 13). Both are
gaps in the kit that the business fills.
10. Binding of freelancers and contracted service providers. Article 4 covers them by its wording; how an
internal policy binds them is contract law and national employment law and not covered (handbook,
section 13). The training record includes them; the policy does not say that it applies to them.

<!-- Seite 23 -->
11. Co-determination. Whether an AI tool triggers the involvement of staff representatives is national law;
the handbook shows it for Germany and Austria and does not pursue it further. Step 5 of the plan is
therefore a pointer to a professional, not a requirement.
12. Recognition of a usage policy as a reasonable step to keep a trade secret. No court decision was
found that brings an AI usage policy under Art. 2(1)(c) of Directive (EU) 2016/943 (handbook, section 12).
Area 3 therefore says that without a rule there is no indication of any step — not that with a rule the
protection exists.
13. The sources from the handbook were not reopened for this kit. They were read for the handbook on 8
September 2026 and recorded there with references; the kit adopts those references. Whether any of
the pages has changed since was not checked on 14 September 2026, because the pages were not
reachable from the working environment.
14. Verbatim agreement with the handbook. The list “what never goes in” on the notice and the example
sentences in the policy are derived from the handbook’s areas of regulation, not taken sentence by
sentence from its final version. A comparison with the printed wording of Volume 2 is open.
